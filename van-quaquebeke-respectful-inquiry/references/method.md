# Respectful Inquiry: method and application

## Foundation and evidence

Van Quaquebeke and Will Felps define Respectful Inquiry as open questioning combined with attentive listening during leader-follower communication. Neither behavior alone constitutes the combination. Their theoretical account connects this behavior to competence, relatedness, and autonomy, while emphasizing context and the relationship's interaction history. Treat this as a rationale for designing conversations, not a promise of motivation, agreement, or performance. [S1]

The unit of practice is the exchange: a question, the response, and what the listener does with it. A polished script cannot demonstrate listening before another person has responded. The package's reflection prompts, record fields, decision boundaries, and review criteria are agent adaptations. They operationalize the public principles without claiming to be the researchers' own checklist. [S1] [S2]

A later laboratory experiment crossed open questioning versus statements with attentive listening versus lack of listening. It involved 22 four-person teams, a confederate leader, and a personnel-selection task with distributed information. The public abstract reports the lowest decision performance in the asking-without-listening condition. That supports caution about hollow questioning; it does not establish that this condition is worst in every workplace or that this package improves a particular outcome. [S7]

## Frame the conversation

Start with an observable issue rather than a judgment of character. Record the participants, what is known, whose perspective is missing, and what the exchange is meant to help determine. An observation such as two missed handoffs is different from an interpretation such as insufficient commitment. Holding that distinction lets an answer change the diagnosis. This framing is a practical application of perspective-taking. [S3]

Separate the following before drafting:

- **Fixed requirements:** A deadline, decision already made, or responsibility that cannot be delegated in this exchange.
- **Open questions:** Causes, local experience, implementation options, tradeoffs, or support needs that remain genuinely discussable.
- **Authority:** Who can commit resources or decide, and whose input will inform that decision.
- **Evidence gaps:** Facts or perspectives that the agent cannot supply from the available record.

These categories are an adaptation of balancing inquiry and advocacy. State a real constraint without presenting it as the respondent's choice. If nothing is open, explain the direction and invite questions about its meaning or implementation where relevant. Do not stage a consultation merely to obtain assent. [S2] [S3] [S6]

Choose an output mode. A preparation plan contains intended behavior and conditional branches. A revised script repairs wording but still needs actual listening in use. A completed exchange record contains only reported contributions and supported commitments. Never promote a hypothetical answer into evidence of consultation.

## Test intention and recognition

Before asking, name what you could learn. A useful internal test is whether a surprising answer might change your understanding, next question, or proposed action. If you are only preparing a rebuttal or trying to make someone discover your preferred answer, the problem is intention as well as wording. Van Quaquebeke explicitly distinguishes listening for understanding from listening to control or reply. [S2]

Recognize equal personal worth despite unequal roles, expertise, or decision authority. Van Quaquebeke distinguishes recognition of a person from admiration for someone's performance or capability. Hearing a person should not depend on whether they flatter the leader, excel at the task, or agree with the initial interpretation. [S6]

Operational test: can the person challenge the premise without the listener treating the challenge as misconduct? Revise phrases that demand deference under the label of respect. The broader public interviews distinguish respect from obedience, fear, and conventional manners. [S5] [S6]

## Protect attention

Look for power differences, time pressure, physical distance, cognitive load, follower dissatisfaction, and an organizational emphasis on control. The theory identifies these as conditions in which inquiry may be both unusually valuable and unusually scarce. A history of ignored input can also change how the same question is received. [S1]

Felps adds that experienced leaders may stop looking for other perspectives, new leaders may feel obliged to constantly direct, and stress may push people toward premature closure. Use these as situational warning signs, not diagnoses of personality. The relevant repair is observable behavior: make space to explore before deciding. [S3]

Practical adaptations:

- Choose an interval in which an answer can actually be attended to. If that is impossible, identify a concrete continuation.
- Reduce interruptions or narrow the issue to something that can be meaningfully explored within the available time.
- In remote chat, use a single clear prompt and wait. Do not compensate for silence by inventing the answer.
- If past consultations disappeared, acknowledge the reported history and explain where this contribution will go. Do not promise that every suggestion will be adopted.

## Ask an open question

Ask about the person's experience, assessment, or reasoning without embedding your explanation. An open question permits an unforeseen account and gives the respondent room to develop it. The method is a behavioral combination, so openness matters together with what happens after the answer. [S1]

Evaluate the invitation with three practical tests:

1. Could the person disagree with the diagnosis?
2. Could they introduce a cause or priority you have not considered?
3. Will you follow that answer before advancing your own solution?

Prefer one concrete invitation at a time. For example, ask about how a handoff works from their side before suggesting a new reporting routine. These are package drafting conventions, not required wording from the expert. Closed questions remain useful for factual verification after exploration; they should not substitute for an invitation to express a perspective.

If a person immediately requests advice, invite their current assessment when useful, then listen. Felps describes asking for the other person's thinking as a way to encourage ownership and reduce dependence. Apply this selectively: an inexperienced colleague may need facts or explicit instruction before they can make a useful judgment. [S3]

## Listen and reflect

Once you ask, yield space. Attend to what the person says instead of searching for an opening to deliver your prepared answer. Van Quaquebeke warns that asking without listening can be experienced as an insult and emphasizes the listener's attention as space in which the speaker can explore their own thinking. [S2]

For an agent, attentive listening means waiting for a live response, using its specific content, and not continuing a simulated dialogue as though it happened. In a plan, write a listening instruction rather than fill the silence with invented employee statements.

Reflect tentatively: summarize the point you understood, preserve uncertainty, and invite correction. A reflection should help the person examine their account; it should not intensify their claim or assert an emotion they never expressed. Reflection is this package's implementation choice, not a mandatory technique specified in the public abstract.

When corrected, use the correction in the next turn. If the person says sequencing is the issue rather than workload, change your summary and inquiry accordingly. Do not ask them to repeat their explanation while maintaining the discarded assumption. Sustained listening matters beyond the first question. [S2] [S4]

## Follow meaning

Choose follow-ups from the answer, not just from a prepared list. Explore an introduced obstacle, tradeoff, example, or contradiction. Ask for clarification when meaning is ambiguous. Conditional branches in a plan are prompts for attention, not a prediction of the participant's answers. [S2] [S4]

Adapt the form of respect to the person. In his public interview, Van Quaquebeke explains why a socially accepted courtesy can be unwelcome in an individual case and cautions against assuming others share one's norms. Do not prescribe eye contact, a particular tone, or emotional disclosure as universal proof of listening. When your delivery is unwelcome, explore what would make the conversation useful. [S5]

## Check motivational signals

The three needs provide a design lens. They are not a scoring system for the person's psychological state. [S1]

| Signal | Design question | Repair when missing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Autonomy | Can the person develop and express a view within honest boundaries? | Remove coercive premises and identify actual choices. |
| Competence | Is their experience or reasoning treated as relevant? | Engage with a specific contribution before advising. |
| Relatedness | Does the response show that this person matters? | Respond to their account and make room for correction. |

Do not infer motivation from agreement, silence, smiling, or speed of response. Consider how earlier interactions could affect trust. One pleasant exchange cannot erase a history of ignored contributions. [S1]

## Combine inquiry with advocacy

Invite the person's possible next step and support needs after understanding the situation. Then offer useful information, your position, or a decision. Both Van Quaquebeke and Felps describe room for inquiry alongside leadership advocacy. Continual questioning is not a substitute for necessary guidance. [S2] [S3]

Distinguish the person's proposal, your recommendation, and what was accepted. A proposed action can be declined while still being taken seriously: explain the constraint, discuss alternatives where possible, and retain the contribution in the record. Do not equate being heard with a guarantee of adoption.

## Close and preserve disagreement

Show the effect of the contribution. Record what changed in the working interpretation, which option is being pursued, and the reasons for declining another. Van Quaquebeke explicitly warns that collecting employee input and then disposing of it turns consultation into a symbolic exercise. [S6]

Preserve unresolved disagreement. His broader discussion of equal-worth respect emphasizes remaining in dialogue and tolerating frustration rather than abruptly denying the other person's standing. This package does not convert that principle into a requirement to delay every decision until consensus. When a decision must be made, state authority, explain the reasoning, retain dissent accurately, and identify any useful review or continuation. This operational boundary also reflects the public discussion of advocacy. [S2] [S3] [S6]

Use an owner and review point for concrete follow-through where the task warrants them. Those fields are a package convention for making the contribution traceable, not an expert-prescribed format. If commitment has not been obtained, label the action proposed or pending.

## Explicit warnings and practical edge cases

### Warnings in the fetched public material

- **Questions without listening:** Van Quaquebeke warns that an unheard answer can make questioning insulting. The laboratory abstract provides a limited empirical caution about the same combination. [S2] [S7]
- **Control or rebuttal as the real aim:** He challenges questioning undertaken to steer the exchange rather than understand. [S2]
- **Premature fixing:** He describes the value of giving the speaker attentive space instead of immediately supplying a solution. [S2]
- **Question followed by a pep talk:** His public post emphasizes continued listening, not a motivational speech after a token question. [S4]
- **Courtesy substituted for respect:** He rejects the assumption that conventional good manners always suit the individual. [S5]
- **Obedience or rank treated as respect:** The public interviews distinguish these from recognition and earned admiration. [S5] [S6]
- **Symbolic consultation:** He warns against gathering input that disappears, and against abandoning equal consideration when disagreement is frustrating. [S6]

### Application rules for difficult settings

These rules are adaptations for agent use, informed by the principles above.

- **Urgent action:** State the necessary instruction and reason. Explore implementation constraints briefly if feasible and return to fuller inquiry afterward. Do not pretend a time-critical instruction is an open vote.
- **No response or reluctance:** Leave room to answer later or in a different format. Do not interpret silence as agreement or compel personal disclosure.
- **Distrust from earlier experience:** Acknowledge the stated history, make a bounded follow-through commitment, and demonstrate it. Do not demand immediate trust.
- **Advice requested again:** After hearing their reasoning, supply the useful guidance. More questioning can become avoidance.
- **Unsupported account:** Respect the speaker while separating their report from verified facts. Seek clarification or evidence without implying that listening requires accepting every claim.
- **Group discussion:** Respond to contributions individually, invite relevant missing perspectives, and avoid letting the loudest voice stand for the whole group. No participant is obliged to publicly disclose sensitive concerns.
- **Harsh feedback:** State the observable concern and standard plainly, inquire into the person's experience, and keep disagreement distinct from a judgment of personal worth.
- **Incomplete record:** Mark missing answers or uncertain commitments. Return a preparation plan when only preparation occurred; never fabricate a completed exchange.
