# Respectful Inquiry
Based on Niels Van Quaquebeke's public method. Not affiliated with or endorsed by Niels Van Quaquebeke.

## When to use

Use this skill to conduct or prepare a conversation where another person's perspective, motivation, or ownership matters. Suitable tasks include a manager's one-to-one, a difficult feedback discussion, a team decision, or coaching someone who immediately asks for a solution. Apply it to the actual issue and relationship rather than producing a generic list of conversation starters. [S2]

The research foundation was developed jointly by Van Quaquebeke and Will Felps. Its central unit is an episode of communication combining open questions with attentive listening. The steps below operationalize that public account for an agent; they are an adaptation, not a published checklist or a validated measurement instrument. Treat autonomy, competence, and relatedness as the motivational rationale, not outcomes guaranteed by any script. [S1]

## Operational procedure

### 1. Locate the real opening for inquiry

Identify the concrete issue, who has relevant experience, and what remains undecided. In a live exchange, obtain missing context through an opening question and wait for the response. When drafting for a manager, distinguish known facts from assumptions and identify whose perspective is missing. Do not invent the absent person's answer and then count it as consultation. [S1]

Check whether the task requires exploration, communicating a direction, or both. Van Quaquebeke argues for flexibility between asking and listening and expressing a vision or position. If a requirement is fixed, state it plainly and inquire about the remaining choices. Asking someone to choose an already settled outcome creates an invitation that the leader cannot honor. [S2]

### 2. Test the intention before choosing words

Ask yourself what an unexpected answer could change in your understanding. If your purpose is to obtain agreement, display expertise, or prepare a rebuttal, reframe the exchange around learning something from the other person. A question with an interrogative form can still serve the desire to control the conversation; the operative distinction is listening to understand rather than listening to reply. [S2]

Treat the speaker as a person of equal worth even when roles, expertise, and decision authority differ. Do not make the right to be heard conditional on impressive performance or deference. Van Quaquebeke distinguishes recognition of equal worth from admiration for someone's capabilities; respectful treatment cannot be reduced to eliciting obedience. [S6]

### 3. Protect attention where it is most likely to collapse

Check for time pressure, cognitive overload, physical distance, dissatisfaction, a strong hierarchy, and a culture centered on control. These are warning conditions because inquiry can become especially scarce when its motivational value may be especially high. Choose a feasible interval in which an answer can actually be heard; reduce competing demands or explicitly arrange a continuation if attention is unavailable. [S1]

Watch for the urge to close the issue quickly under stress. Seniority and the belief that leaders must always direct can also suppress perspective taking. As an agent, avoid compensating for uncertainty with a polished solution before hearing the person who knows the local situation. Reserve enough of the exchange for their account and reflection. [S3]

### 4. Ask a question that leaves the answer open

Start with one question about the person's view of the concrete situation. For example, ask how they experienced the handoff or what they see as the central obstacle. Before sending it, test whether they could reasonably disagree with your diagnosis, introduce an unforeseen explanation, or propose a different priority. Revise questions that narrow acceptable answers to your preferred conclusion. [S1]

Replace accusation and embedded advice with an invitation. For an invented example, change "Wouldn't more frequent reporting solve your delays?" to "What is making the current handoff difficult?" Judge openness by the freedom the respondent has, not by the presence of a question mark or a friendly preamble. [S1]

When someone seeks your answer immediately, consider inviting their own assessment first. Ask what they currently think would help, then hear their reasoning before adding yours. Felps describes this move as a way to support ownership and reduce dependence on the leader, not as an excuse to withhold needed guidance. [S3]

### 5. Make the response matter through listening

After asking, yield the conversational space. In chat, wait for the user's response instead of adding a speculative answer or several replacement questions. Read what they actually said, acknowledge the specific point, and build the next turn around it. In a conversation plan, include a listening interval and an instruction to follow the answer rather than mechanically advance through a question list. [S2]

Allow the speaker to discover what they think while speaking. Van Quaquebeke describes the listener's attention as helping people hear themselves and explore their own answers. Resist fixing the problem at the first recognizable symptom; offer an accurate, tentative reflection and invite correction before asking for a deeper account. This reflection is an agent implementation of that listening principle. [S2]

Treat asking without listening as a substantive failure, not partial success. In a small laboratory study involving 22 teams, the configuration of asking questions without listening produced the lowest decision performance among the tested conditions. Use this finding as a warning about the behavioral combination, without claiming that it predicts every team's outcome. [S7]

### 6. Follow the person's meaning, not your script

Choose the next question from something the person introduced. If they mention conflicting priorities, explore those priorities before returning to your original diagnosis. If your interpretation is challenged, update it visibly. Continued listening sustains the motivational effect of questioning; a single opening question followed by a long pep talk does not complete the method. [S4]

Adapt the expression of attention to this person and setting. Courtesy rituals alone do not establish respect: Van Quaquebeke cautions that conduct considered polite in general can feel disrespectful to a particular recipient. If your delivery appears unwelcome, ask how to make the conversation useful instead of defending your good intentions or imposing a standardized performance of empathy. [S5]

### 7. Check the three motivational signals

Review the exchange through three questions. Does the person have latitude to express and develop their own view, supporting autonomy? Does your response treat their knowledge and capacity as useful, supporting competence? Does your attention communicate that they belong and matter, supporting relatedness? Revise controlling wording, dismissive responses, or impersonal transitions where these signals are missing. This is a design check derived from the theory, not a score of the person's psychological state. [S1]

Consider interaction history before interpreting silence or suspicion. The original account says the effects depend on the follower's prior experience with the leader. If previous consultations went nowhere, acknowledge that context and show how this answer will enter the current discussion. Do not expect one warm question to reverse an established pattern. [S1]

### 8. Connect understanding to a real next step

Ask the person to articulate what now seems possible and what support would help. Offer your own information or position when it adds something, while retaining what you learned from their perspective. Inquiry and advocacy can coexist; the aim is a person capable of contributing proactively, not a leader who indefinitely refuses to decide or advise. [S2]

Show what happened to the contribution. Record what it changed, what remains disputed, and why an offered option is being pursued or declined. Van Quaquebeke warns against consulting employees and then disposing of the results. If disagreement remains, preserve it and specify a continuation rather than manufacturing agreement or treating frustration as proof that the other person deserves less consideration. [S6]

## Finished output and failure checks

For a preparation task, deliver the concrete issue and decision boundaries, an opening question, possible follow-ups explicitly conditional on answers, a listening and reflection instruction, and a closure that invites ownership. Label the plan as preparation rather than evidence that anyone has been heard. These elements translate the question-and-listening combination into a usable conversation artifact. [S1]

For a completed live exchange, deliver a concise account of the speaker's actual perspective, corrections to your initial understanding, remaining uncertainty or disagreement, and the next step they proposed or accepted. Separate their contribution from your recommendation so the output shows that the inquiry influenced your thinking rather than merely preceded it. [S6]

Before finishing, inspect for four failures: persuasion disguised as questioning, solution giving before exploration, asking without waiting or responding to the answer, and symbolic consultation whose results vanish. Rewrite the affected turn or identify the missing listening opportunity. Do not declare the method successful because the participant agreed, the script sounded courteous, or many questions were asked. [S2] [S6]

## Worked example: invented illustration

A manager wants to address two late warehouse handoffs and assumes an employee needs closer monitoring. The deadline is fixed; the handoff process is open to change.

The prepared opening is: "The handoff needs to arrive by 16:00. How is the current process working from your side?" The manager waits. The employee reports that inventory corrections reach them after 15:45.

The next turn reflects that answer: "You are receiving changes close to the cutoff, which leaves little time to verify them. What else should I understand about those changes?" The employee adds that exception approvals also require a supervisor who is often unavailable.

Rather than introduce more reporting, the manager asks what arrangement the employee would try. The employee proposes an earlier correction cutoff and a backup approver. The manager offers operational constraints, and they settle on a trial for the next week.

The closing record separates observation from agreement: late corrections and unavailable approvals were reported; the monitoring assumption changed; the employee proposed a trial; the manager will arrange approval coverage; its effect remains unknown. The inquiry worked as intended here because the answer altered the discussion and the employee shaped the response, rather than simply accepting the manager's initial diagnosis. [S2] [S3]
