# Worked examples

Both scenarios below are invented illustrations. All participants, answers, agreements, and results are fictional. They demonstrate the package's adaptations and are not research evidence or transcripts from the expert.

## Illustration 1: A warehouse handoff

### Situation and weak first draft

A manager is preparing a conversation about two late warehouse handoffs. The handoff deadline is fixed at 16:00. The manager assumes the employee needs closer monitoring; the cause and process changes remain open.

Weak draft:

> You missed the handoff twice. Wouldn't sending me hourly updates make you more accountable? Tell me what happened, but we need to move quickly. Starting tomorrow, send the updates and confirm you agree.

This embeds the proposed remedy, treats lateness as a character problem, and asks for an account without leaving room for it to affect the result. The language performs consultation while the decision is already complete.

### Improved preparation

- **Known:** Two handoffs were late; the cutoff is 16:00.
- **Assumed:** Monitoring would solve the problem.
- **Boundary:** The cutoff is fixed; causes and process improvements are open. The manager can arrange approval coverage.
- **Opening:** The handoff needs to arrive by 16:00. How is the current process working from your side?
- **Listening instruction:** Wait for the employee's account. Summarize the obstacle they describe and invite correction before proposing changes.
- **Conditional follow-up:** If late changes are mentioned, explore when they arrive and what verification requires. If approval delays are mentioned, explore how exceptions are handled. If neither appears, follow the obstacle actually reported.
- **Ownership prompt:** What change would you try first, and what support would make that possible?

### Illustrative exchange and result

Employee: Inventory corrections often arrive after 15:45, so I have little time to check them. Exception approvals also depend on a supervisor who is sometimes unavailable.

Manager: You are describing late corrections and gaps in approval coverage. Have I understood the main obstacles, or is something missing?

Employee: Those are the main ones. The approval gap is the bigger problem when corrections involve an exception.

Manager: What arrangement would you try for those exceptions?

Employee: An earlier correction cutoff and a backup approver.

Manager: I can arrange backup coverage. The correction cutoff needs the inventory team's agreement. Could we test backup coverage next week and ask that team about the earlier cutoff?

Employee: Yes. I can log which exceptions still miss the deadline.

Improved closure record:

- **Reported perspective:** Late corrections reduce verification time; approval gaps are especially important for exceptions. These reports still need operational verification.
- **Changed interpretation:** The conversation shifted from an assumed accountability problem to possible process dependencies.
- **Accepted trial:** The manager will arrange backup approval coverage for next week; the employee will log remaining late exceptions. They will review the log at Friday's handoff meeting.
- **Pending proposal:** An earlier correction cutoff requires consultation with the inventory team.
- **Uncertainty:** The trial's effect is unknown. No claim of improved performance is warranted yet.

### Rules that made the difference

The opening allowed an unforeseen explanation; attentive follow-up distinguished two obstacles rather than imposing monitoring. The employee shaped the trial, while the manager contributed authority and constraints. This illustrates open questioning plus listening, ownership, and inquiry alongside advocacy. Recording both accepted and pending actions avoids symbolic consultation. [S1] [S2] [S3] [S6]

## Illustration 2: A rollout decision and prior distrust

### Situation and weak first draft

A support director must announce that a new routing tool will launch Monday. The date is already committed, but coverage and escalation procedures can still change. A team lead previously submitted concerns that received no answer.

Weak draft:

> What do you think about launching Monday? We all need to stay positive. I know you have concerns, but this is an exciting opportunity. How can you get your team on board?

The first question implies that the date is open. The rest discourages disagreement and asks the team lead to sell a settled decision without understanding the concerns. Warm language cannot repair that mismatch.

### Improved preparation

- **Known:** Monday's launch is committed; implementation arrangements remain adjustable.
- **Missing:** The lead's current assessment and whether previous concerns still apply.
- **Boundary statement:** The launch date is Monday. I can change coverage and escalation arrangements, and I want your assessment of what those arrangements need.
- **History acknowledgment:** You said the previous concerns received no response. I will record what we address, what remains open, and who will respond to it.
- **Opening:** From your team's perspective, where do the current arrangements leave the greatest risk?
- **Listening instruction:** Hear the concern without turning immediately to reassurance. Reflect the specific risk and ask for correction.
- **Conditional follow-up:** If the lead proposes delaying launch, explore the reason before restating the fixed date. If capacity is the issue, examine the support coverage they believe is missing.

### Illustrative exchange and result

Lead: You asked us last time and nothing happened. The training sandbox still does not match the live escalation flow. I would delay launch.

Director: You see a mismatch between practice and live escalation, and the unanswered concerns make this invitation hard to trust. What happens in the live flow that the team has not been able to practice?

Lead: A failed transfer sends the ticket back to the queue without an owner. We have no named person to catch those tickets on the first shift.

Director: So the immediate gap is ownership of failed transfers, rather than training time alone. What coverage would reduce that risk on Monday?

Lead: A named escalation owner on each shift would help, but I still favor a delay.

Director: I can name those owners and publish the roster before Monday. I will also send the sandbox mismatch to the tool team today and ask for a response before Friday's readiness review. The date remains Monday under the current decision. I will record that you favor a delay and why.

Improved closure record:

- **Perspective:** The lead reports an escalation mismatch and an ownership gap; they continue to favor delaying launch.
- **Changed understanding:** The concern became a concrete failed-transfer problem rather than generalized resistance.
- **Director commitments:** Publish named shift owners before Monday and request a tool-team response for Friday's review.
- **Pending:** The tool team's response and any sandbox repair are unconfirmed.
- **Disagreement:** The director retains the committed date; the lead has not endorsed that choice or accepted responsibility for promoting it.

### Rules that made the difference

Honest boundaries removed a false choice. Acknowledging interaction history avoided treating distrust as disrespect. Follow-ups used the lead's specific concern, and the record preserved dissent and pending responses. Respect did not require agreement, guaranteed adoption, or withholding the director's position. These choices apply the theory's interaction-history qualification, the broader warning about discarded consultation, and the balance of inquiry and advocacy. [S1] [S2] [S3] [S6]

## Transfer note

Use these examples to study decisions, not to copy a dialogue sequence. In a real exchange the next question depends on the answer. A preparation plan should not insert these fictional employee responses into an actual meeting record. Review such records with [review-checklist](../checklists/review-checklist.md).
