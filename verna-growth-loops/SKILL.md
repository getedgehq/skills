# Elena Verna's Growth Loops
Based on Elena Verna's public method. Not affiliated with or endorsed by Elena Verna.

## When to use

Use this skill when a founder or growth team needs to explain how a product grows, diagnose stalled distribution, design a repeatable acquisition mechanism, or prioritize investments across a growth system. Begin with Verna's three recurring questions: which loops exist, how they perform, and which new loops might work. [S1]

Use it to connect product behavior with distribution and business outcomes. If the business has not established compelling customer value, treat the growth model as an early hypothesis to develop with the founders, rather than a reason to immediately hire a growth executive or import another company's playbook. [S4]

## Method and attribution

Verna explicitly credits Reforge for the growth loops framework and Lenny Rachitsky and Dan Hockenmaier for its racecar extension. This procedure encodes her public application of those ideas alongside her own operational judgments. The templates, calculations, and delivery format below are adaptations for agent work, not a proprietary formula attributed to her. [S1]

### 1. Establish the product's actual growth context

Collect the core audience, customer problem, evidence of product-market fit, meaningful product actions, acquisition sources, activation behavior, retained usage, and monetization path. Separate observations from assumptions. When evidence is missing, produce a hypothesis and a specific data request instead of pretending the first growth model is already validated. [S4]

Map acquisition, retention, and monetization against product-led, marketing-led, and sales-led motions. State which motion currently drives each lever. A business can acquire through marketing, retain through product, and monetize through both product and sales; do not equate a growth model with choosing one department to do everything. [S4]

### 2. Inventory existing loops before proposing more tactics

Describe every plausible existing loop using this operational template: starting input, participant action, generated output, reinvestment mechanism, next input. Point to the actual transition that closes the cycle. An acquisition funnel ending in a purchase remains incomplete as a loop unless some output supplies the next cycle. [S2]

Anchor the map in product behavior. Verna's Dropbox example is a new user adding content, sharing it, and a recipient becoming a new user. Identify the equivalent behavior in the user's product and explain why participants would perform it while pursuing their own value. Do not start with an arbitrary referral incentive simply because another company used one. [S1]

Distinguish acquisition loops that create new participants from engagement loops that bring existing participants back. For example, Verna describes SurveyMonkey's new-response notification prompting creators to return and analyze results. Give each loop its own outcome so returning users are not mislabeled as newly acquired users. [S4]

### 3. Check that the loop has a viable engine and fuel

Classify proposed work using the racecar model: engines are recurring loops; fuel supplies their inputs; lubricants improve their efficiency; turbo boosts provide temporary acceleration. Identify whether the product currently needs an engine hypothesis, initial seeding, better performance from an established engine, or additional fuel. [S3]

Match the fuel to the engine. A viral engine needs users who expose others to the product; a content engine needs useful distributable content; a paid marketing or sales engine needs capital recovered through monetization. For a paid loop, explicitly trace cash recovery and reinvestment, since payback controls how quickly it can run again. [S3]

Check proposed pricing and product interventions against that mechanism. Higher monetization can fund a paid engine but reduce conversion; excessive referral prompts can damage retention. Reject a plan that improves fuel supply while impairing the behavior that powers the engine. [S3]

### 4. Validate activation and retained value

Define activation through setup, initial value realization, and the first habit cycle. Keep people who have not reached that state in the activation diagnosis; do not frame their disappearance as a retention problem requiring more reminders. In B2B, define success at the team or account level when that is where customer value resides. [S5]

Choose activity that reflects value, not superficial movement. Verna found that increasing SurveyMonkey logins through lifecycle emails did not improve long-term retention; receiving survey responses was more predictive. Select the analogous value event and check whether repeated occurrence actually predicts continued use. [S5]

Measure recurrence at the product's natural frequency. A single active week does not establish a weekly habit. Specify a repeated-use window, such as value activity in three of four weeks for a weekly product, and distinguish that habitual cohort from a conventional one-period active-user count. [S5]

Include retained free users in the model. Evaluate their contribution to future paid prospects, acquisition through viral or content loops, and adjacent use cases or network effects. Do not discard users solely because they have not paid. [S5]

### 5. Translate the diagram into a measurable model

For each transition, record the event, denominator, observed rate or explicit assumption, resulting output, and elapsed time. As an operational implementation of Reforge's quantitative-model requirement, estimate next-cycle output from eligible inputs, actions per input, and downstream conversion. Show the assumptions beside the estimate instead of presenting a diagram as proof of compounding. [S2]

Concentrate on the strongest one or two loop candidates. Compare their expected repeated output over a relevant horizon, not only their first-week acquisition spike. Reject a catalogue of weak circles masquerading as a strategy; a plausible loop still needs evidence that its output is reinvested. [S2]

Treat attribution as directional evidence. State what is observed, what is inferred, and what remains unknown, especially where exposure and acquisition span channels. Do not manufacture precise causal credit for a loop because a dashboard offers a clean percentage. [S6]

### 6. Choose the next investment and give it ownership

Select the intervention that addresses the diagnosed constraint. Seed an empty engine with direct outreach or another temporary boost; improve a promising existing engine before abandoning its remaining upside; build a new engine when optimization alone cannot unlock meaningful distribution. Name which racecar component each recommended initiative changes. [S3]

Assign accountability across the whole loop. Verna describes a Dropbox growth pod dedicated to its main loop. Use that principle to specify an owner, cooperating functions, and a shared loop outcome, rather than allowing each department to maximize its own stage while degrading the next. [S1]

Require a hypothesis, learning objective, relevant events, and a decision point for the proposed work. Choose these around the uncertain growth mechanism, not an automatic backlog of tiny conversion experiments. Verna's framework roundup warns that tactical testing can obscure a strategic problem. [S1]

### 7. Plan for decline and return a decision-ready artifact

Monitor saturation, rising costs, and weakening output. Verna warns that loops deteriorate and need reinvention. Specify the evidence that would trigger renewed product investment, a revised mechanism, or exploration of an adjacent loop; never extrapolate historical growth indefinitely. [S6]

Check organizational feasibility before recommending execution. Identify unavailable capacity, departmental barriers, and dependencies on core product work. Do not make a small growth team solely responsible for a problem created by the product or the organization. [S6]

Return a concise growth-model brief containing the audience and value behavior; current and proposed loop maps; reinvestment arrows; activation and retention definitions; transition metrics and assumptions; the primary constraint; selected investment and owner; and the evidence needed for the next decision. Keep product, channel, and monetization connected throughout the brief. [S2]

## Worked example: invented illustration

A fictional collaborative research tool has 100 newly activated teams per month. Teams create findings pages and share them with external collaborators. The hypothesized acquisition loop is activated team, shared findings page, recipient experiencing useful research, recipient creating an activated team. This illustrates Verna's Dropbox-style distribution logic, not a result from her work. [S1]

Suppose each team shares two pages with five distinct external recipients each, producing 1,000 exposures. If 10% create accounts and 20% of those activate teams, the model predicts 20 new activated teams per cycle. These invented assumptions imply 0.2 next-cycle teams per starting team; they do not establish self-sustaining acquisition or causal lift. [S2]

The team-level value event is collaboratively reviewing findings, with a proposed weekly habit threshold of activity in three of four weeks. Recipients who register but never reach collaborative value remain an activation problem. Free teams remain in the retained population because their sharing may distribute the product. [S5]

The recommended first investment is to investigate recipient-to-team activation and simplify collaborative setup, with a product growth owner accountable for resulting activated teams. A conference sponsorship might seed the engine but would remain a temporary boost. The brief would request measured recipient cohorts before forecasting larger returns. [S3]
