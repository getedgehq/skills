# Growth loops: detailed method

## Attribution and scope

This is a practical synthesis of public material. Verna recommends Reforge's loop framework and the racecar extension by Lenny Rachitsky and Dan Hockenmaier, then describes how she uses them in growth work. Preserve those origins when explaining the approach. [S1] [S2] [S3]

The central questions are what loops the product already has, how those loops perform, and which new mechanisms might work. A useful answer connects product value, distribution, retained behavior, and monetization. A collection of channel tactics is insufficient unless it explains how outputs become subsequent inputs. [S1] [S2]

Evidence labels, formulas, decision tables, deduplication rules, and review thresholds below are package adaptations. They implement the public principles; they are not a proprietary Verna scoring system. Source-backed guidance carries [S#] citations. Illustrative thresholds must be chosen for the user's context.

## 1. Establish context and evidence

Gather the target persona, core use case, customer problem, evidence of delivered value, meaningful actions, distribution sources, retained usage, and revenue path. Ask what decision the brief must support and by when. Verna distinguishes a compelling value hypothesis from a growth model that makes distribution predictable and defensible. Strong value alone does not establish how a company will scale. [S4]

For each important claim, record one of these evidence states:

| State | Meaning | Treatment |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Observed | Directly supported by a stated dataset or behavior | Include cohort, period, unit, and limitations |
| Inferred | An interpretation consistent with observations | State alternatives and what would distinguish them |
| Assumed | A planning input without measured support | Show the assumption next to the projection |
| Unknown | No defensible value available | Request the evidence or propose a bounded discovery step |

These labels are an operational convention. They prevent a plausible diagram from silently becoming a validated growth claim. The initial growth model should be treated as a hypothesis whose assumptions need learning and revision. [S4]

When customer value is uncertain, prioritize founder-led discovery and proof of the core use case. Do not recommend hiring a senior growth leader as a substitute for understanding the first mechanism. Use existing organic behavior as a starting point: what customers already try to accomplish, share, publish, or purchase. [S4]

## 2. Connect levers and motions

Separate what must improve from how the business will improve it:

- Acquisition brings new participants into the product or business.
- Retention includes helping customers activate and continue receiving value.
- Monetization turns delivered value into revenue.

Verna describes product, marketing, sales/success, support, and community as possible motions against these levers. A business may use different motions for different levers, or layer multiple motions within one lever. Product-led does not mean that marketing and sales disappear. [S4]

Create a small matrix of lever, current motion, evidence, owner, and dependencies. Revenue is an outcome; the controllable actions often sit earlier in the customer journey. Include data readiness when choosing an intervention rather than trying to optimize only a financial total. [S4]

Treat channel requirements and monetization as part of product strategy. A channel's constraints shape the product and the business model; separate departmental plans can produce incompatible choices. Define one loop outcome so increasing low-quality leads cannot look successful while downstream value deteriorates. [S2]

## 3. Map and test loop closure

Use the following chain for each candidate:

**Starting input -> participant action -> generated output -> reinvestment mechanism -> next eligible input.**

Specify who participates, what motivates the action, where value occurs, and what actually closes the cycle. A funnel can explain a transition inside a loop. It becomes a loop only when an output supplies future inputs. Merely drawing a return arrow does not establish reinvestment. [S2]

In Verna's Dropbox example, creating content leads to sharing, and a recipient becomes a new user. The distribution behavior follows from the product's use. Identify the equivalent mechanism in the user's product before proposing a generic referral reward. [S1]

Separate outcomes:

- An acquisition loop produces new participants who can contribute to future cycles.
- An engagement loop returns existing participants to valuable activity.
- A content mechanism creates distributable material that attracts or re-engages participants.
- A paid mechanism recovers cash from customers and reinvests available cash into further acquisition.

Loops can serve more than one kind of value, but state each output separately. SurveyMonkey's response notification bringing a creator back to analyze results is a retention example, not proof that a new customer was acquired. [S2] [S4]

As package-level measurement rules, deduplicate recipients and resulting accounts; separate existing from genuinely new participants; and identify whether the output is usable in the next cycle. If a recipient cannot share, create content, or otherwise reproduce the behavior, signup counts overstate next-cycle supply. For team products, individual invitations within the same account are not automatically new accounts.

Focus analysis on the strongest one or two candidates while retaining a short inventory of alternatives. Reforge warns that drawing many low-powered loops is not a sustainable strategy. This is a prioritization principle, not a claim that every business has exactly two mechanisms. [S2]

## 4. Match the investment to the engine

The racecar framework separates four roles. Classify an initiative by its mechanism in the current business, not by its channel name alone. [S3]

| Component | Role | Diagnostic question |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Engine | Recurring mechanism whose output can feed future growth | What repeats, and what is reinvested? |
| Fuel | Input required for that mechanism | Does it need users, content, capital, or another specific input? |
| Lubricant | Improvement to conversion, activation, or retention | Which transition or retained behavior becomes more effective? |
| Temporary boost | Activity that supplies a short-lived increase | What durable mechanism receives the initial spark? |

Use these decision rules, adapted from the framework's stage-dependent pitfalls: [S3]

1. **No initial inputs:** Seed a plausible engine with direct outreach, an event, or another bounded activity. Small-scale manual work can be appropriate before scalable growth exists.
2. **Only repeated launches or publicity spikes:** Form and test an engine hypothesis. Do not rebrand a calendar of one-off campaigns as a compounding mechanism.
3. **Established engine with avoidable friction:** Improve activation, conversion, or retained value before abandoning substantial remaining upside.
4. **Tiny flow or exhausted engine:** Micro-optimization may have little absolute impact. Test a new engine or adjacent audience when evidence supports that constraint.
5. **Wrong fuel:** Align monetization and inputs to the actual mechanism. Paid acquisition and sales need available capital; content needs useful distributable material; viral mechanisms need participants who expose others to value.

Avoid a fixed allocation across components. Verna uses the categories to balance work and prevent excessive focus on fuel or lubricants alone. The appropriate mix depends on product stage and the diagnosed constraint. [S1]

Evaluate changes across the complete system. Higher prices may increase cash per customer while lowering conversion or retention. More referral prompts may increase sharing while damaging product experience. Protect the value behavior that makes the engine work. [S3]

## 5. Define activation and retention

Verna's activation boundary includes setup, initial value realization, and a first habit cycle. Participants who have not reached that boundary need an activation diagnosis; reminders cannot solve a failure to experience initial value. In the brief, define observable events for all three stages and report incomplete activation explicitly. [S5]

Use activity that represents value. Verna describes increasing SurveyMonkey logins through lifecycle messaging without improving long-term retention; receiving survey responses was more informative. Choose the analogous event, then examine whether it predicts continued value rather than declaring a convenient dashboard event sufficient. [S5]

Distinguish habitual use from one-period activity. Verna proposes recurrence measures such as value activity in three of four weeks for a weekly product. Apply a window appropriate to natural usage, label it as a habit measure, and retain conventional active-user counts separately. Do not silently redefine a team's existing WAU or MAU metric. [S5]

For B2B, measure the team, account, organization, or logo when that is the customer value unit. User activity can still explain account health, but many active individuals do not necessarily mean a healthy or monetizable account. Track retained free participants as well as paid customers: free users may create acquisition, become future paid prospects, or reveal adjacent use cases. [S5]

Package adaptation: prevent retrospective cohort selection from hiding losses. Report signup-to-activation separately, define the activated retention cohort at a known date, and give that cohort enough follow-up time. For seasonal or infrequent products, a weekly habit threshold is inappropriate; use a recurrence window justified by the actual need. A purchase or login is not automatically the value event.

## 6. Build a quantitative model

Reforge calls for translating the qualitative loop into a quantitative model. The racecar framework highlights payback for cash-fueled engines. The following calculations are implementation aids, with all terms defined for the product. [S2] [S3]

For every transition, record:

- Event and entity unit, such as unique account, recipient, or published page.
- Denominator and rate, including cohort, measurement window, and exclusions.
- Quantity produced per eligible input and the share usable for reinvestment.
- Elapsed time and any capacity limit.
- Evidence state and uncertainty or plausible range.

### Participant loop calculation

For a simple acquisition chain:

`Next-cycle eligible participants = starting eligible participants x qualified exposures per participant x new-participant conversion x eligibility conversion.`

`Reproduction ratio R = next-cycle eligible participants / starting eligible participants.`

Use conditional conversion rates with compatible denominators. If a term already measures activated participants, do not multiply by activation again. Explain how overlaps, existing users, and same-account joins are excluded.

For a one-time seeded cohort with a constant ratio and no external inputs, generation n contains `N0 x R^n` eligible participants. This simplified model describes new generations, not the entire installed base. It does not include continuing contributions from retained earlier participants. An R below one does not imply the product has no useful loop, but it cannot justify self-sustaining expansion of that seeded generation alone. When retained participants keep generating output, model their surviving contribution separately so it is counted once.

Include cycle time. A slower high-yield mechanism and a faster low-yield mechanism cannot be compared by ratio alone. Compare output over the same decision horizon, report uncertainty, and identify the assumptions that control the recommendation. Do not extrapolate a fixed ratio through saturation or rising costs. [S6]

### Paid loop calculation

Map `available capital -> acquisition spend -> activated and retained paying customers -> collected contribution cash -> reinvestment -> available capital`.

Distinguish revenue, collected cash, and funds available after service costs and other commitments. Lifetime value may inform economics but does not pay the next invoice. Payback describes when enough cash is recovered to run the mechanism again. More favorable monetization can accelerate fuel recovery while also reducing customer conversion, so evaluate both effects. [S3]

Package adaptation: state cash collection timing, contribution assumptions, reinvestment fraction, and a cash floor. Do not recommend scaling from an LTV:CAC ratio alone. Show the effect of delayed collections, early churn, and cost increases before presenting a cash-funded mechanism as repeatable.

### Identify the constraint

Compare feasible changes in absolute repeated output, time, evidence quality, cost, and product risk. The lowest transition percentage is not automatically the binding constraint. A large improvement on a small denominator can matter less than retaining valuable participants who power every subsequent cycle. Retention and product quality should remain central to the diagnosis. [S3] [S6]

Treat attributed channel percentages as directional information. Separate observed paths from causal claims and disclose missing exposure data or overlapping channels. Verna warns against precise faith in attribution; that does not mean abandoning measurement. [S6]

## 7. Select an investment and learning plan

Recommend a primary intervention that addresses the diagnosed constraint. Explain whether it seeds an engine, improves a transition, supplies fuel, or develops a new mechanism. Give the alternatives a concrete reason to wait, such as insufficient evidence, limited absolute upside, unavailable cash, or dependency on activation improvements. [S3]

For each recommended initiative, specify a hypothesis, learning objective, owner, scope, relevant events, observation window, success condition, stop condition, and next decision. These are practical fields, not expert-prescribed thresholds. A data gap may justify cohort instrumentation or interviews before an experiment.

Use the learning method appropriate to the uncertainty. A/B testing can estimate a bounded intervention when volume and follow-up allow useful comparison. It cannot by itself decide whether the company has selected the right market or engine. Verna's framework discussion warns that tactical testing can obscure strategic problems. Do not turn this into a blanket ban on experiments. [S1]

Keep forecasts conditional until the relevant transitions are supported. If evidence is missing, a decision-ready result can be a hypothesis brief: state what cannot yet be concluded, which assumption is decisive, and the smallest practical next step to learn about it. [S4]

## 8. Own the system and plan for decline

Assign one accountable owner to the loop outcome, with cooperating functions and actual capacity. Verna describes a Dropbox growth pod focused on its main loop; the broader framework recommends cross-functional alignment on loop outputs rather than disconnected departmental targets. [S1] [S2]

If organizational design or core product capacity blocks execution, include that dependency in the recommendation. Verna warns that growth teams may be created too early, under-resourced, placed incorrectly, or expected to absorb responsibility that belongs across the organization. Product quality and retained value cannot be delegated away. [S6]

For hiring advice within the requested scope, let the model determine the needed profile and placement. Verna favors proving the first model with a builder, then using optimizers when traction exists or innovators when the hypothesis needs revision. Consider internal talent and initially embedded responsibility rather than automatically hiring an external Head of Growth. These are contextual recommendations from her hiring article, not universal staffing mandates. [S4]

Set deterioration signals and a review date. Rising acquisition costs, falling qualified exposure, weaker retention, slower payback, or audience saturation can change the mechanism. Explore adjacent users by checking where nearby personas or use cases already receive value; do not assume a broad new audience will behave like the core audience. Sequencing new products or loops may be necessary as existing mechanisms mature. [S1] [S6]

## 9. Explicit warnings and edge cases

The warnings below paraphrase the fetched public material. The response column turns them into practical review decisions.

| Source-backed warning | Practical response |
| --- | --- |
| Tactical tests can distract from a strategy problem. [S1] | State the engine hypothesis before selecting conversion experiments. |
| Linear funnels omit reinvestment and encourage strategic or functional silos. [S2] | Show closure and give cooperating functions a common output. |
| Many weak loops do not equal a durable growth strategy. [S2] | Concentrate evidence and resources on the strongest candidates. |
| Temporary boosts, optimization, and engine building can each be used at the wrong stage. [S3] | Diagnose inputs, remaining engine upside, and saturation before choosing work. |
| Pricing or referral pressure can damage the engine while increasing its fuel. [S3] | Evaluate conversion, product experience, and retained value together. |
| Outsourcing the first model invites context-poor imitation. [S4] | Keep founders accountable for the first hypothesis and use observed organic behavior. |
| Revenue alone misses the journey where controllable levers sit. [S4] | Include acquisition, activation, retention, and monetization evidence. |
| Logins, isolated activity, paid-only cohorts, non-activated retention, and user-only B2B metrics mislead. [S5] | Define value, frequency, activation boundary, free cohorts, and customer unit. |
| Loops degrade, organizations block growth, and attribution is uncertain. [S6] | Set review triggers, expose capacity constraints, and qualify causal claims. |

Additional package adaptations for difficult cases:

- **Sensitive or private workflows:** Sharing may be unnatural or inappropriate. Inspect voluntary customer behavior and consider paid, content, or sales mechanisms rather than assuming every product should be viral.
- **Multiple participant types:** Track each role and the transitions between them. A buyer, collaborator, and account creator may have different eligibility rules.
- **User-generated content:** Content is fuel only if it becomes discoverable and attracts useful participants. Include quality, distribution eligibility, and publishing or indexing delays.
- **Networks and defensibility:** A network effect may improve value as participation rises, but it is not itself evidence of customer acquisition. Name the actual distribution transition.
- **Concurrent engines:** Keep separate denominators and cash or participant pools before modeling interactions. Do not add overlapping cohorts as independent growth.
- **Long enterprise cycles:** Use a horizon that includes activation, contract signing, collection, and renewal where relevant. Do not infer payback from a short lead-generation pilot.
- **No validated loop:** Say so. Return a testable mechanism and evidence plan instead of manufacturing a circle.

Return the brief using [the output template](../templates/output-template.md), then apply [the review checklist](../checklists/review-checklist.md). The final artifact should explain what the system produces, what powers its next cycle, what currently limits it, and which owned decision the evidence supports.
