# Worked examples

Both scenarios are invented illustrations. Their facts, drafts, fees, and targets are fictional, not Moore's cases or evidence of performance. Citations identify the rules applied. See [method.md](../references/method.md) for the published guidance and agent adaptations.

## Illustration 1: A LinkedIn post for an onboarding consultant

### Brief and buyer picture

A consultant helps small software businesses investigate onboarding friction. The intended reader is a founder whose new users register but do not complete their first useful task. The seller has no approved case study for this draft. The objective is a relevant conversation about the drop-off, not a guaranteed activation increase.

Known in the invented brief: the offer includes an onboarding audit and, if warranted, a rewrite. Unknown: whether any reader's problem is onboarding, unsuitable traffic, or something else.

### Weak first draft

> Our proprietary onboarding optimization framework combines UX heuristics, lifecycle orchestration, and behavioral analytics. We offer audits, workshops, and implementation packages. We're building an amazing consultancy and our peers love the framework. Book a call today and we'll double your activation.

Problems: it speaks to specialists, foregrounds components, gives an unexplained menu, and promises an unsupported result. It also diagnoses every reader implicitly without evidence. Peer praise is not buyer-relevant proof. [S1][S3][S6]

### Apply the method

1. Replace the jargon with an observable founder problem.
2. Distinguish onboarding friction from acquisition mismatch.
3. Give the reader a useful inspection step.
4. Explain the seller's bounded diagnostic approach rather than inventing a testimonial.
5. Invite a reply that helps establish the context before proposing a call.

### Improved result: Ready-to-use post

> New users sign up, but few complete the first task that makes your software useful.
>
> Before rewriting every onboarding email, check two things: where users stop, and whether those users arrived expecting what your product actually does.
>
> If suitable users repeatedly stop at the same setup step, the instructions may be getting in their way. If users from one campaign disappear before setup even starts, you may need to inspect the campaign promise first.
>
> Pick one recent signup group. Compare the acquisition message with the first useful task, then inspect the steps between them.
>
> In an onboarding audit, I start by separating those explanations so the work targets the likely cause.
>
> If this is happening in your product, reply with the first task you want a new user to complete and where they stop. We can work out what to inspect next.

### Rules that made the difference

- **Buyer relevance:** A founder can recognize the opening without knowing industry jargon. This implements Moore's audience warning. [S3]
- **Help plus direction:** The post provides a useful distinction and one next step. [S2]
- **Credibility through reasoning:** It demonstrates a diagnostic approach without fictional proof. This is an agent adaptation of Moore's expertise guidance. [S1]
- **Uncertainty preserved:** It names possible causes without claiming to know each reader's situation. This is an agent adaptation of diagnosis before prescription. [S1]
- **Outcome relevance:** The focus is reaching the first useful task rather than buying an audit as an end in itself. [S6]

Review result: the draft passes the content checks. Actual effectiveness would require tracking relevant replies and subsequent conversations; no conversion outcome is assumed. [S2][S3]

## Illustration 2: Discovery, recommendation, and a price concern

### Brief and buyer picture

An operations consultant sells a focused intake and handoff review. In this invented scenario, a design agency director says projects repeatedly begin late. The seller initially suspects scheduling software. After discovery, the director confirms that six of the last eight projects waited for missing briefs; three people thought someone else was responsible for collecting them. The agency already has a scheduling tool. The director wants fewer preventable delays, and one team lead can own a pilot.

All figures are fictional scenario inputs. They are not testimonials. The available budget is initially unknown.

### Weak first draft

> You need our scheduling system. Choose Basic at EUR 2,000, Growth at EUR 5,000, or Complete at EUR 9,000. Complete is best because it includes the most support. If that feels expensive, I can cut the fee today. This will eliminate your delays.

Problems: the seller prescribes a tool before understanding the cause, delegates the diagnosis through a package menu, and discounts without investigating hesitation. The guarantee ignores other possible sources of delay. [S1][S7][S9]

### Apply the method: Discovery plan

Ask what is waiting when a project misses its start date. Then ask which recent projects show that pattern, how intake responsibility is assigned, and what the current scheduling tool already handles. Ask what a workable pilot would require and who could approve it.

If briefs are complete but calendars conflict, inspect scheduling. If missing inputs repeatedly block starts, inspect intake responsibility. If both happen, determine which is the first bottleneck rather than choosing the most expensive package.

### Improved result: Confirmed summary and recommendation

> You described six recent starts waiting for incomplete briefs, while responsibility for collecting those inputs was unclear. Your scheduling tool is already in place. It sounds as though the first problem to test is ownership of intake rather than another calendar system. Does that match what your team sees?
>
> If that summary is correct, I recommend a two-week intake pilot at EUR 2,000. We will define what a complete brief contains, assign one owner to collect it, and test the handoff on the next eligible projects. Your team lead would approve the checklist and record which starts wait for missing inputs.
>
> The goal is fewer starts delayed by incomplete briefs. We will compare the pilot projects with the recent pattern and review the remaining causes together. This pilot does not address resource shortages or promise that every project starts on time.
>
> The next decision is whether this scope and fee fit your priorities. If they do, we can confirm the team lead and pilot start date.

### Improved result: Objection response

The director says, in the invented scenario: the fee is difficult to justify.

> What makes the fee difficult to justify: the amount available now, uncertainty that intake is the main issue, or the time your team would need to contribute?

If the director says the budget ceiling is EUR 1,000:

> That ceiling rules out the full pilot. A single intake review at EUR 1,000 could identify the handoff changes, with your team running the test afterward. It would exclude implementation support. If that still does not fit, we can leave the project here.

If the director instead doubts the cause:

> Let's check that explanation before committing. Could your team lead review the blocked starts and identify which actually waited for missing inputs? If the pattern does not hold, I would revise the recommendation.

### Follow-through plan

If the pilot is agreed, the consultant and team lead review it after two weeks. They inspect the recorded reasons for delayed starts, whether the new ownership process was followed, and whether missing briefs remain the main obstacle. A small pilot is directional evidence, not proof of a lasting effect. New evidence may require a different diagnosis.

### Rules that made the difference

- **Diagnosis first:** The questions distinguish missing inputs from scheduling problems. [S1]
- **Confirmed understanding:** The seller invites correction before prescribing. This is an agent adaptation of listening and making the buyer feel understood. [S1][S9]
- **One justified recommendation:** The pilot addresses the observed bottleneck and gives a decision path. [S1][S6]
- **Concern before rebuttal:** The response explores hesitation before modifying the offer. [S7][S9]
- **Real constraints respected:** A budget limit changes scope or ends the discussion. This is an agent adaptation, not a claim that Moore's trust advice eliminates affordability issues.
- **Results checked:** Follow-through tests whether the proposed intervention helps. [S1]
