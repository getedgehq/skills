# Worked examples

Both scenarios are invented illustrations. Channels, analytics, observations, titles, and outcomes are fictional. These examples demonstrate reasoning, not predicted performance. The compact strategy page would link to the candidate lists and briefs shown here.

## Illustration 1: A gardening investigation

### Weak first draft

A weekly backyard gardening channel proposes **Tomato Pest Management Update, Episode 12**. Its thumbnail contains the host, three plants, two insects, a logo, four arrows, and technical text. The team claims the idea is strong because a different gardening video has 200,000 views, but has not checked that channel's usual performance. The planned intro opens with a lengthy channel update.

The draft hides the consequence from newcomers, makes the image hard to process, offers weak demand evidence, and delays confirming the premise. [S1][S4][S6]

### Apply the method

The creator can run documented trials in a demonstration garden, an access and knowledge advantage. The starting sub-niche is preventable tomato harvest loss. Core viewers want causal detail, casual viewers want practical prevention, and new viewers can understand losing a harvest despite healthy-looking leaves. [S1][S5][S6]

For this fictional strategy exercise, the team has 100 ideas. One internal pest experiment ranks second among 20 comparable uploads. A fictional external investigation has 180,000 views against a stated 60,000-view average across ten comparable uploads at 30 days: a ratio of 3.0. A real deliverable would include URLs and observation dates; none is supplied here because the evidence is invented. [S1][S2]

The lead premise becomes: **Investigate why apparently healthy tomato plants produced a damaged harvest, using documented garden evidence.** Demand, viewer fit, creator advantage, and package clarity pass. The team does not claim a pest is responsible until the investigation supports that conclusion.

### Title exploration

2. The Hidden Cause of Our Ruined Tomato Harvest
3. Our Tomatoes Looked Fine. The Harvest Didn't.
4. What Destroyed This Tomato Harvest?
5. Can Healthy Leaves Hide a Ruined Harvest?
6. We Investigated a Tomato Harvest That Went Wrong

9. What Our Failed Tomato Harvest Taught Us
10. We Checked Every Clue Behind These Damaged Tomatoes

These explore investigation, contradiction, comparison, and lesson angles, rather than ten adjective substitutions. Ten candidates follow the earlier lesson list. [S2]

### Thumbnail exploration and production briefs

Five concepts: two contrasting harvest trays; a cut-open damaged fruit; a healthy canopy above damaged tomatoes; the host inspecting a tiny suspect; a documented close-up of an overlooked clue.

| Variant brief | Composition | Footage or assets needed | Truth check |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A: contrasting trays | Two real harvest trays, one intact and one damaged, on a plain surface | Matching photographs from the documented harvests | No manipulated yields or claim that trials were identical |
| B: hidden interior | One tomato cut in half with damage clearly visible | Macro shot of an actual affected fruit | Show the observed damage, not an invented pest |
| C: misleading appearance | Healthy-looking foliage above a small cluster of damaged fruit | Photographs of the plants and fruit from the investigation | Avoid implying the fruit is visibly attached if it is a composite |

Five concepts and three planned executions follow the public recommendations. Actual rendering remains pending. [S1][S3]

### Improved result

**Selected title:** Our Tomatoes Looked Fine. The Harvest Didn't.

**Selected thumbnail:** Variant B. A brief small-size test makes the damaged interior easier to identify than the tray comparison. The title supplies the contradiction; the image supplies visible stakes. The opening shows the real cut fruit and healthy-looking plants, then states the investigation question within five seconds. The final video documents the evidence before naming a cause. [S4][S6]

**Learning plan:** Review at 7 and 30 days against comparable uploads, inspect early retention, and record which audience groups arrived. These windows are illustrative planning choices. At roughly twice the stated baseline, commission a related investigation; after a second success, test it as a recurring format. [S2][S5]

**Rules that changed the result:** A baseline replaced raw popularity; viewer mapping made the stakes understandable; simplified visual evidence replaced clutter; opening confirmation replaced housekeeping. [S1][S4][S6]

## Illustration 2: A new woodworking channel

### Weak first draft

A new creator proposes **WX3400 Router Technical Review** with a thumbnail showing a catalogue product photograph, a specification table, a logo, and promotional badges. The first title revision is **This Cheap Tool Beats Every Expensive Router**, even though the test has not happened. The creator wants weekly uploads but can only film one careful experiment monthly.

The revision promises an unsupported result and the schedule conflicts with execution capacity. There is no internal outlier history. [S1][S4][S6]

### Apply the method

Choose one quality-focused monthly upload initially, within the low frequency model. The sub-niche is practical tool comparisons for small workshops. The creator's skill advantage is building repeatable cutting fixtures and explaining measurements clearly. Core viewers want test conditions, casual viewers want buying relevance, and newcomers understand comparing a cheap object with an expensive one. [S1][S6]

The strategy pool contains 100 fictional concepts. An external test is interesting but lacks baseline data, so it is labelled an unverified comparable. The selected idea is tagged **innovation**, with an explicit hypothesis: a controlled visual comparison can make price versus performance compelling. No invented demand ratio is used.

**Premise:** Test whether an inexpensive and a premium router can produce equally clean edges under the same stated conditions. The demand gate is provisional, supported by the hypothesis rather than confirmed outlier evidence. Production can proceed as a bounded experiment with a pre-agreed budget, while outcome-specific packaging waits for the footage.

### Title exploration

1. Can a Cheap Router Match a Premium One?
2. Two Routers, One Cut: What Does Price Change?
3. What Does the Expensive Router Actually Buy You?
4. We Put Budget and Premium Routers Through the Same Test
5. Can You Spot the Cheap Router's Cut?
6. The Router Price Gap, Tested on Real Wood
7. Is a Premium Router Worth It for This Cut?
8. I Tested Two Router Prices in My Small Workshop
9. Same Wood, Different Routers: Which Edge Is Cleaner?
10. The Cut Test Behind My Next Router Purchase

Candidate 5 is usable only if the footage supports a fair comparison and the video includes the promised identification challenge. The titles keep the unknown result open. [S2][S4]

### Thumbnail exploration and production briefs

Five concepts: two edge samples; two tools with price labels; a macro comparison of tear-out; the host holding both samples; a single divided workpiece showing the two tested edges.

| Variant brief | Composition | Footage or assets needed | Truth check |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A: edge samples | Two equal-sized wood samples with clear edge detail | Same lighting and scale for both actual test results | No invented defects or selective smoothing |
| B: tool prices | Two routers with concise actual price labels | Original tool photographs and dated purchase prices | No price claims based on unavailable sale offers |
| C: divided workpiece | One workpiece with the separately tested edges marked | Documented test setup and resulting board | Explain which cut each tool made; do not imply simultaneous cutting |

### Improved result

**Pre-filming title:** Can a Cheap Router Match a Premium One?

**Selected planning thumbnail:** Variant B, because the actual result images do not exist yet. Two tools and their price labels express the comparison without claiming a winner. Price labels are grouped with each tool so the image has two principal focal clusters. The creator must verify the glance test with actual images. [S6]

**Opening plan:** Show both tools and the test board, then state the comparison in the first five seconds. Document wood, bit, settings, and limitations. If the later result supports a stronger truthful angle, revisit the package before publication; do not turn a single test into a universal product claim. [S4]

**Learning plan:** Build an initial baseline from comparable uploads, without calling the first video's performance a verified internal outlier. Track retention and audience reached. Continue controlled comparisons if viewer response supports them; a twice-average rule becomes usable only after a meaningful channel baseline exists. [S2][S3][S5]

**Rules that changed the result:** The schedule follows capacity; absence of evidence stays visible; questions preserve uncertain outcomes; practical stakes make technical content accessible. [S1][S4][S6]
