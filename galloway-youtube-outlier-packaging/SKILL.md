# Paddy Galloway's One Page YouTube Strategy

Based on Paddy Galloway's public method. Not affiliated with or endorsed by Paddy Galloway.

## When to use

Use this skill when a creator or team needs a compact YouTube plan, a prioritized idea slate, or a title and thumbnail brief for a proposed video. It is especially useful before filming, when the biggest decisions are still cheap to change. It can also diagnose a channel that publishes consistently but has few videos that outperform its own average. [S1][S3]

## Procedure

### 1. Establish the channel's operating model

- Gather the channel's niche, recent long form uploads and view counts, production capacity, creator strengths, intended audience, and any constraints on access, budget, or filming. If the channel is new, state which inputs are estimates. Pick one of Galloway's three models for the planning period: a low frequency, high quality approach with roughly one or two uploads a month; a high frequency approach with at least two a week; or a weekly long form approach. Do not prescribe a cadence that the team cannot execute with strong ideas and packaging. [S1]
- Identify the channel's defensible advantage in four possible forms: unusual knowledge, a skill others cannot readily perform, a distinctive personality, or access to people, places, or things others cannot show. Write one specific proof point for each claimed advantage. Reject generic claims such as "we are passionate" until they become something a viewer could actually see in a video. [S1]
- If the niche is wide, select one or two sub-niches to win first. Expand outward only after a recognizable format works there. Avoid a channel whose next video attracts a substantially different audience from its last, because the repeat viewing pattern is harder to build. [S5]

### 2. Define three viewer circles

- Describe the core viewer who watches most uploads, the casual viewer who watches some, and the new viewer who has never watched the channel. For each, write the topics, stakes, and recognizable language that might draw them in. Seek ideas that reward core viewers while offering an understandable entry point for casual and new viewers. Do not make every idea so insider focused that only subscribers understand it, or so broad that the creator's actual expertise disappears. [S1][S6]
- Test a promising idea by asking how someone outside the niche would understand its consequence. Galloway's public example turns a specialist land management topic into a question about the Sahara Desert. This is a change in framing and audience reach, not a reason to promise content the video does not deliver. [S6][S4]

### 3. Build a large, evidence tagged idea pool

- Generate at least 100 rough ideas for a channel strategy pass, and repeat the exercise monthly. Keep three source tags. "Internal outlier" means a topic or format that already ranked in roughly the top tenth of this channel's uploads. "External outlier" means a comparable video on another channel that earned about three times that channel's usual views. "Innovation" means an original concept or surprising combination that is not merely a remake of the evidence set. Record the source video and comparison baseline whenever an outlier claim is used. [S1][S2]
- Schedule regular study of YouTube itself. Save unusually successful videos to a playlist, then review them for the transferable idea, format, viewer promise, and packaging choice. Galloway recommends about an hour a day or five hours a week of deliberate study, plus frequent idea generation. Treat a raw view count without a channel baseline as weak evidence: a million views on a channel that normally gets millions is different from a million on a small channel. [S1][S2][S3]
- Adapt the mechanism of an external outlier to the creator's advantage and audience. Study formats outside the niche when they expose a useful pattern. Preserve a lane for original concepts, since Galloway's one page framework includes innovation alongside internal and external outliers. Do not copy another creator's exact promise, image, or execution and call it strategy. [S1][S2]

### 4. Select ideas before production

- Shortlist ideas using four gates. First, is there evidence of demand or a clearly reasoned novel hook? Second, can core, casual, and new viewers each find a reason to care? Third, can the creator uniquely execute it? Fourth, can a stranger grasp its appeal through one short title and one thumbnail? Mark a failed gate explicitly, then revise or discard the idea before spending on production. An interesting subject that cannot be conveyed in the package is not ready to film. [S1][S4][S6]
- For each finalist, write a single sentence that names the premise, the surprising tension, and the result or question the video will resolve. Ask a few relevant people whether that sentence makes them curious. Use their reaction to diagnose clarity, not as a vote that overrides the creator's judgment. Galloway describes novelty, broad appeal, simple packaging, and genuine excitement from people in the niche as traits of a strong idea. [S6]
- Reserve occasional "home run" ideas that can reach a wider slice of the niche and justify greater filming, budget, and packaging effort. Do not make every upload such a swing. Galloway frames these videos as potential lifts to the whole channel, while also warning that repeated attempts are needed because outcomes remain uncertain. [S4]

### 5. Package the idea as one combined promise

- Draft at least ten materially different titles for each selected upload. Vary the angle, concrete stakes, and unanswered question, rather than making ten near synonyms. Write a thumbnail concept alongside the title before filming so the shoot can capture the necessary visual proof. If the package needs a scene or object the production cannot actually obtain, revise the idea or plan the shot. [S2][S6]
- Brainstorm at least five distinct thumbnail concepts and make at least three actual variants for a serious upload. Test different visual arguments, such as a startling comparison, a visible outcome, or an unusual object, rather than changing only colors or fonts. Keep the thumbnail to one to three focal elements. View it small and briefly: if the central image cannot be understood at a glance, remove elements or choose a clearer frame. [S1][S3][S6]
- Evaluate each title and thumbnail pair through Galloway's three stages of a click: does an image, emotion, or concise phrase stop scrolling; does the pair open a clear curiosity gap; and does the viewer believe the promised payoff is worth watching? Make title and image complementary. The package should raise a question the video answers, without hiding the subject so completely that the viewer cannot tell what it is. [S2][S4]
- Prefer the package that communicates the strongest truthful idea quickly, even when a prettier design is available. Do not use packaging that implies a result, person, access, or experiment absent from the video. The opening should visibly or verbally confirm the promise almost immediately; Galloway suggests delivery within the first five seconds, and he treats viewer satisfaction as more important than a click that ends in disappointment. [S4][S6]

### 6. Learn from the result and repeat winners

- After publishing, compare views with the channel's normal range, inspect retention drop points, and note who the video reached. Interpret click metrics in context rather than treating a single click through rate or average view duration as a universal score. If the video is performing, keep the working title and thumbnail. If viewers leave when the promise should be delivered, fix the mismatch in the next production instead of assuming a new thumbnail can repair the video. [S1][S2][S3][S4]
- When an idea or format reaches about twice the channel's average views, try a related episode promptly. If it works twice, turn it into a repeatable format and continue exploring it. Galloway warns creators against abandoning a winning format merely because they fear repetition; he also recommends continuing to test other ideas so the channel can find its next outlier. [S2][S5]
- Keep the process moving. Spend meaningful time on ideas, titles, thumbnails, and intros, but set a practical deadline for choosing among good variants and publish. Galloway's lesson is to study, improve, and make repeated attempts, not to seek certainty before every upload. [S3][S4][S6]

## Finished output

- Produce one readable strategy page containing the operating model, chosen sub-niche, specific creator advantage, core/casual/new viewer map, and the current idea sourcing routine. Include the size of the idea pool, the selected ideas, their internal/external/innovation tags, and links plus baselines for cited outliers. Flag missing evidence rather than inventing performance data. [S1][S2][S5]
- For the lead idea, provide its one sentence premise, why it passes the four gates, at least ten title candidates, five thumbnail concepts, three production ready thumbnail briefs, the selected title and thumbnail pair, the visual proof needed during filming, and the opening promise check. End with the planned post upload review and the condition for repeating the format. [S2][S3][S4][S5][S6]

## Worked example: illustration

An invented backyard gardening channel posts weekly. Its host can test plants in a public demonstration garden, an access advantage. Core viewers want cultivation detail, casual viewers want practical results, and new viewers understand the cost of a ruined harvest. The team logs 100 ideas. One internal outlier shows that pest experiments outperform routine care guides; an external video about unexpected garden damage exceeds its channel's usual views threefold. The candidate is: "Can one hidden pest destroy a tomato harvest before the leaves look sick?" This is a hypothetical premise and invented evidence, not a claim about any real channel.

The team explores ten title angles and five visual concepts. Three thumbnail briefs show a healthy looking plant beside damaged fruit, a close view of the pest against a tomato, and two harvest trays with sharply different yields. A small size glance test favors the two trays. The chosen title asks what destroyed the harvest, while the image shows the stakes without giving away the answer. The opening shows the damaged fruit and begins the investigation promptly. After publication, the team compares views with its own baseline and checks whether viewers stay through the first explanation. If the episode exceeds twice the usual views, it tries another hidden cause investigation soon. This illustrates the sequence, not a guarantee of performance. [S1][S4][S5][S6]
