## Illustration 1: Founder perspective on agent tools

### Brief and fictional inputs

A founder wants a LinkedIn post for teams deploying their first internal agent. In this invented scenario, the founder's team added several tools during a pilot. The agent sometimes selected a plausible but incorrect tool, and debugging required untangling those choices. The supplied fictional record contains no measured failure rate or comparison proving a universal advantage for fewer tools.

### Weak first draft

> AI agents are powerful, but teams should be thoughtful about deployment. Good guardrails and the right tools are essential. What do you think is the best approach?

### Applying the method

- Observation: More tool choices made this particular pilot harder to diagnose.
- Interpretation: Early deployment should prioritize understandable behavior over breadth.
- Assertion: Begin with the smallest tool set that completes a useful job.
- Credible opposing view: Broader tool access exposes workflow needs sooner and gives the agent more ways to recover.
- Obvious objection: A restricted agent may not be able to finish useful work.
- Boundary: This is a starting recommendation for a team's first internal deployment, not a claim about every mature agent.

### Improved result

> Give your first internal agent the smallest tool set that can finish one useful job.
>
> In our pilot, adding tools gave the agent more plausible choices, including choices we did not intend. When it failed, we had to untangle tool selection before we could diagnose the underlying task.
>
> Broader access may help you discover new workflows. For a first deployment, I would prioritize a workflow we can understand and support.
>
> Pick a job worth completing. Give the agent the tools that job requires. Add another tool when a reviewed failure shows a capability is missing, then check whether the addition solves that problem.

### Rules that made the difference

The opening advocates a disputed priority rather than repeating accepted advice. The opposing view names a real benefit of breadth. The pilot story supports a bounded recommendation without inventing a percentage. The final passage shows a decision rule and explains what to do differently. [S1][S3][S9][S13]

Confidence stays at the level of a founder's recommendation based on one pilot. If a real user has no such pilot, replace the experience paragraph with an explicitly proposed mechanism and present the recommendation as a hypothesis to test. [S5]

## Illustration 2: Hiring for constructive disagreement

### Brief and fictional inputs

A people leader wants an essay opening for founders making early hires. In the invented scenario, their interview rubric rewarded fluent statements of company values, but did not test how candidates raised concerns. The leader can describe that gap but has no evidence that disagreeable candidates outperform other hires.

### Weak first draft

> Culture fit is important in an early team. You want people who align with your values but also bring diverse perspectives. Hiring is ultimately about finding the right balance.

### Applying the method

- Observation: Candidates can describe agreement without demonstrating judgment under disagreement.
- Interpretation: A values conversation alone misses an important early-team behavior.
- Assertion: Include a disagreement exercise before treating alignment as sufficient evidence of fit.
- Credible opposing view: A small team needs fast execution, and extra debate can consume scarce founder attention.
- Obvious objection: This could reward combative candidates.
- Boundary: Test constructive challenge and follow-through, while retaining essential values criteria.

### Improved result

> Early-team interviews should test how candidates disagree before treating shared values as sufficient evidence of fit.
>
> Our old rubric rewarded candidates who could explain why they liked our values. It told us little about what they would do when a founder's plan looked flawed.
>
> My recommendation is to add a realistic decision exercise: ask the candidate to identify a weak assumption, explain its consequence, and propose a workable alternative. Then introduce a constraint and ask how they would proceed if the team chose another path.
>
> Small teams need speed. That is why the exercise should test whether someone can surface a material concern and still help execute a decision. Reward useful judgment and follow-through, rather than the volume of objections.

### Rules that made the difference

The revised position disputes the sufficiency of a common hiring signal without inventing an opponent who rejects all diversity. A concrete exercise makes the interpretation usable. The objection changes the design of the exercise, and the scope preserves essential values checks. [S1][S8][S9]

The draft avoids unsupported claims about superior hiring outcomes. A real author should validate the rubric gap before adopting the first-person paragraph. [S5][S13]

## Illustration 3: Observation to a provisional assertion

### Brief

A consultant notices founders posting daily but has no data on their conversion or time allocation.

### Weak first draft

> More founders are posting every day. Is daily posting the new normal?

### Improved result

> Before committing to daily posting, an early-stage founder should name what the extra post is meant to change.
>
> My hypothesis is that a sustainable schedule tied to a clear learning or pipeline goal is more useful than a daily quota. If the extra post has no purpose, use that time to investigate a customer question and turn the answer into a more specific piece.

### Rule notes

The writer answers the question with a scoped recommendation and explicitly labels the explanation as a hypothesis. It has a credible alternative: daily repetition may build skill and distribution. It is a draft for investigation, not a validated result or a claim based on invented experience. [S3][S4][S5]
