# Spiky Point of View

Based on Wes Kao's public method. Not affiliated.

A spiky point of view (SPOV) is "a perspective others can disagree with", a belief you feel strongly about and will advocate for, your thesis about topics in your realm of expertise [1]. The goal is not agreement. It is to start a conversation and make the reader see their problem in a new way [1][2].

Why this skill exists: by default Claude rounds edges (summaries, balanced both-sides takes, stacked qualifiers), or overcorrects into rage-bait. Wes Kao rules out both: if everyone agrees it is "too middle of the road", and a contrarian "who just wants to stir the pot" is "intellectually lazy" [1].

## The method

### Step 1. Mine raw candidates (go wide before you pick)
1. Draft at least 10 candidate POVs; expect some to be tame and some worth doubling down on [2]. Start from 3 to 5 if the user only needs one post [1].
2. Generate them with her exercise questions [1]:
   - What are 3 to 5 spiky points of view you have about your craft?
   - What do you believe that others might disagree with? (Only on topics where you are credible and that matter to your audience; otherwise "it's a random hot take or opinion, and opinions are cheap.")
   - What do you wish more people understood? It should make you "a little angry or frustrated". If you are ambivalent, it is not worth convincing anyone.
   - What have you said that made customers or clients say "Hmm I never thought of it like that before"?
   - What generally accepted best practice do you think is not useful or does not work, based on your experience?
3. When working from a user's material, pull candidates only from their domain, experience, and evidence. Do not copy someone else's SPOV; borrowed conviction has less energy [1].

### Step 2. Convert observations into assertions
4. An observation or "insight" alone gets "Hmm interesting. That's nice to know" and nothing changes [3]. Push every candidate up the ladder: insight (what I noticed) -> suggestion (options) -> assertion (what we should do and why) [3].
5. An assertion must answer "So what?", carry personal conviction, and show ownership ("here's what I'm betting on") [3].
6. Interpret the facts. A fact alone is just a fact; given meaning through your lens it becomes a point of view [3]. Facts have multiple interpretations, so state the "so what" yourself instead of making the reader do it [6].
7. If the draft ends on a question, answer it. Asking keeps you "at arm's length"; answering puts skin in the game [4][3]. A smart question followed by your assertion is "the next level" [3].

### Step 3. Test each candidate against the five traits [1]
8. Debatable: a credible person could disagree. If everyone nods, it is too middle of the road.
9. Not controversial for its own sake: there is a real reason behind it, not a stir.
10. Teaches something relevant the audience does not already know. Do not just summarize, do not only ask questions. Target reaction: "I hadn't thought about it that way, but this is so true."
11. Rooted in evidence, but need not be a proven universal truth. It must be defensible; waiting for 100% consensus means never saying it.
12. Requires conviction: advocacy and a bias toward action, because you believe the reader will be better for it.
13. Debatability check: write the strongest opposing SPOV a smart peer would hold. Wes Kao does this herself for her failure take and calls the opposing view "totally valid" [1]. If you cannot write a credible opposing view, the claim is not spiky. If the opposing view is a strawman nobody holds, the claim is fake-spiky.
14. Frames count. A SPOV can be the frame you use to describe an idea (for example "there are 2 ways to build community"), which others can contest by proposing 3 ways or different ways [1].

### Step 4. Sharpen generic to specific
15. First drafts come out with "rounded edges"; keep refining them to be more specific [1]. Her worked case: the slide bullet "teach as you learn" became (paraphrased) "not having it all figured out is a feature; there are 2 ways to build community: be an expert, or be a beginner", plus named creators as examples [1].
16. Pattern visible in her own SPOVs [1][2]: name the common belief or behavior, state your stance, give the mechanism, add a concrete number or scope where you have one ("Prototyping is unnecessary in 90% of cases"; "you only need to be a little better to win"; "take 30 minutes to scenario plan").
17. Show the Super Specific How: an example, script, screenshot, or exact words beats explaining that something matters [9]. Move fast past a "what" or "why" the audience already believes ("communication is important") and either show the how or offer a spikier POV [9].
18. Avoid the "black box of logic where suddenly it works" [13]. Make the mechanism explicit: upside, downside, data rooted in reality, constraints [13].

### Step 5. Calibrate conviction and language
19. Match confidence to evidence: do not overstate hypotheses as facts or understate strong recommendations [8][5]. On low-stakes public takes you can sound fully confident; on high-stakes decisions overclaiming is irresponsible [5].
20. Show reasoning with "I believe X because Y" style framing [8], or her assertion template: "Based on my insight of X, I believe that Y is true... I think it'll work because A and B. If it doesn't work, there's a risk of C" [3].
21. Do not bury the take under preambles, caveats, disclaimers, and counterpoints; she names that urge and publishes anyway because the SPOV starts the conversation [2].
22. Run MOO (Most Obvious Objection): spend a few seconds anticipating the most obvious objection and address it [8]. One objection handled, not a both-sides essay.
23. Prefer affirmative phrasing ("do this") over negation ("don't do that"); negatives make the reader take an extra mental step [10]. State things in the affirmative by default; break the rule only on purpose [11].
24. Delete weakening filler: "honestly", stacked "very/truly/really", "however" where "but" works [11].
25. Concise means density of insight, not short [7][8]. If you cannot state the main point in 2 to 3 sentences, the thinking is not done yet; clear thinking is the bottleneck [7].

### Step 6. Choose what to say, then test it
26. "Strategy, not self-expression": before publishing a line, ask whether it gets you closer to the goal (changing how the reader thinks or acts) or is venting or having the last word [15]. Trim the self-expression.
27. Sell before logistics: lead with why the reader should care now, in their interest, before the how [14].
28. Test with real people. Eyes Light Up (ELU) is the only reaction that matters; "Hmm interesting" is politeness [12]. Ask friends, post it, mention it on customer calls; lots of replies, including disagreement, is a good sign [1].

## Self-review checklist (run before returning output)
- [ ] Is there one clear claim, stated in the first lines, that a credible peer could disagree with? [1]
- [ ] Did I write the strongest opposing view, and is it a view real people hold (not a strawman)? [1]
- [ ] Is it in the author's domain of credibility and relevant to their audience? [1]
- [ ] Does it teach something non-obvious, not a summary of common knowledge? [1][9]
- [ ] Is the "so what" stated (what to do or believe differently), not left to the reader? [3][6]
- [ ] Is there evidence or a mechanism: an example, number, case, or observed pattern from the user's material? No invented stats. [1][9][13]
- [ ] Does certainty match evidence: no hedge stacking, no fake absolutes? [5][8]
- [ ] Is the most obvious objection handled in one beat? [8]
- [ ] Does any question in the draft get answered? [4]
- [ ] Is it phrased affirmatively, with filler words cut? [10][11]
- [ ] Is every line strategy, not self-expression? [15]
- [ ] Would a reader's eyes light up, or would they say "Hmm interesting"? [12]

## Failure modes (what plain Claude does, and the fix)
- Middle-of-the-road take everyone agrees with ("AI agents need good guardrails"). Fix: find the part of it a credible peer would dispute [1].
- Contrarian for its own sake ("X is dead", "everyone is wrong about Y") with no evidence or stake. Wes Kao calls this intellectually lazy [1]. Fix: keep the edge, add the mechanism and evidence, scope it.
- Observation dressed up as insight ("I'm seeing more teams do X"). Fix: add the assertion and "so what" [3].
- Ending on "What do you think?" with no answer of your own. Fix: answer first, then invite debate [4].
- Hedge stacking and pre-emptive disclaimers. Fix: one calibrated qualifier where the evidence is genuinely thin, otherwise commit [2][5].
- Generic "it's not X, it's Y" lines, where X is a position nobody holds. Fix: state the claim affirmatively [10][11] and pass the debatability check in Step 3 [1].
- Abstract "why" with no "how". Fix: Super Specific How, show the example or script [9].
- Borrowed take (another creator's SPOV reworded). Fix: ground it in the user's own experience [1].
- Hot take outside the author's credibility. "Opinions are cheap" [1]. Fix: move the claim into their domain.
- Treating "concise" as "short" and cutting the evidence. Fix: keep density, cut filler [7].

## Worked examples (illustrative, written for this skill, not Wes Kao's)

**1. Founder on AI agents**
- Bland: "AI agents are powerful, but companies need to be thoughtful about how they deploy them."
- Spiky: "Most teams should give their first agent fewer tools, not more. Every tool we added in our pilot widened the space of wrong actions faster than it widened the useful ones. Start with one tool and one job, and add the second only when the logs show the agent stalling for lack of it."
- Why it passes: debatable (many builders believe broad tool access is the point), mechanism given, scoped to "first agent", ends on an action. Evidence must come from the user's real pilot; if there is none, say "in my experience" and do not invent numbers.

**2. Hiring**
- Bland: "Culture fit matters when hiring early employees."
- Spiky: "Stop screening early hires for culture fit. Screen for how they disagree. A 10-person team gets more from one person who can say 'I think this is wrong, here's why, here's what I'd do instead' in the first month than from five who nod."
- Opposing view (credible): "Early teams can't afford friction; alignment beats debate at 10 people." The opposing view is real, so the claim is spiky, not a strawman.

**3. Observation to assertion**
- Observation: "Lots of B2B founders are posting daily on LinkedIn now."
- Assertion: "If you are a B2B founder with fewer than 20 customers, post twice a week and spend the saved hours on 10 customer calls. Daily posting builds reach you cannot convert yet; calls give you the specific stories that make the posts worth reading later."

## Output guidance for a LinkedIn post
1. Line 1 to 2: the claim itself, affirmative and specific. No warm-up, no "hot take incoming" [1][10].
2. Next 2 to 4 lines: the evidence or mechanism (example, number, case from the user's own material) [1][9].
3. One line naming the most obvious objection and answering it [8].
4. The "so what": what the reader should do or believe differently, framed in their interest [3][14].
5. Optional close: invite disagreement after you have committed to your answer, never instead of it [4][1].
6. Length: as long as the evidence needs, no filler [7]. Calibrated certainty: low-stakes public take, so commit unless the evidence is thin [5].
7. When the user gives several bland opinions, run Step 3 on each, pick the one with the strongest credible opposing view plus real evidence, say briefly why you picked it, then write the post.
8. Never fabricate numbers, customers, or quotes to make a take feel evidenced. Ask, or use explicitly hedged experience language.
