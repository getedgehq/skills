# Detailed method

Based on Wes Kao's public method. Not affiliated with or endorsed by Wes Kao.

## Scope and attribution

A spiky point of view is a personally held interpretation or recommendation that others can dispute. It reflects how the author practices their craft and explains the choices they make. It should help an audience reconsider a relevant problem. Disagreement is compatible with success; unsupported provocation is not. [S1]

The five traits and discovery prompts below come from Kao's core essay. The supporting communication principles come from her other public articles. The selection procedure, evidence labels, template, and pass/fail review are this package's operational adaptations. They are not a separate scoring system invented or endorsed by Kao. All source material is paraphrased; titles and method names identify the public references.

## Candidate discovery

Before polishing, generate alternatives. Kao's introductory exercise starts with three to five beliefs about one's craft, while her follow-up recommends ten or more when exploring a broader inventory. These serve different levels of exploration. Use the smaller set for one constrained deliverable and the larger set when the author is defining a body of positions. [S1][S2]

Ask the author, or inspect their supplied material for:

- A belief a capable practitioner might dispute.
- A recurring misunderstanding they wish their audience would resolve.
- A subject that creates enough frustration or enthusiasm to motivate advocacy.
- An explanation that has caused customers or colleagues to reconsider a problem.
- An accepted practice that has performed poorly in their experience.

These are prompts for discovery, not permission to invent the answers. Stay inside the intersection of author credibility and audience relevance. A strongly felt opinion outside that intersection is a poor candidate. Studying another person's perspective can help with form, but adopting their conclusion does not supply the author's own experience or commitment. [S1]

If supplied material contains only consensus statements, probe for decisions: When would the author allocate time differently? Which common recommendation would they replace? What constraint changes the usual advice? If there is still no stance, return a provisional hypothesis with the missing reasoning identified. Do not manufacture a controversial identity for the author.

## Observation to assertion

An observation identifies something noticed. A suggestion offers a possible response. An assertion interprets the situation, advocates a response, and takes ownership of the judgment. Kao describes action, conviction, and ownership as the distinguishing ingredients. [S3]

Develop each candidate through three questions:

1. What happened, and how do we know?
2. What does the author think it means, and why?
3. What should the reader now believe or do differently?

Facts do not force a single interpretation. An author may possess context the reader lacks, so make the bridge between evidence and conclusion visible. Emotional context can also communicate the seriousness of a situation, provided it is accurate and proportionate. Being objective does not require withholding judgment or acting detached. [S6]

A question may open the argument, but the author should attempt an answer. Kao warns that asking alone leaves the difficult decision with someone else. Her point supports inquiry followed by advocacy, rather than banning questions. A closing invitation to debate works after the position is clear. [S3][S4]

For a thought leadership piece, ownership means accurately representing a position the author endorses. For a decision memo, it may also include assumptions, downside, and accountability for the proposed course of action. Do not imply the writer can personally guarantee an outcome.

## The five traits

Use all five traits as qualitative tests, rather than rewarding the loudest wording. [S1]

| Trait | Passing condition | Failure signal |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Debatable | A credible reader could reasonably choose another position. | Agreement is nearly automatic because the statement is generic. |
| Useful purpose | Disagreement serves a relevant insight or better decision. | The main attraction is shocking people. |
| New learning | The audience gets a useful interpretation, distinction, or application. | The piece summarizes what its readers already know. |
| Defensibility | Evidence and reasoning can support the stated scope. | The conclusion depends on invented facts or unexplained leaps. |
| Conviction | The author is prepared to advocate the position. | The draft only lists possibilities or borrows someone else's certainty. |

Defensibility is not the same as universal proof. Kao explicitly allows evidence-based positions that are not settled objective truths. Waiting for complete agreement prevents sharing any debatable perspective. But that permission does not justify presenting speculation as an established result. [S1][S2][S5]

## Disagreement and selection

Write the strongest plausible opposing position in a working note. Identify its goal, assumptions, or trade-off. Kao illustrates that a competing perspective can be valid, even when she advocates her own. This package uses that example as a practical test for substantive disagreement. [S1]

A peer may disagree about timing, costs, priorities, acceptable risk, or the frame used to describe a problem. A numerical taxonomy or a proposed distinction can be spiky because others may contest its categories. The claim need not accuse conventional wisdom of being foolish. [S1]

Decision rules:

- If the opposite sounds absurd, check whether the draft is a truism or whether you have written a strawman.
- If the opposite is reasonable but the author has no reason to prefer their claim, return to evidence and conviction.
- If the author can defend a narrow claim but not a broad one, narrow the audience, context, or outcome.
- If several candidates pass, select the one most relevant to the requested reader change and best supported by the author's material.
- If none pass, explain the missing foundation and offer a hypothesis or a focused question rather than a fabricated finished conviction.

The opposing view is usually an internal test. Include it in the finished output only when it helps the reader understand a real boundary or trade-off.

## Specificity and mechanism

Kao notes that first attempts often become generic because writers soften their ideas to avoid pushback. Refinement should make the position more precise, rather than merely harsher. State the relevant actor, situation, decision, and reason. The disputed element can be a practice, priority, or conceptual frame. [S1]

Specificity may take the form of a condition, a workflow, an exact script, or a concrete example. It does not require a statistic. Never introduce an unsupported percentage to mimic a striking example in Kao's writing.

Her detailed teaching guidance favors showing actual artifacts and actions when the audience already accepts the underlying principle. A reader who believes communication matters may still need to see the words to use, the sequence to follow, or the decision rule to apply. This package adapts that teaching principle to opinion writing: include enough concrete application to make the recommendation usable. The course-design ratios in that article are not universal rules for posts. [S9]

Explain the causal bridge. Why should the recommended behavior improve the outcome? What costs, assets, constraints, and risks affect it? Kao warns against plans where a difficult step disappears into an unexplained leap to success. A mechanism should expose assumptions instead of hiding them behind a slogan. [S13]

Classify support in working notes:

- **Verified fact:** A supplied or checked source supports the exact statement.
- **Author experience:** The author actually reports the event or pattern; its scope remains limited.
- **Hypothesis:** A proposed explanation or prediction that has not been established.
- **Illustration:** An invented example used to explain the method, explicitly labeled.

These labels are package conventions. Keep them out of the finished piece when unnecessary, but preserve their distinctions in wording. Writing in the first person does not fix missing evidence. An unsupplied pilot cannot become a real experience simply by adding a phrase about personal experience.

## Confidence and language

Match confidence to evidence, stakes, intent, and power dynamics. Kao permits a forceful style for low-stakes public opinions but warns against overstating certainty when others depend on the recommendation for consequential decisions. She also warns that authoritative wording from a boss or coach can suppress dissent. [S5]

Use a direct recommendation when it is well supported. Use a scoped belief or hypothesis when uncertainty is material. Retain modifiers that change the meaning; remove qualifiers that only cushion discomfort. Avoid both unsupported absolutes and timid wording around a strong recommendation. [S5][S8]

Public posting is not automatically low stakes. Consider the claim's subject and consequences, rather than the platform alone. When a claim has significant consequences, preserve its evidence limits and conditions even if that makes the piece less punchy. This is this package's application of Kao's cost-of-being-wrong principle. [S5]

Prefer instructions that state the desired action. Negative phrasing makes the reader infer what to do instead and can sound reprimanding. A purposeful prohibition or familiar short negative expression can still be effective; Kao presents affirmative wording as a default with exceptions. [S10][S11]

Review unnecessary intensifiers, declarations of honesty, formal transitions, double negatives, and apologetic descriptions of neutral constraints. Keep an emphasis word when it carries deliberate meaning. This is a contextual edit, not a mechanical blacklist. [S11]

## Objections and reader motivation

Before sharing, anticipate the most obvious substantive objection. Kao's podcast recap identifies this as a quick preparation habit. This package recommends answering the main objection in one passage for a short piece, while allowing fuller treatment when stakes or complexity require it. [S8]

Use an objection to clarify the claim's boundary, not to add a ritual counterargument. If answering it changes the recommendation, revise the claim. If it reveals an unsupported premise, investigate or lower certainty. Do not erase a genuine exception just to preserve a sharper opening.

When action requires buy-in, show why the change benefits this reader before explaining logistics. Kao warns that more instructions cannot solve a motivation problem. A writer's reason for wanting compliance may not be the recipient's reason to act. When buy-in is already secure, proceeding directly to the how can be appropriate. [S14]

There is no conflict between reader motivation and concrete application: establish relevance where needed, then show how. An expert audience that already accepts the why may benefit from moving quickly to a specific implementation. [S9][S14]

Use the strategy test to decide what belongs. Does a sentence help the audience think or act differently? Kao warns against using persuasion or feedback as an outlet for venting, scoring a point, or forcing remorse. Emotion can discover a position; the finished argument should use that energy toward the intended outcome. [S1][S15]

## Concision and format

Concision means useful information per word, not minimal length. Kao explicitly warns against cutting necessary context or simplifying beyond what preserves meaning. Try summarizing the main position in two or three sentences to clarify thinking; this is preparation, not a universal length cap. [S7]

A short post can use claim, mechanism, boundary, and action. An essay can unpack the opposing position and more evidence. A talk thesis needs a central interpretation with examples that can support the talk. A positioning line may require a separate rationale because the line alone cannot carry the proof. Respect the requested format rather than forcing every assignment into a social post.

When a hard channel limit excludes necessary qualification, narrow the claim or move the fuller argument into an appropriate accompanying format. Do not satisfy brevity by misrepresenting certainty. [S5][S7]

## Feedback and iteration

Kao recommends trying ideas with real people and observing genuine interest: energetic follow-up, questions, remembered wording, and a desire to hear more. Polite approval is weaker evidence than active engagement. Different phrasings can reveal which parts resonate or confuse. [S1][S12]

Treat resonance and correctness separately. Excitement suggests an angle matters; it does not establish the factual claim. Disagreement can reveal a useful trade-off or a real flaw. This distinction is a package safeguard for applying the engagement method responsibly.

Record the tested audience, version, setting, reactions, and passages that triggered them. Preserve the parts that create relevant interest, clarify confusion, and reconsider assumptions when substantive criticism warrants it. If no test occurred, state that plainly in working notes. Propose testing without implying that publication or customer outreach has occurred.

## Explicit warnings to preserve

- Avoid universal agreement as the goal; a position should allow disagreement. [S1]
- Avoid contrarianism pursued only for attention. [S1]
- Avoid summaries or questions that never develop into a point of view. [S1][S3][S4]
- Avoid copying another person's position instead of developing an authentic one. [S1]
- Avoid generic first drafts and excessive defensive framing. [S1][S2]
- Avoid certainty that exceeds the evidence or suppresses dissent through authority. [S5]
- Avoid explaining the importance of a familiar principle without showing its application. [S9]
- Avoid causal gaps that assume the difficult parts will solve themselves. [S13]
- Avoid treating shorter text as automatically better. [S7]
- Avoid adding logistics when the audience still needs a reason to act. [S14]
- Avoid venting that undermines the behavior change you want. [S15]
- Avoid mistaking polite reactions for genuine resonance. [S12]
