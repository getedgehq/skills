# Method

Based on Yaël Meier's public method. Not affiliated with or endorsed by Yaël Meier.

## Evidence and attribution boundary

This is an operational synthesis of Meier's public participation approach. It draws on the sources in [sources.md](sources.md), not the text of the jointly edited book. The public publisher listing confirms shared editorship; do not attribute the whole book to Meier or advertise this package as its official framework. [S1]

Two concrete structures are supported by public sources: the Love-Job model, developed by Meier and Jo Dietrich, and the five-phase reverse mentoring program Meier describes. The decision brief, evidence classifications, recruitment suggestions, experiment rules, and review gates below are this package's application tools. They are not additional named Meier frameworks, validated scales, or replicas of proprietary ZEAM surveys and conversation cards. [S3] [S7] [S8]

## Principles

**Participation precedes interpretation.** A group should influence decisions made for it. Meier describes youth-facing teams without young members as an origin of ZEAM and repeatedly prioritizes dialogue with affected young people. Participation means influence over interpretation and options, not simply appearing in campaign photographs. [S2] [S3] [S6]

**Business relevance has distinct branches.** Becoming attractive to young employees and becoming relevant to young customers require different diagnoses. Recruitment, retention, product development, and brand positioning are separate decisions, even if findings inform more than one. [S4]

**Appreciation becomes credible through experience.** Work attractiveness involves financial conditions, treatment inside the organization, and the employer's standing outside it. Responsibility, useful feedback, development, and coaching should be visible in how work happens. [S2] [S7]

**Exchange should be reciprocal.** Younger and experienced people have different knowledge to contribute. Meier's mentoring example pairs young-market perspective with management experience; it does not reduce either person to age or digital ability. [S5]

**Communication is part of a continuing relationship.** Meier advocates sustained social presence and testing ideas through feedback. This supports ongoing learning and accountable channel choices, rather than assuming that a youthful tone resolves a weak offer. The offer-first sequence here is an operational application of her data-based consulting and experience-focused advice. [S4] [S6] [S7]

## 1. Scope and decision

Write: To improve [business outcome] for [specific affected group], [owner] must decide whether to change [lever], because [observed friction]. Keep the scope within what the user or decision owner can actually influence.

Choose a branch:

| Branch | Main diagnostic question | Suitable output |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Recruitment or retention | Why do relevant candidates or staff reject, struggle with, or leave this experience? | Role, hiring, development, or working-experience change |
| Product or brand | Which benefit, access barrier, reputation issue, or journey affects adoption? | Offer or journey change, with communication where needed |
| Intergenerational collaboration | Which decision or knowledge exchange is blocked, and by what? | Participation arrangement or reciprocal mentoring pilot |

The branches implement Meier's employer/customer distinction and participation advice. Do not force mentoring into a price problem or a social campaign into a hiring-process problem. [S4] [S5]

Define the segment by circumstances that could change the answer: location, career stage, occupation, need, access to money, product familiarity, and available channels. Age can help recruit the intended group; it cannot substitute for understanding their situation. Treat age-versus-life-stage comparisons as hypotheses to investigate. This segmentation rule is a package safeguard against overgeneralizing the public generational framing.

If the brief asks why all young people are lazy, convert the premise into observable events: missed shifts, declined offers, or an unmet target. Meier explicitly challenges the assumption that young people do not want to work. Look for evidence about conditions and mutual understanding before accepting the premise. [S6]

## 2. Evidence and participation

### Build an evidence ledger

Separate four categories:

- **Observed behavior:** Application completion, departures, purchase abandonment, or other supplied records, with period and denominator.
- **Participant account:** A person's description of an actual experience, with relevant context and limitations.
- **Hypothesis:** A proposed explanation, including managerial anecdotes and inferred needs.
- **Public-method guidance:** Meier-specific advice with a source citation; useful for choosing questions, not proof of a local finding.

For each strong theme record a counterexample or unresolved alternative. Do not convert a handful of interviews into percentages for the whole cohort. A self-selected staff panel can reveal problems while missing people who already left. These evidence rules are application tools, motivated by Meier's account of identifying a data gap before collecting employee evidence and developing measures. [S3]

### Recruit for contrast, not symbolic representation

For employers seek relevant new hires, longer-staying young staff, declined candidates, and leavers where accessible. For customers seek users, nonusers, and people who stopped or abandoned the journey. These are suggested perspectives, not a mandatory universal sample. Match recruitment breadth to the decision and uncertainty.

Ask about episodes: What happened the last time you considered this role or product? Where did you hesitate? What did you compare? What happened after you raised an idea? What made you stay, leave, try, or stop? Ask what would change the decision and then seek supporting behavior. Avoid asking one person to explain an entire generation.

State what participants can influence, who decides, and which constraints are fixed. Give them a chance to interpret themes and revise proposals. Meier's VBS account describes a survey followed by workshops across generations producing concrete measures; its value here is the progression from evidence to action, not a required sample size or guaranteed outcome. [S3]

### Handle incomplete or conflicting evidence

If access is unavailable, complete only a provisional diagnosis. Provide recruitment channels, selection criteria, discovery questions, and the specific decisions evidence would unlock. Never simulate participant evidence without an illustration label.

If participants disagree, separate contexts instead of averaging them into a persona. If managers and young staff disagree, examine both the policy and the experienced implementation. If leadership cannot alter anything, identify that constraint and recommend a bounded decision they can change, or discovery only. These are package decision rules for meaningful participation, derived from the participation principle. [S3] [S6]

## 3. Diagnose the offer

### Employer branch: three Love-Job dimensions

Meier and Dietrich's public model considers money, internal appreciation, and external appreciation. The recap also discusses working climate, flexibility, meaning, security, feedback, participation, and coaching. Use the dimensions diagnostically; the public sources do not provide a scoring formula, weighting scheme, or validated cutoff. [S7]

| Dimension | Investigate | Example of a concrete change |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Money | Pay clarity, affordability, comparison options, financial tradeoffs | Publish the actual range or resolve a compensation gap |
| Internal appreciation | Listening, treatment, manager support, feedback, growth, flexibility where feasible, and responsibility | Define a first project, support person, decision boundary, and feedback rhythm |
| External appreciation | How peers and family perceive the employer and how candidates experience its reputation | Show credible work and staff experience that answer specific concerns |

Do not assume purpose offsets poor conditions. [S2] gives vision prominence relative to salary, while [S7] explicitly includes money as a pillar. Preserve both as public perspectives; use local evidence to assess tradeoffs instead of claiming a universal ranking.

Early responsibility means contribution with support, not automatic promotion or unchecked authority. Meier explicitly distinguishes involving young people early from assuming that an 18-year-old should immediately lead a company. Define task, scope, mentor, feedback, and escalation path appropriate to capability and the job. [S5]

Flexibility may differ across roles. If remote work is infeasible, test other forms of agency such as predictable scheduling, task ownership, or development access. This is an operational adaptation, not a claim that Meier prescribes those substitutes.

### Customer branch: benefit, experience, and reputation

Start with the need served, actual utility, access and price friction, reputation, and the discovery-to-use journey. Include evidence from people who did not adopt. Meier describes analysis that produces product and brand recommendations alongside creative work; use that distinction to decide whether the change belongs in the offer, journey, message, or several of them. [S4]

The Love-Job model is an employer structure. Do not rename its dimensions as an official consumer model. A reputation issue may require changes to organizational behavior, evidence of existing practice, or clearer communication; interviews alone do not establish which.

## 4. Design participation and mutual mentoring

Give young contributors a real job in the work: review discovery themes, shape a role preview, prototype an experience, or challenge a proposed option. Specify what changes because of their input. Responsibility without resources and support is not a credible participation plan. This is an operational interpretation of Meier's emphasis on listening, trust, and delegated responsibility. [S5] [S6]

Use reciprocal mentoring when mutual understanding or knowledge exchange is a plausible lever. Both people state what they offer and what they want to learn. For example, one contributes experience of the target market while another contributes management judgment. Do not presume the younger participant must teach social media. [S5]

Meier's public program advice includes five conditions: [S8]

1. Match through personal fit and interests rather than relying on technical matching alone.
2. Give the process a clear sequence and defined content.
3. Support substantive conversations across hierarchy.
4. Include top leaders visibly in the program.
5. Make participation voluntary.

The described five phases, paraphrased, are initial acquaintance, personal connection, exchange of knowledge and culture, observation, and innovation. An application might use introductory prompts, shared goals, reciprocal learning sessions, a work observation, then a small joint experiment. Meier describes six months; a shorter pilot is an adaptation, not the published standard or an equivalent proven program. [S8]

Do not recreate proprietary conversation cards. Write original prompts tied to the user's decision: What do you find hard to understand about this workflow? What have I assumed about your role? What could we test together? Consider confidentiality and power differences when arranging sessions; voluntary participation must be feasible in practice. These prompts and safeguards are package additions.

## 5. Communication and channel decisions

Map where the selected segment discovers, evaluates, acts, and asks for help. Use recent local evidence before choosing channels. Meier emphasizes being present where young audiences pay attention and sustaining that presence; the package does not turn historical platform recommendations into a fixed current list. [S2] [S4]

Use real work, product demonstrations, credible people, and specific benefits. Young contributors should influence creation and review where relevant. Do not assume every young employee is a creator or wants to appear publicly. The public talk encourages using available young staff content expertise; participation and role suitability still matter. [S7]

Tie promises to a supported experience. For employers, connect copy to the diagnosed pay, appreciation, and responsibility. For products, connect claims to verified capabilities and actual customer friction. If the underlying change is not approved or implemented, present it as a proposal, not a public promise.

Specify a sustainable cadence, owner, feedback route, and resource requirement. Meier advocates sustained presence and measurable creative/media work, but her promotional performance claims are not forecasts for another organization. Do not infer a universal budget split, a requirement to abandon other media, or guaranteed efficiency from her examples. [S4] [S9]

## 6. Test, decide, and transfer ownership

This package's testing protocol applies Meier's emphasis on feedback and measurable work: [S6] [S9]

- Choose the smallest comparison that can inform the decision: a revised role preview versus the existing version, one improved onboarding cohort, or a product prototype tested with the relevant segment.
- State the baseline, period, population, metric definition, and data limits. If no baseline exists, collect it or identify why the test cannot support a comparison.
- Pick a decision-linked outcome: qualified applications, accepted offers, appropriate retention interval, trial, repeated use, or acquisition efficiency. Views may diagnose reach; they do not automatically establish adoption or retention.
- Add participant feedback about what was useful, confusing, implausible, or difficult.
- Agree a success threshold and revise or stop conditions with the owner before interpreting results. Thresholds are contextual, not Meier benchmarks.
- If reach improves while the business outcome does not, inspect the downstream offer and journey. If changed conditions outperform youthful copy, retain that learning.

With a small or uncontrolled pilot, report directional evidence and plausible alternatives. Do not declare causality or broad effectiveness from an encouraging anecdote. If participants contradict the initial cohort story, update the story, segment, or recommendation.

Deliver the brief using [the output template](../templates/output-template.md). Each change needs an owner, a next action, and a dated review. Keep source citations for method claims separate from local evidence identifiers. A brief without direct evidence can be useful as a research plan, but must not pass as a validated implementation recommendation.

## Explicit warnings and application safeguards

| Warning or boundary | Status and evidence | Required response |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Assuming the market is represented by one's children | Explicit warning in the episode summary [S2] | Recruit affected people beyond family anecdotes |
| Speaking about young people without involving them | Repeated explicit emphasis [S2] [S3] [S5] | Give participants influence over findings and choices |
| Assuming young people do not want to work | Explicit rejection in the interview [S6] | Investigate conditions and observed events |
| Delaying all responsibility until long tenure | Explicit criticism, with a qualification against premature top-level authority [S5] | Offer bounded early contribution with support |
| Matching mentoring mechanically, omitting structure or leaders, or forcing enrollment | Explicit program advice to do the reverse [S8] | Use personal fit, a sequence, senior participation, and voluntary enrollment |
| Promising a better experience through copy alone | Package safeguard based on offer and appreciation advice [S4] [S7] | Fix or disclose the underlying experience gap |
| Treating the package as proprietary instruments or a validated scoring system | Package attribution boundary [S1] [S7] [S8] | State the public synthesis and avoid invented scores |

## Edge cases

**Only a copy edit is authorized.** Review the message within scope, identify unsupported promises, and propose honest alternatives. Flag experience gaps as recommendations rather than expanding the task into an unsolicited organizational program.

**A rapid deadline prevents discovery.** Use existing evidence with dates and limitations, draft provisional options, and reserve claims requiring participant validation. Speed does not turn assumptions into findings.

**Young participants are also dependent employees or minors.** Design participation so declining is feasible and feedback is not tied to performance judgments. For minors, establish the organization's relevant consent and safeguarding process before recruitment. These are contextual implementation safeguards, not sourced Meier-specific rules.

**Age and role are confounded.** Compare people doing similar work or facing similar customer needs where possible. Shared needs across generations may make the best improvement organization-wide rather than age-exclusive; the public talk explicitly notes substantial overlap in needs. [S7]

**An unsuccessful offer has good social engagement.** Inspect conversion, fit, and the experience after the click. Do not scale solely from attention metrics. Treat media measurement as part of the decision, not proof that the offer is sound. [S9]

**Mentoring is proposed as a remedy for every conflict.** Diagnose workload, incentives, authority, and policy first. Use reciprocal exchange only where understanding or knowledge transfer can change the actual problem. The proposed fit test is an application rule; Meier's public mentoring discussion supports exchange, not universal applicability. [S5]
