# Worked Examples

Both scenarios are invented illustrations. Companies, observations, prices, thresholds, and pilot sizes are fictional. Citations identify the principles being applied; they do not substantiate the invented business results. Neither example reports a completed pilot or a promised outcome.

## Illustration 1: RelayBoard needs team adoption before enterprise selling

### Scenario and weak first draft

RelayBoard sells collaborative workflow software. In an invented monthly cohort of 200 eligible accounts, 120 created a workflow, 20 used one with a colleague, and 12 paid within 30 days. Interviews suggest that users hesitate to invite colleagues because the invitation screen immediately asks for a paid seat. There is no reliable estimate of incremental sales impact.

**Weak draft:**

> Free users are not converting, so hire two SDRs to call every signup. Hide prices for companies with recognizable logos. Limit collaboration on the free plan, raise the entry price, and push all interested users toward a company-wide annual contract. Measure success by MQL volume, enterprise bookings, and cash collected.

This is an invented draft, not a quotation from a source.

### Improved decision memo

**Decision:** Prioritize the individual-to-team transition. Test a clearer invitation experience and useful free collaboration before hiring sellers or changing prices. The observed drop from 120 workflow creators to 20 collaborating accounts is a stronger diagnostic lead than signup-to-paid conversion alone. It does not prove collaboration causes payment. [S1] [S2]

**Best-fit account hypothesis:** Operations teams with recurring cross-functional workflows. Compare those accounts with solo users on activation, conversion, retention, and expansion. Use account progression rather than logo prestige to decide where to focus. [S6]

**Activation hypothesis:** At least two colleagues complete a shared workflow during the first 14 days. This event and timing are local proposals to test against repeat use and retention. They are not borrowed Figma thresholds. [S5]

**Offer and package proposal:**

| Package | Customer job | Proposed offer | Graduation trigger |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Free | Prove a shared workflow works | Limited workflows with colleague participation | Need more recurring workflows |
| Team | Run routine collaborative work | $15 per active editor per month, collaboration and team reporting | Need organizational administration |
| Organization | Govern wider deployment | Team charges plus a proposed $300 monthly organization fee for governance | Verified deployment and governance needs |

An active editor is a person who creates or changes a workflow in the month; viewers are included. Seats are a plausible unit here because value spreads through participating editors. Validate whether that definition is understandable and whether it discourages useful participation. [S2] [S8]

**Illustrative bills:** A new paid team with four active editors pays 4 × $15 = $60 monthly. An expanding team with 12 pays $180. A mature organization with 40 active editors and governance pays 40 × $15 + $300 = $900. These are research inputs, not final prices. Increased spend should accompany wider use or governance value. [S8]

**Add-on decision:** Evaluate a specialized audit export separately only if interviews establish a distinct compliance buyer, late adoption, and sufficient demand. Include standard team reporting in Team. Avoid turning normal collaboration into a series of required extras. [S3]

**Sales play:** Offer rollout assistance to activated best-fit accounts showing governance needs. Help the champion describe organizational value and identify the operations budget holder or IT approver. Do not pitch every signup or require an organization contract before users have value. [S4] [S5]

**Learning plan:** Product lead owns a four-week invitation usability and adoption pilot, with another 30 days for payment observation. Track account-level collaboration and conversion among eligible new accounts. Use a concurrent unchanged-experience group where feasible. A proposed decision rule is a 10 percentage point improvement in collaboration with no more than a 3 percentage point decline in paid conversion; report uncertainty and extend observation if the cohort is too small. Those thresholds are fictional business choices. Pricing research runs separately so the pilot does not confound experience and price changes.

**Economics and open questions:** Finance must estimate support costs and margin before interpreting paid growth. A $60 monthly team with $120 acquisition cost and assumed 80% contribution margin would recover acquisition cost in 2.5 months only under constant spend and no churn. Actual expansion and churn require a cohort model. No seller hiring decision is justified until assistance costs and incremental outcomes are known. [S9]

### Rules that changed the draft

- Journey diagnosis changed the intervention from sales pressure to a plausible activation constraint. [S1]
- Team pricing protected the collaboration bridge instead of treating invitations as immediate monetization events. [S2]
- Account fit and buyer navigation replaced logo-based outreach and calls to every signup. [S4] [S5] [S6]
- The pilot tests an identified uncertainty; economic payback replaces cash and bookings as proof of success. [S9]

## Illustration 2: EventPipe needs a value-linked unit and credible budget controls

### Scenario and weak first draft

EventPipe provides event-processing infrastructure. Invented account history shows that three engineers operate both a 2-million-event monthly workload and a 30-million-event workload. A new prospect cannot forecast its first year's volume. An established retailer has holiday spikes and requires an annual budget. Finance has not allocated implementation costs, so true payback is unknown.

**Weak draft:**

> Copy a competitor's per-seat model and charge $100 per engineer monthly. Require all customers to prepay a $20,000 annual contract. Offer unlimited processing to make the page simple. Pay sellers on bookings and count annual cash received as immediate CAC recovery. Target a famous infrastructure company's retention rate.

This is an invented draft, not a quotation from a source.

### Improved decision memo

**Decision:** Research processed events as the pricing unit, preserving an easy entry offer. Compare it with processed bytes if event size drives cost more strongly. Seats fail the illustrated expansion test because engineer count stays constant while valuable processing grows. [S7] [S8]

**Unit hypothesis:** A successfully processed customer event, excluding retried duplicates and vendor errors. The exclusions are proposed billing definitions. Validate customer understanding, metering accuracy, relationship to business value, and cost sensitivity before rollout. [S8] [S9]

**Packages:** Entry provides pay-as-you-go processing for new workloads. Production adds reliability and operational features for established workloads. Organization adds governance for multiple teams. The proposed processing rate is $50 per million events, with a Production platform fee of $200 monthly. Higher packages should correspond to an actual need, not compulsory charges for every initial customer. [S3]

**Illustrative monthly bills:**

| Customer stage | Calculation | Proposed bill |
| --- | --- | --- |
| New Entry account | 2 million events × $50 per million | $100 |
| Expanding Production account | $200 + 10 million events × $50 per million | $700 |
| Mature Production account | $200 + 30 million events × $50 per million | $1,700 |

Keep ordinary usage units consistent across packages unless evidence justifies a different model. Explain that increased processing is valuable only if it corresponds to productive workloads. A bill driven by waste, duplication, or vendor errors would undermine this rationale.

**Enterprise option:** For the established retailer, evaluate a 120-million-event annual allowance at a proposed 10% processing discount: 120 × $50 × 90% = $5,400 annually, plus 12 × $200 = $2,400 for Production, totaling $7,800. Usage can vary across months. Unused allowance expires under this illustrative proposal; excess events cost $50 per million, with alerts and an upgrade conversation before forecast exhaustion. Test whether those terms suit the buyer. Offer Entry without a commitment to the prospect lacking usage history. [S7] [S9]

**Sales and incentives:** Sales helps established accounts forecast workloads and resolve procurement requirements. Compare net revenue and contribution after discounts and commissions against continued self-service. Do not reward a seller merely for converting existing $8,400 annualized self-service spend into a $7,800 commitment unless the resulting predictability or adoption benefit justifies the reduction. [S9]

**Learning plan:** RevOps owns eight independent win-loss interviews and a six-week unpublished pricing pilot with willing value-oriented reps. Research unit clarity, willingness to pay, forecast uncertainty, and allowance terms. Match historical deals cautiously and disclose selection bias. Reject the proposal if bills cannot be reconciled or if margin falls below the team's proposed floor. A sample too small to distinguish deal mix from pricing effects yields a further-learning decision. [S7]

**Economics:** With $3,000 acquisition cost, a stable $700 monthly account, and an assumed 70% contribution margin, the shortcut payback estimate is $3,000 ÷ ($700 × 70%) = about 6.1 months. Implementation costs, adoption ramp, and usage variation are missing, so this is provisional. Cash prepaid under the commitment does not erase ongoing service costs. Evaluate retention by segment alongside actual payback rather than importing a historical public-company figure. [S9]

### Rules that changed the draft

- Value-metric selection linked spending to useful processing rather than an unchanged engineer count. [S8]
- Entry flexibility and annual allowances addressed different levels of customer certainty. [S7]
- Explicit units, overages, and metering made the offer testable and understandable. [S9]
- Consumption and margin checks exposed the risks of discounted booking incentives and cash-based payback. [S9]
