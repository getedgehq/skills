# Worked Examples

Both scenarios are invented illustrations. All people, terms, amounts and outcomes are fictional. They demonstrate the package's practical adaptations rather than reproduce course cases or simulator results. Source citations identify the underlying principles, not evidence for the fictional facts.

## Illustration 1: Packaging supplier and a price increase

### Scenario and evidence

A buyer pays EUR 1.00 per unit and receives a request for EUR 1.08. They expect to negotiate with the supplier every quarter. Last quarter, the buyer repeatedly changed orders at short notice, and the supplier stopped reserving production slots. The buyer's operations lead has authorized a price ceiling of EUR 1.06 only if deliveries meet the agreed schedule and committed volume stays within approved demand. This boundary is an invented scenario input, not an inferred industry benchmark.

### Weak first draft

> Reject EUR 1.08. Open at EUR 0.95 and tell the supplier that competitors are cheaper. If they resist, offer EUR 1.04 to keep the relationship friendly. They obviously want volume, so promise bigger orders now and work out deliveries later.

This draft invents competitor evidence, treats a counterpart priority as fact, promises unauthorised volume and negotiates price separately from delivery. It also ignores how past ordering behavior changed capacity access. A friendly tone does not make these commitments feasible. [S2][S4][S5]

### Applying the method

The agent records the order changes and lost slots as relationship-state facts. It asks which costs drive the increase and which form of predictability would change the supplier's cost or capacity decision. In the illustration, the supplier answers that a rolling forecast with four weeks of firm orders reduces rescheduling; the operations lead confirms that this window is feasible within approved volumes. This changes the volume hypothesis into a reported preference, while the claimed cost saving remains unverified. [S2][S4][S6]

The agent then links price, the firm-order window and capacity rather than accepting them independently. It checks a demand downturn before recommending the commitment. [S3][S5]

### Improved result: negotiation brief

**Decision and diagnosis:** Negotiate a quarterly supplier package by Friday. This is a repeated, multi-issue negotiation, with price and delivery reliability linked to production predictability.

**Relationship state:** Frequent late changes preceded the supplier's withdrawal of reserved slots. A feasible forecast commitment could restore access, but an excessive volume promise could constrain later demand responses.

**Positions:** Limit: EUR 1.06 with the approved schedule and no unapproved volume obligation. Target: EUR 1.04 with reserved slots. Opening: EUR 1.03 conditional on a four-week firm-order window and the agreed delivery schedule. The fictional opening is supported by the proposed operational exchange, not by fabricated competitor prices.

**Critical unknowns:** What delivery slots can the supplier actually reserve? Does the quoted price include the same specifications? Can both sides confirm that the four-week commitment meets the supplier's scheduling need? Do not finalize until the specifications and capacity are confirmed.

| Issue | Buyer priority | Supplier priority | Evidence and exchange |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Price | High | Higher revenue sought | EUR 1.08 request is known; reduced rescheduling cost is not yet verified |
| Firm-order window | Preserve flexibility outside four weeks | Reduce rescheduling | Supplier stated preference; buyer confirmed four weeks is feasible |
| Delivery slots | High | Manage capacity predictably | Specific reserved schedule still requires confirmation |

**Proposed package:** Offer EUR 1.03 with four weeks of firm orders within approved volumes, an eight-week nonbinding forecast and reserved slots for the agreed schedule. A move toward EUR 1.04 requires confirmation of the schedule and unchanged specifications. Do not accept the price while leaving the reciprocal delivery term open. Never exceed EUR 1.06 or extend firm volume without the required authority.

**Uncertainty and architecture:** If demand falls, the buyer honors the existing four-week commitment and adjusts later forecast periods. Confirm how forecast updates, reserved slots and missed deliveries are recorded and reviewed before agreement. Assess the cost of the committed window under a plausible downturn; if it exceeds approved demand, reduce the commitment and reopen the package.

**Tension response:** “I understand that late changes have made scheduling difficult. Which confirmed order window would let us agree a price and reserve reliable delivery slots together?” This is invented dialogue, not a source quotation.

**Round record, fictional outcome:** The parties agree EUR 1.04, four weeks of firm orders and a written slot schedule, with no larger volume commitment. The buyer separately decides to appoint one person to send forecasts. Record that unilateral decision separately from the supplier's commitments.

**Next-round update:** Check whether forecast discipline improved capacity access. Ask an observer whether the buyer tested the supplier's priorities before arguing about price. The proposed behavior change is to ask about a constraint before responding to the next price request.

### Rules that made the difference

- Recording history revealed a negotiable cause of lost options instead of treating the price increase as isolated. [S2]
- Inquiry replaced a convenient assumption with a reported scheduling priority. [S4]
- Different priorities supported a conditional exchange; they did not guarantee the exact fictional price. [S6]
- Feasibility checks removed the unauthorized promise and linked all reciprocal terms. [S5]
- Separate process feedback and next-round updates prevented a price result from being the entire debrief. [S7]

## Illustration 2: Internal launch deadline and an escalating conflict

### Scenario and evidence

Marketing requests three product integrations for a launch in six weeks. Engineering estimates ten weeks for all three and has one team available. The two functions must work together on future launches. Marketing has begun copying senior leaders into accusatory emails; engineering has responded by withholding provisional dates. The user asks for an internal call plan and a feasible agreement proposal.

### Weak first draft

> Tell engineering the launch cannot move and insist on all three integrations. If they refuse, ask leadership to force overtime. Offer an extra week only if they guarantee everything. Their resistance is probably just reluctance to prioritize marketing.

The draft assumes a motive, ignores capacity, offers a deadline concession without a workable scope trade and escalates pressure. It treats the conflict as a contest to win rather than a structure to diagnose. [S4][S5]

### Applying the method

The agent asks what makes the launch date valuable, which integration supports that outcome, and which engineering estimates are fixed or uncertain. In this invented scenario, marketing says that integration A supports the launch demonstration; B and C can follow later. Engineering confirms A can be ready in six weeks if marketing freezes its requirements by Tuesday. B and C depend on a vendor specification that is not yet available. The different priorities create room for a scope-timing trade. [S4][S6]

The agent also recognizes that copied accusations and withheld information have narrowed cooperation. It prepares a courteous reset and an agreement that makes dependencies explicit. [S2][S5]

### Improved result: call plan and negotiation brief

**Decision and diagnosis:** Agree launch scope and a credible delivery plan at tomorrow's call. This is an internal negotiation about scope, timing and capacity, embedded in an ongoing relationship.

**Relationship state:** Accusatory emails were followed by less date-sharing. Record the sequence without claiming that engineering's motives are proven. The next agreement should restore timely visibility and reduce avoidable requirement changes.

**Boundary and positions:** Limit: no promise of all three integrations in six weeks without a feasible delivery plan and authorized resources. Target: launch with A in six weeks, with a credible plan for B and C. Opening: A for the launch in exchange for a Tuesday requirements freeze, with a separate review for B and C after the vendor specification arrives. Do not concede the freeze while retaining the same engineering date.

**Critical questions:** Is A sufficient for the launch demonstration? Can the requirements owner actually freeze scope? What acceptance test defines A as ready? Which vendor information changes the B and C estimates? These questions have concrete consequences: if A is insufficient, reopen scope or the date; if the freeze is infeasible, engineering must revise its plan.

| Issue | Marketing interest | Engineering interest | Possible exchange |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Launch timing | Preserve announced demonstration | Avoid infeasible commitment | Launch with A rather than all three |
| Scope stability | Keep essential capability | Reduce rework | Freeze A's requirements by Tuesday |
| B and C schedule | Credible follow-up communication | Resolve vendor dependency | Estimate after specification review rather than invent dates |
| Information cadence | Know risks early | Avoid adversarial reporting | Joint weekly status review with explicit decisions |

**Proposed package:** A is planned for six weeks, conditional on the Tuesday freeze and an agreed acceptance test. The product owner confirms requirements; engineering confirms feasible delivery and reports material changes at a weekly joint review. B and C receive estimates within two working days of a complete vendor specification, followed by a scope-and-date decision. Do not describe these estimates as launch commitments.

**Adverse scenario:** If A's scope changes after Tuesday, the parties explicitly revisit scope or timing. If the vendor is late, keep B and C dates unresolved and communicate the dependency. Assign named owners during the call; do not invent their authority.

**Call sequence:** Acknowledge the launch and capacity concerns; confirm the demonstrated outcome; ask the critical questions; propose the linked package; confirm owners, acceptance criteria and the next review. If no feasible package emerges, record non-agreement and the decision that must go to the actual authorized owner.

**Opening words:** “The launch date matters, and the current plan asks engineering to commit beyond its estimate. Can we agree which capability makes the demonstration useful, then connect its delivery date to a scope freeze?” This is invented dialogue.

**Round record, fictional outcome:** The parties agree A for six weeks, contingent on the Tuesday freeze and acceptance test, plus weekly risk reviews. Marketing separately decides to remove B and C from launch promotion. B and C delivery dates remain open pending the vendor specification. Record the dependency and the next review rather than claiming the whole conflict is solved.

**Feedback and next-round adjustment:** The user reports that the call improved after scope priorities were clarified. Treat this as user feedback, not independent proof. For the next launch, ask about priority and feasibility before broadcasting a commitment to all stakeholders.

### Rules that made the difference

- A useful difference in scope priorities replaced an unsupported judgment about motives. [S4][S6]
- The package joined scope stability and delivery timing instead of splitting them into isolated concessions. [S5]
- Promises were limited to feasible commitments and explicit dependencies. [S5]
- Process repair addressed information sharing and future cooperation, not only this launch. [S2][S5]
- The record preserved agreed terms, a unilateral decision and unresolved dates as separate items. [S7]
