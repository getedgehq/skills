# The Mom Test for Customer Conversations

Based on Rob Fitzpatrick's public method. Not affiliated with or endorsed by Rob Fitzpatrick. Sources: [references/sources.md](references/sources.md).

## Output contract

Read this first. It decides what the user receives.

- **The deliverable is the interview guide, ready to use on the call:** the questions in the order the founder will ask them, each with one or two short follow-ups. If they asked to review questions or notes, return the rewritten questions or the conclusions from the notes, plus a few lines on what changed.
- **Length and format:** one or two pages the founder can glance at mid-call. Roughly 10 to 15 core questions grouped under short headings (for example: their current workflow, the last time it went wrong, what they have tried, money, the prototype or offer, next steps), with minutes per section that add up to the call length if one is given. One line before the questions states what the calls must find out. A short after-call sheet (the five or six facts to write down per interview) is welcome. If you save a file, the chat reply is two or three lines.
- **Cover every assumption in their notes.** Each belief, feature choice, price point or decision the founder mentions gets questions that could confirm or break it. A price they are considering is tested the Mom Test way: first what they spend today and on what, who pays, what they have bought before; only after that, introduce the actual price neutrally and ask what would make it worth it, then ask for a real commitment (pre-order, deposit, pilot) rather than an opinion.
- **What the method changes:** the questions, not the length. Every discovery question is about the person's life and a specific past occasion ("Walk me through the last time you..."), not about the idea or the future ("Would you use...", "How much would you pay..."). Discovery comes before any prototype or price. Follow-ups dig into what happened, what they tried, what it cost and why. The product is not pitched during the learning part. If a sales step fits, the guide ends with one concrete commitment ask (time, reputation or money).
- **Facts:** use only facts from the request and the files provided. Never invent interview results, quotes, personas, numbers or the founder's hypotheses. If the idea is not described, write questions that work for the stated audience and job.
- **Never output:** a discovery brief, learning-objective tables, participant plans, evidence records, "risk" annotations on each question, commitment ledgers, review results, source tags like [S2], an explanation of the Mom Test rules, or the expert's name. A short "avoid asking" line with two or three examples is fine if it helps the founder.
- **Open questions:** none unless the answer would change which questions to ask; then at most two.

## The method, as decisions

Each rule is sourced in [references/method.md](references/method.md), which also covers detail and edge cases.

1. **Talk about their life, not your idea.** Questions about the idea invite compliments, even from honest people.
2. **Specifics in the past, not generics or the future.** "What happened the last time..." beats "Do you usually..." and "Would you...". If they have never done the task, that is the answer; ask about the nearest real experience.
3. **Listen more than talk.** Short follow-ups: what happened next, what did you try, why that, what did it cost, how did it end. Do not answer their story with your pitch.
4. **Know what you need to learn.** Before writing questions, pick the few uncertainties that would change the founder's plan, including the scary one; the questions should be able to disprove it.
5. **Money and effort reveal priority.** Ask what they currently spend (time, money, tools, people) on the problem and what they have already tried; a problem they have never tried to solve is rarely urgent.
6. **Deflect compliments and dig under feature requests.** Return to a concrete event; ask what the requested feature would let them do and how they cope today.
7. **Learning and selling are different conversations.** If there is an offer, end with a real commitment ask (next meeting with the decision maker, a pilot, a pre-order). Enthusiasm without commitment is not a signal.

## Before you hand it over (silent)

Check without writing it down: no question asks for an opinion about the idea, a prediction, or a hypothetical price; each core question points at a real past occasion or current behavior; nothing invented about the founder or the interviewees; the founder could use the page as is.
