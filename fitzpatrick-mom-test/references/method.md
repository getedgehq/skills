# Method: customer discovery and real progress

Based on Rob Fitzpatrick's public method. Not affiliated with or endorsed by Rob Fitzpatrick.

## Scope and attribution

The method improves what can be learned from customer conversations and distinguishes encouraging feedback from meaningful purchasing progress. The official site frames it as a practical way to improve learning and sales. This reference paraphrases public interviews and essays; it does not reproduce book passages. [S1]

Sections on question design, customer behavior, commitments, and workflow fit describe Fitzpatrick's public advice. Evidence labels, report fields, commitment statuses, and review gates are this package's operational adaptations. They are organizational tools, not a validated scoring model or an expert-authored universal sequence.

## Learning and selling

Discovery has two different outcomes. Learning changes the team's understanding of the customer, constraints, and choices. Selling produces an actual next step toward buying, or a reason to stop pursuing that opportunity. A friendly meeting can accomplish neither. Identify which outcome is intended before deciding whether a conversation succeeded. [S2]

During learning, avoid centering the idea and asking the customer to judge it. During selling, an explicit offer and appropriate ask become necessary. Do not interpret the advice to avoid pitching during discovery as a permanent prohibition on selling. Make the transition visible in the guide and evidence review. [S2] [S4]

**Decision rule:** If the task concerns a behavior or constraint, investigate an episode. If it concerns willingness to adopt a specific offer, obtain appropriate action on that offer. Understanding the problem and establishing demand for a solution remain different claims. [S4] [S6]

## Objectives and participants

Choose three important learning objectives before talking. These are uncertainties, not three compulsory questions. A prepared objective can be resolved through a short natural exchange rather than an hour-long appointment. Examples include why a competitor was rejected, how a recent task was completed, and how an organization previously approved a purchase. [S4]

Seek the answer that could undermine the plan. The useful question is often the uncomfortable one because discovering a fatal constraint early avoids commitment to an unsuitable feature set or launch. Do not construct an interview that only collects supporting reasons. [S5]

Talk to people who actually experience the situation. Where the team can choose among audiences, existing understanding and access reduce the cost of learning basic industry context. Friendly contacts make practice easier; expand beyond them as evidence develops. Their friendliness does not make their opinions purchase evidence. [S4] [S6]

**Package rule:** State the participant's role, relevant experience, relationship to the interviewer, and recruiting route. Differentiate end user, internal advocate, buyer, and approver when those roles differ. Do not infer a market-wide pattern from a small convenience sample.

## Question design

Apply three principles together: discuss the person's circumstances, investigate specific past occurrences, and give the person room to speak. Questions about the idea, imagined future behavior, and broad generalizations invite misleading answers even when the respondent is trying to help. [S3]

| Weak approach | Better direction | Why it helps |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ask whether the idea sounds useful | Ask how the relevant task was last handled | Removes pressure to encourage the founder |
| Ask whether they would pay a proposed price | Explore the last comparable purchase and, later, make an honest offer | Separates purchase history from actual buying action |
| Ask what they usually do | Reconstruct the latest specific occasion | Reveals behavior rather than an idealized self-description |
| Ask which features they want | Investigate the triggering event and desired result | Avoids mistaking a proposed implementation for the need |

The principles behind these rewrites come from the public interviews; the table is an agent adaptation. [S3] [S5]

Use brief follow-ups after listening: what happened next, what was tried, why that option, and what the result was. Do not immediately answer a customer's story with your own story or a product explanation. Doing so takes attention away from their circumstances. [S3]

**Package rule:** Do not force a nonexistent past episode. If the person has never done the task, record that and investigate the nearest relevant experience, if any. A revised question is a plan for learning, not a fabricated answer.

## Behavior and workflows

Build an understanding of what the person already does and why. Record the trigger, sequence, tools, dependencies, outcome, and rationale. Where practical, observing the work exposes details that a high-level description misses. When observation is impractical, ask for a concrete account. [S4]

Existing investments matter: time, purchases, manual effort, searches, and abandoned alternatives show how the person has responded to the situation. No current solution can mean indifference, unsuccessful searching, or unsuitable options. Investigate which explanation fits before concluding that there is no demand. [S3]

Spreadsheets, phone calls, and paper are not automatic evidence of a software opportunity. They may be flexible, familiar, reliable, or compatible with surrounding work. Understand their role before proposing replacement. [S4]

Fitzpatrick warns that changing established workflows and beliefs can be a severe adoption obstacle. People can care intensely about a problem and reject a solution that requires an unacceptable change. Adding every possible workflow increases development and support costs; trying to educate customers into the founder's preferred behavior can also be slow. [S7]

Prefer understanding the user's world and drawing a useful product boundary. Determine what the product takes responsibility for and how its outputs cooperate with existing tools. Narrowing scope can improve fit. Treat this as a design hypothesis requiring evidence, not a universal rule that every product must remain tiny. [S7]

## Contamination and feature requests

Compliments, imagined adoption, and noncommittal interest are weak evidence. Fitzpatrick describes how founders can mistake polite reactions and postponements for buying commitments, leading to expensive development based on reassurance. The danger lies in what the interviewer concludes, not in proving that the customer is dishonest. [S5]

When praise appears, return to a real event already mentioned. If the interviewer has pitched, retain concrete facts but mark later reactions as influenced by the pitch. Alternatively, explicitly shift into sales and ask for a meaningful next step. [S2] [S5]

Customers can describe their frustrations while proposing an implementation that misses the underlying goal. Investigate why a requested feature matters, what result it should enable, and how the person gets that result today. Even paying customers' feature requests need this investigation. Payment is not a guarantee that each requested feature is a good design decision. [S5]

**Package rule:** Review supplied transcripts without silently rewriting history. Show the original question, explain the weakness, propose a replacement for a future conversation, and mark the missing evidence unresolved. Do not turn a hypothetical response into a factual finding by paraphrasing it.

## Evidence and next experiments

An explicit problem allows substantial learning through conversation before development. Where value depends on a new experience, entertainment, or a noticeably different interaction, an early prototype may be necessary. Asking whether someone likes fun, or whether an imagined interface sounds better, cannot establish the actual experience. [S4] [S6]

Conversations after prototype use can explore what the person actually did and why. The purpose is to resolve particular uncertainties rather than seek perfect validation. Fitzpatrick explicitly warns that certainty is not the outcome; different evidence reduces different risks. [S6]

Use these package labels to keep claims legible:

- **Observed behavior:** An action directly witnessed or supported by an available artifact.
- **Reported past behavior:** A concrete account given by the participant; valuable but not independently verified.
- **Opinion or prediction:** A preference, compliment, generalization, or imagined future action.
- **Commitment:** An agreed or completed sacrifice tied to an offer or experiment.
- **Interpretation:** The team's inference, kept separate from the underlying account.
- **Unknown:** An unanswered question or missing detail that matters to the decision.

These labels implement the evidence distinctions in the interviews; they are not a numeric evidence hierarchy. A reported past purchase and a completed prototype session answer different questions. Do not assign fake confidence percentages. [S3] [S4] [S6]

## Commitment and advancement

When there is something to offer, ask for a resource the prospect values. Time, reputation, and money are the main categories discussed publicly. Examples include substantive testing, an introduction to a relevant decision maker, or payment for honestly described access. The ask should make seriousness visible rather than manipulate the person. [S4]

Time can be meaningful before money is appropriate. A substantial beta-reading or testing effort offers stronger evidence than praise, yet remains narrower than a purchase. In organizational selling, introductions to management, technical stakeholders, or legal contacts can advance an opportunity before revenue. [S2] [S6]

**Package rule:** Record four statuses: requested, agreed, completed, or declined. A scheduled test is an agreement to give time; an attended test demonstrates that time was given. A promised introduction is not a completed introduction. For each, specify the remaining purchase steps.

A declined ask requires context. It can reflect insufficient interest, an unsuitable offer, a lack of authority, or a genuine constraint. Ask a short follow-up and preserve the explanation rather than treating every refusal as proof that the problem is absent. [S4]

## Edge cases and limits

- **Remote conversations:** In his AMA, Fitzpatrick describes his own difficulty learning through email while acknowledging others' successful remote approaches. Do not convert that personal preference into a rule banning calls or remote interviews. Adapt the medium while preserving concrete episodes and follow-ups. [S2]
- **Industry experts:** Experts can clarify industry facts and access paths, especially when customers are hard to reach. Their explanations are context, not evidence of an individual customer's demand. [S2]
- **No current spending:** Investigate effort, failed searches, and alternatives. Absence of a payment does not resolve importance by itself. [S3]
- **Strong pain, weak adoption:** Examine workflow and belief changes rather than assuming stronger marketing or more features will solve the mismatch. [S7]
- **Experience-led products:** Move to an observable prototype task instead of extending abstract interviews indefinitely. [S4] [S6]
- **Contradictory interviews:** As a package practice, keep the contradiction visible and check roles, circumstances, and episodes before merging people into one segment.

## Reflection and decisions

Record what changed in the team's understanding, the evidence responsible, and the resulting action. Include contrary facts and unresolved assumptions. A neat summary without changed understanding or a decision-relevant next step is not enough.

This reporting practice adapts Fitzpatrick's essay about active reflection while learning. That essay discusses reading and note-taking, not a prescribed discovery report. Its relevant contribution is engaging with information until it changes thinking or behavior; the template here applies that idea to customer evidence. [S8]
