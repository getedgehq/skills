# Worked examples

Both scenarios below are invented illustrations. Their dialogue, numbers, participants, and outcomes are fabricated to demonstrate the method. They are not research evidence or accounts of Fitzpatrick's customers.

## Illustration 1: agency reporting product

### Weak first draft

A founder proposes a campaign dashboard for small agencies. The interview guide asks:

- Would a dashboard save you time?
- Which charts would you want?
- Would you pay EUR 49 per month?

The invented agency owner responds enthusiastically and suggests several charts. The founder's draft conclusion is that demand and pricing are validated and the chart builder should be developed next.

### Apply the method

Set three objectives: reconstruct the latest client report, understand the recipient's needs, and learn the last relevant software purchase process. Replace the opening with a request to walk through the most recent report. Probe the sequence, what took time, why that format was chosen, and what happened after delivery. This moves attention from the proposed product to past behavior. [S3] [S4]

In the invented follow-up, the owner describes spending 90 minutes exporting campaign data, copying it into a branded document, and emailing clients. Clients receive an attachment because they rarely open tool accounts. The owner wants charts to make that attachment readable, rather than to give clients a new dashboard.

Investigating the goal behind the chart request reveals a different design opportunity. Preserving delivery through the customer's existing tools also avoids imposing unnecessary workflow changes. [S5] [S7]

### Improved result

**Decision:** Test document generation before building a client dashboard.

**Evidence:** Reported past behavior: the latest report required exports, manual document preparation, and email delivery. The 90-minute figure is the owner's account, not a measured result. The owner reports that client login adoption has been poor.

**Weak signals excluded:** Enthusiasm and imagined willingness to pay EUR 49 do not establish adoption or price acceptance. The feature request identifies a desired result to investigate, not a roadmap instruction.

**Next experiment:** Try a narrow prototype using a real report, measuring whether it creates an acceptable client attachment with less preparation work. Treat the prototype as a solution hypothesis rather than a proven consequence of the problem.

**Commitment:** The owner agrees to a 30-minute session next Tuesday using an existing export. Status: agreed. Attendance, actual testing effort, payment, and purchasing authority remain unresolved. Time is a stage-appropriate signal, with a narrower meaning than a purchase. [S4] [S6]

**Rules that made the difference:** Specific episodes replaced forecasts; the feature's purpose was investigated; the product boundary respected existing delivery; and the agreed test was kept separate from completed action. [S3] [S5] [S7]

## Illustration 2: enterprise interview review

### Weak first draft

A team supplies notes from three meetings with an operations manager after a pitch for compliance software. The manager praises the idea and predicts that every department would use it. The team writes: three positive calls prove enterprise demand; request another informal catch-up and begin a full deployment build.

The notes contain no recent compliance incident, no account of an existing purchase, no agreed test, and no introduction to procurement. The manager's role in approving spending is unclear.

### Apply the method

Audit the existing notes instead of improving their wording into apparent facts. Label praise and forecasts as reactions after a pitch. Mark incident frequency, existing workarounds, approval requirements, and buying authority as unknown. Learning about a customer and advancing an offer are different outcomes; repeated friendly meetings alone establish neither. [S2] [S5]

Prepare a learning follow-up about the most recent compliance review: what triggered it, who gathered evidence, which tools were involved, and how the work ended. Because an offer already exists, plan a separate sales transition asking which people must approve a limited pilot and whether an introduction is possible. [S4]

In the invented continuation, the manager describes one annual review handled successfully with an existing vendor. The manager does not control the budget and declines an introduction because there is no current purchasing project. No new purchase action occurs.

### Improved result

**Decision:** Stop treating this account as an advancing sale. Reconsider the proposed segment and timing before investing in a full deployment.

**Evidence:** Reported past behavior: the latest review used an existing vendor and was completed successfully. The manager reports no current purchase project and no budget authority. The introduction ask was declined.

**Interpretation:** This account presently offers weak evidence of urgency or buying progress. That does not establish that compliance is unimportant or that every organization would reject the solution. The proposed offer could be wrong, the timing could be wrong, or another participant could face different constraints.

**Learning gap:** Investigate customers with a recent vendor failure or new review requirement. Determine what they actually changed, what resources they committed, and how the last purchase was approved.

**Commitment record:** Introduction requested and declined; no completed advancement; payment not requested because the purchase path is unresolved.

**Rules that made the difference:** Pitch contamination stayed visible; missing facts stayed unknown; an actual offer received a concrete ask; and the refusal was interpreted in context rather than converted into a universal market verdict. [S2] [S4] [S5]
