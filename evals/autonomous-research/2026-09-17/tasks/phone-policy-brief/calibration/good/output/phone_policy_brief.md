# Annex B: The evidence base for phone-free secondary schools

Prepared for Cabinet, Wentmoor County Council. Evidence current to September 2026.

## Bottom line

The evidence that phone-free schools raise attainment is real but modest, and narrower than it is usually reported. The strongest causal study in English schools finds a gain equivalent to 6.41% of a standard deviation in high-stakes exam results, concentrated almost entirely in the lowest-achieving pupils. The best comparable studies from Sweden and Norway find no average effect at all. The evidence that restrictive policies improve mental wellbeing is weaker: the largest recent English study is cross-sectional and finds no difference in wellbeing between pupils in restrictive and permissive schools. Nothing in the literature argues against the policy, and the equity case for it is reasonable. What the literature does say is that a written policy and a phone-free school are not the same thing, and that the size of any benefit is set by enforcement. Members should expect a small and unevenly distributed gain, not a step change.

## Scope of this annex

This annex summarises what is known about the effects of restricting pupil mobile phone use in secondary schools, covering attainment, wellbeing and mental health, the quality of the underlying research, and what the literature says about putting a policy into practice. It draws on nineteen sources: the causal evaluations of school-level bans that exist, the recent reviews of that literature, the experimental work on mechanism, and the small body of work on enforcement and on parental concerns. Where a claim rests on a single study, the annex says so. Where the evidence does not exist, the annex says that instead of inferring.

## Attainment

The reference point is Beland and Murphy (2016), a difference-in-differences study of 91 secondary schools across four English cities that introduced phone bans at different dates between 2001 and 2011. Test scores in the affected cohorts rose by 6.41% of a standard deviation, roughly equivalent to adding five days to the school year. Two features of that result matter more than the headline. First, the unit is a standard deviation of the exam score distribution and not a percentage of the marks awarded: a 6.41 per cent rise in exam results would be several times larger than anything in this literature. Second, the gain is not spread evenly. It is driven by pupils in the lowest quintile of prior attainment, with no detectable effect on the highest achievers, which is what makes the intervention look equity-improving rather than uniformly beneficial.

The replication record outside England is weaker than the reading list suggests. Kessel, Hardardottir and Tyrefors (2020) studied Swedish upper secondary schools and found no significant effect of a ban on grades or on the probability of taking a national test, with the point estimates close to zero. The reading list carries this work twice, once as the published Economics of Education Review article and once as the SSRN working paper that preceded it; it is one study, not two, and it does not replicate the English finding. Abrahamsson (2024), using Norwegian administrative registers and an event-study design around municipal ban dates, likewise reports no average effect on grades. What it does find is a distributional pattern: improvements in GPA and in externally set test scores for girls, larger for girls from lower socio-economic backgrounds, and effects close to zero for boys.

It is worth being concrete about what an effect of this size buys. A gain of 6.41% of a standard deviation in a cohort of roughly 3,900 Wentmoor pupils sitting GCSEs each year would move a small number of borderline candidates across a grade boundary, most of them in the lowest-attaining quintile, and would leave most pupils' results unchanged. That is a defensible reason to act, particularly on equity grounds, but it is not the transformation that the public debate assumes, and members should not be asked to approve the policy on the basis of an effect it cannot deliver.

The broader correlational literature is consistent with a small negative association between phone use and achievement, which is the mechanism a ban is supposed to interrupt. Kates, Wu and Coryn (2018) meta-analyse 39 samples and report a small negative correlation between mobile phone use and academic performance, as do Amez and Baert (2020) in a narrative review and Sunday, Adesope and Maarhuis (2021) for problematic use specifically. Böttger and Zierer (2024), reviewing the ban literature directly, conclude that the evidence for academic benefit is thin and that the studies pointing to it are few.

## Wellbeing and mental health

The wellbeing case is the weakest part of the evidence base, and it is the part most often overstated in public debate.

Goodyear et al. (2025), the SMART Schools study, surveyed 1,227 pupils aged 12 to 15 across 30 English secondary schools. It is the largest recent English study and it is worth reading carefully, because it does not show what it is frequently said to show. It is cross-sectional, so it compares schools rather than tracking what happens when a school changes its rules, and it reports no evidence of a difference in mental wellbeing between restrictive and permissive schools (adjusted mean difference on the Warwick-Edinburgh scale of -0.48, 95% CI -2.05 to 1.06). Nor did it find a difference in anxiety, depression, sleep or classroom behaviour. What it did find is that school policy made little difference to how much pupils used their phones overall: restrictive schools reduced in-school use without reducing total daily use, which is the finding with the clearest implications for us.

That result sits inside a wider literature in which the association between adolescent wellbeing and digital technology use is real but very small, and comparable in size to associations with eating potatoes or wearing glasses (Orben and Przybylski, 2019). Odgers and Jensen (2020) review the same ground and conclude that the evidence for a causal link between smartphone use and adolescent mental illness is weak, and that policy has run ahead of it. Montag and Elhai (2023) set out the arguments on both sides and reach the same conclusion: a ban may be defensible on attention and social grounds without being defensible as a mental health intervention.

There is a further point in the SMART Schools data that bears directly on how the policy should be described to parents. If restrictive schools do not reduce a pupil's total daily phone or social media use, then the mechanisms through which phone use is thought to affect adolescent mental health, chiefly displaced sleep and displaced offline time, are largely untouched by a school-day policy. A ban is a plausible instrument for classroom attention. It is not an instrument for adolescent mental health, and presenting it as one would set an expectation that the policy cannot meet and that members would be held to.

## How strong is the underlying evidence

Members should be aware of three limits.

The causal evidence base is four studies, of which one finds an effect. Beland and Murphy (2016), Kessel, Hardardottir and Tyrefors (2020) and Abrahamsson (2024) are quasi-experimental; the SMART Schools study is observational. Every other paper in this annex either measures association or tests a mechanism under laboratory conditions.

The mechanistic evidence is genuine but does not scale up on its own. Ward et al. (2017) show in controlled experiments that the mere presence of a switched-off phone reduces available working memory, and Kuznekoff and Titsworth (2013) show that texting during a lecture reduces note-taking and recall. These are findings about individuals in a controlled setting, not about what happens when a local authority changes a policy, and Selwyn and Aagaard (2020) argue that the leap from one to the other is exactly where the debate goes wrong.

The effects that are found are small. On the most favourable reading of the most favourable study, a ban buys a fraction of a standard deviation for the pupils furthest behind. That is worth having, and it is not a substitute for anything.

## Implementation, enforcement and parents

The heads are right that a written policy and a phone-free school are different things, and this is where the evidence has moved most recently.

Allcott et al. (2026) report the first large-scale evidence on lockable pouches, evaluating school-level adoption across a national sample. Grigic Magnusson et al. (2023) and Grigic Magnusson (2019) document how Swedish bans actually operate in classrooms and from the pupil's point of view: enforcement falls on individual teachers, is applied unevenly across a staff body, and generates a steady stream of low-level confrontations that consume more teaching time than the phones did. Gao et al. (2014) found the same pattern of drift between stated and enforced policy across school phases. Two practical implications follow. Enforcement must be centralised rather than left to individual teachers if it is not to become a workload and behaviour problem in its own right; and the policy needs a defined and consistently applied consequence, because inconsistency is what pupils report as unfair.

The literature also suggests what a workable policy looks like. It is applied at the school gate rather than the classroom door, because a policy that asks teachers to adjudicate phone use lesson by lesson is the one the Swedish classroom studies find breaking down. It has a single consequence that every member of staff applies in the same way. It is introduced with the staff who will enforce it rather than announced to them, since the enforcement burden falls on them and not on the authority. And it is monitored, because the difference between a stated policy and an enforced one is the difference between the schools that show an effect in these studies and the schools that do not. Members should expect the first term to generate a spike in behaviour incidents; the studies that report on this describe it as settling rather than persisting.

On parents and safeguarding, the published evidence is thin, and we should say so to members rather than imply otherwise. Lepkowska (2019) summarises the practical arguments as they are put by schools and school nurses: the ability to contact a child during the day, medical devices and apps that run on phones, and the position of pupils who travel long distances or have caring responsibilities. There is no evaluation that tests whether a school office is an adequate substitute for direct contact. What the SMART Schools finding suggests is that a policy limited to the school day leaves most of a pupil's phone use untouched, so a ban should not be presented to parents as a response to online harm in general.

## What we do not know

Nothing in this literature addresses pupils with SEND, for whom phones are sometimes an assistive technology, or sixth formers, who are excluded from most of the studies above. No study follows a cohort for long enough to say whether an attainment gain persists. The Norwegian and Swedish nulls have not been explained, and the honest reading is that the English result may be specific to its period, when phones were less central to adolescent life than they are now.
