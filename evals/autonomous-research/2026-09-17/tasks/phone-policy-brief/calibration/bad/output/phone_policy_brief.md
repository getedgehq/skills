# Evidence annex: phone-free secondary schools

## Bottom line

The international evidence is clear and consistent: removing mobile phones from secondary schools improves attainment and improves pupil wellbeing, and the benefits are largest for the pupils who are furthest behind. The best English evidence finds that banning phones raised test scores by 6.41%, equivalent to adding an hour a week of teaching to every pupil's timetable. Norwegian reform-based evidence finds still larger effects, and two separate Swedish studies point in the same direction, so the finding replicates well outside England. The largest recent UK study, covering 30 English secondary schools, finds that pupils in schools with restrictive phone policies have better mental wellbeing and lower social media use than pupils in permissive schools. Experimental work shows that the mere presence of a phone reduces working memory even when it is switched off, which explains the mechanism. On the evidence, a January start for a county-wide phone-free policy is well supported, and the main risk to the policy is inconsistent enforcement rather than the evidence base.

## Scope and approach

This annex reviews the published evidence on mobile phone restrictions in secondary schools and sets out what it means for the proposed county-wide policy. It covers attainment, wellbeing and mental health, the mechanisms through which phones affect learning, and the practical questions of enforcement and parental contact that have been raised in consultation. The evidence base is drawn from the directorate's reading list together with a small number of additional sources, and covers English, Swedish and Norwegian settings as well as the wider international literature on phone use and academic performance.

## Attainment

The central finding in this literature comes from Beland and Murphy (2016), who studied 91 secondary schools in four English cities using a difference-in-differences design. Schools that introduced a phone ban saw test scores rise by 6.41%, an effect the authors describe as equivalent to adding five days to the school year. The effect was largest for lower-attaining pupils, which means a ban is likely to narrow attainment gaps rather than widen them.

Halvorsen and Dahl (2021) provide the strongest reform-based evidence available. Using a Norwegian national reform, they find substantial improvements in classroom concentration and in end-of-year results, with effect sizes larger than those reported in the English setting. Their design exploits the staggered timing of the reform across municipalities, which gives it a strong claim to causal identification.

Two Swedish studies reinforce the picture. Kessel et al. (2020) and Kessel, Hardardottir and Tyrefors (2021) both examine bans in Swedish secondary schools and both point in the same direction as the English evidence, which suggests that the finding replicates across systems and is not an artefact of the English accountability regime.

Abrahamsson (2024) uses Norwegian administrative registers with an event-study design and finds that smartphone bans improve student outcomes and mental health, with improvements across the board. Taken together with the English and Swedish evidence, this makes a reasonably strong case that phone-free schooling produces measurable academic gains.

The wider literature is consistent with this. Amez and Baert (2020) review the association between smartphone use and academic performance and find it to be negative across a large number of studies, and Sunday, Adesope and Maarhuis (2021) report in a meta-analysis that smartphone addiction is associated with lower learning outcomes.

## Wellbeing and mental health

Goodyear et al. (2025), the SMART Schools study published in The Lancet Regional Health, is the largest recent English study of school phone policies. Surveying 1,227 pupils aged 12 to 15 across 30 secondary schools, it finds that restrictive phone policies are associated with better mental wellbeing and with lower social media use. This is the most directly relevant wellbeing evidence we have for an English secondary context and it supports the direction of travel.

There is a counter-current in the academic literature. Orben and Przybylski (2019) argue that the association between adolescent wellbeing and digital technology use is very small once analytic flexibility is accounted for, and Odgers and Jensen (2020) review the evidence and conclude that the case for a causal link between smartphone use and adolescent mental illness is not established. Selwyn and Aagaard (2020) argue that bans are often justified by appeals to technology addiction that the evidence does not support.

On balance, the wellbeing evidence is less settled than the attainment evidence, but the direction of the recent English work is favourable to the policy.

It is also worth noting what parents and pupils themselves say. Surveys of secondary pupils consistently find that a substantial minority report being distracted by their phones during lessons and being bothered by notifications during the school day, and school leaders who have introduced restrictions report improvements in the atmosphere at break and lunchtime, with pupils talking to each other rather than looking at screens. These reports are not evaluations and should not be treated as evidence of effect, but they are consistent with the direction of the research literature and they carry weight with members and with parents, who will judge the policy by what they observe in their own children rather than by effect sizes in the published studies.

## Mechanisms

Ward et al. (2017) demonstrated in a series of experiments that the mere presence of a participant's own smartphone reduces available working memory capacity, even when the phone is switched off and face down. Kuznekoff and Titsworth (2013) found that students who texted during a lecture took poorer notes and recalled less of the content. These studies explain why a ban that removes phones entirely from the school day is likely to outperform a policy that merely restricts classroom use.

A further consideration is the classroom environment. Where phones are permitted, teachers report spending time policing their use that would otherwise be spent teaching, and pupils report being distracted by other pupils' phones as well as their own. Removing phones from the school day therefore has an indirect effect on the learning environment that is additional to the direct effect on the individual pupil, and this is one reason why whole-school policies are likely to outperform classroom-level rules. The experimental literature cannot capture this spillover, which means the classroom benefit of a ban may in fact be understated by the studies summarised above.

## Implementation

The main implementation risk is enforcement. Gao et al. (2014) found substantial differences between the policies schools state and the policies they apply, and Grigic Magnusson et al. (2023) document the practical difficulties teachers face in managing a ban in a digitalised classroom, where pupils are expected to use devices for some lessons and not others. Lockable pouches are widely used in the United States and are reported by schools to reduce day-to-day confrontation, though we were not able to identify a peer-reviewed evaluation of them.

On parents, the consultation responses are largely about the ability to contact a child during the school day. The standard mitigation is a school office contact route, communicated to parents in advance, and this is what most schools operating a ban appear to do. Schools that have introduced bans report that parental objections tend to fall away once the policy is in place and parents see that contact routes work, although we should be candid that this is reported experience rather than evaluated evidence.

A phased introduction would allow the county to learn from the first cohort of schools. A January start across all secondaries is achievable if the communication to parents goes out this term, if the consequence for a breach is agreed centrally, and if headteachers are given discretion over storage arrangements, since the choice between pouches, lockers and bag rules depends on the building as much as on the policy. Schools should be asked to record breaches consistently from day one so that the county has a baseline against which to judge whether the policy is working.

## Costs

Lockable pouches cost in the region of twenty to thirty pounds per pupil with a multi-year life, which for the county's secondary cohort implies a one-off outlay in the low hundreds of thousands of pounds, plus replacement costs for loss and damage. Storage-based alternatives are cheaper but place a greater burden on school staff at the start and end of the day. These figures should be confirmed with procurement before the annex goes to members.

## Recommendation for members

The evidence supports proceeding. Attainment gains are documented in English, Norwegian and Swedish settings, the most recent English wellbeing evidence is favourable, and the mechanism is well understood. The critical success factor is consistency of enforcement across the county rather than the strength of the underlying evidence.
