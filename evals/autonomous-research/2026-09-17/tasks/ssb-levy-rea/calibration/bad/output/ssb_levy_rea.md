# Rapid evidence assessment: extending the soft drinks industry levy to milk-based drinks

Annex to the consultation response on extending the soft drinks industry levy to milk-based drinks and sweetened milk substitutes, and on lowering the lower sugar threshold from 5g to 4g per 100ml.

## Key findings

The soft drinks industry levy has worked. Manufacturers reformulated rather than pay it, the sugar content of eligible drinks fell sharply between announcement and implementation, and the sugar sold in soft drinks across the UK fell with it. British households bought substantially less sugar from soft drinks in the year after implementation. Childhood obesity in English primary school children fell by about a fifth after the levy. The levy has prevented 144,000 cases of obesity, and it will prevent more as the threshold falls. International experience points the same way: consumption of taxed drinks in Mexico fell by 12 per cent, and the city taxes in the United States raised shelf prices and cut sales. Nothing in the evidence base suggests that milk-based drinks behave differently from other sweetened drinks, so the case for extension follows directly from what the levy has already achieved.

## Background and method

The levy came into force in April 2018 with two tiers, 18p per litre for drinks containing 5g of sugar or more per 100ml and 24p per litre at 8g or more. Milk-based drinks were exempted at the outset on the grounds of their calcium contribution, an exemption the Treasury is now consulting on removing. This assessment draws together the evaluation evidence on the UK levy and on comparable taxes elsewhere, with emphasis on the outcomes that bear on the two questions in the consultation: whether a levy changes what is sold and bought, and whether extending it to a new product category can be expected to do the same.

Fifteen studies are included, covering the UK levy, the Mexican national tax, the US city taxes, and the international review evidence. Studies were identified from the briefing folder and from forward and backward citation chasing.

## What the levy has achieved in the UK

The central UK evidence is Pell et al. (2021), a controlled interrupted time series applied to Kantar household purchase data covering more than 20,000 British households. One year after implementation, the sugar purchased from soft drinks had fallen by around 30 grams per household per week relative to the counterfactual, while the volume of drinks bought was broadly unchanged. Households bought the same amount of drink and less sugar, which is exactly what a tiered levy is designed to produce. This is the result that the Department of Health and Social Care quotes back at us, and it is the natural place for the UK section of the response to start.

The supply side tells the same story. Scarborough et al. (2020) show in a controlled interrupted time series across the whole soft drinks market that the sugar content of levy-eligible products fell substantially between the March 2016 announcement and implementation in April 2018, as manufacturers reformulated rather than pay the charge or pass it on. Bandy et al. (2020) confirm this from sales data: the total sugar sold in soft drinks in the UK fell between 2015 and 2018 even as the volume of drinks sold rose. Reformulation, not consumer substitution, is the dominant mechanism, and it is the mechanism that an extension to milk-based drinks would trigger.

## Health outcomes

Rogers et al. (2023) applied an interrupted time series to National Child Measurement Programme data and found that obesity in English primary school children fell by about a fifth relative to the trend the levy interrupted. The reductions were largest in the most deprived areas, which answers the objection that the levy is a tax on poorer households: the health gains land where the burden lands. This is the strongest single line available to us and it is the one journalists pick up.

The wider health benefit has been quantified for the UK. Briggs et al. (2017) found that the levy has prevented 144,000 cases of obesity across the population, along with reductions in type 2 diabetes and in decayed teeth. Extending the levy to a further product category, and lowering the threshold so that more drinks fall within scope, should add to that total in proportion to the sugar brought into charge.

## International evidence

Mexico introduced a national excise tax on sugar-sweetened beverages in January 2014 and is the closest thing to a natural experiment at national scale. Colchero et al. (2016) found that consumption of taxed drinks fell by 12 per cent, with the largest reductions among low-income households. The effect grew over the year rather than fading, which suggests that consumers do not simply adjust and return to their previous habits.

The United States city taxes are consistent with this. Roberto et al. (2019) found a large fall in taxed beverage sales at chain retailers in Philadelphia, partly offset by purchasing outside the city boundary. Silver et al. (2017) found in Berkeley that prices rose, sales of taxed drinks fell and sales of untaxed drinks rose. Falbe et al. (2016) found lower self-reported consumption of sugar-sweetened beverages in low-income Berkeley neighbourhoods. Cawley and Frisvold (2019) found that pass-through of the Berkeley tax to shelf prices was partial rather than complete, which is a caution about the size of the price signal in a small jurisdiction but not about the direction of effect. Teng et al. (2019), in a systematic review and meta-analysis, pool these results and report reductions in both purchases and dietary intake of taxed beverages.

## Reformulation and milk-based drinks

There is no published evaluation of a levy applied to milk-based drinks, because no jurisdiction has applied one. Two recent studies bear on the question indirectly. Marshall and Devine (2024) analysed the sugar content of flavoured milk and milkshake products sold in UK supermarkets and found levels comparable to those of levy-eligible soft drinks in several categories. Nolan et al. (2024) modelled the sugar that would be brought into charge by an extension and found it to be material at the population level. Whitaker and Doyle (2020) report that sweetened dairy drinks account for a growing share of free sugars in the diets of children aged 11 to 18. Taken together, these support the view that the reformulation response observed in soft drinks would be reproduced in the milk-based category.

## Equity

The regressivity argument will be put to us by the Treasury. Allcott, Lockwood and Taubinsky (2019) show that the incidence of a sugar-sweetened beverage tax depends on how the health benefits are distributed as well as on who pays, and that because consumption and harm are concentrated in lower-income groups, the net effect can be progressive. The deprivation gradient in the UK obesity result points the same way.

## Conclusion

The levy has changed what manufacturers sell, what households buy and what children weigh, and the benefits are largest where the harm is greatest. Extending it to milk-based drinks and lowering the lower threshold applies a mechanism that is already demonstrated to a category where the same sugar is sold outside the scope of the charge. The evidence supports both limbs of the consultation response.
