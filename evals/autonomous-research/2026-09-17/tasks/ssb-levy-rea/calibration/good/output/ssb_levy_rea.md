# Rapid evidence assessment: the soft drinks industry levy and the case for extension

Prepared for the consultation response on extending the levy to milk-based drinks and to sweetened milk substitutes, and on lowering the lower sugar threshold.

## Key findings

The levy changed what manufacturers sell more than what households drink. The sugar content of levy-eligible drinks fell sharply between announcement and implementation, and total sugar sold in soft drinks fell across the same period. Household purchases of sugar from taxed drinks fell in the year after implementation on the corrected reanalysis. Evidence on health outcomes is limited to a single interrupted time series: an absolute reduction of 1.6 percentage points in obesity prevalence in year 6 girls, largest in the most deprived areas, with no change in year 6 boys or in reception children. No published study evaluates a levy on milk-based drinks. The case for extension rests on reformulation, not on demonstrated health effects.

## Scope and method

This assessment covers evaluations of implemented sugar-sweetened beverage taxes and the reviews that pool them, with priority given to studies of the UK levy, to designs capable of supporting a causal claim, and to outcomes further down the causal chain than price. Searches covered Medline, Scopus and Google Scholar to September 2026, with citation chasing from the two most recent systematic reviews. Seventeen studies are included. Where a study is a projection rather than an evaluation it is labelled as such in the table below, because the distinction has been blurred in previous versions of our briefing.

Studies were included if they evaluated an implemented tax on sweetened beverages using a design with a comparison series or a comparison group, or if they pooled such evaluations. Simulation studies were retained only where they are identified as simulations. Studies of announced but unimplemented taxes, and industry-funded price analyses without a public method, were excluded. Every reference below was checked against the registered record, which matters here for reasons set out in the note on sources.

## What the levy did in the UK

The levy was designed to change supply rather than demand, and that is where the evidence is strongest. Scarborough et al. (2020) show, in a controlled interrupted time series on the full drinks market, that the sugar content of levy-eligible products fell substantially between the March 2016 announcement and implementation in April 2018, with manufacturers reformulating rather than paying. Bandy et al. (2020) reach the same conclusion from sales data: the sugar sold per capita in soft drinks fell between 2015 and 2018 even as the volume of drinks sold rose. For a consultation about extending the levy to a new product category, this is the central finding, because it is the mechanism an extension would be expected to trigger.

The tier structure is doing the work. Because the charge steps at 5g and again at 8g per 100ml, a manufacturer that moves a product just below a threshold escapes the charge entirely, which is a much stronger incentive than a flat per-gram duty of the same expected yield. That is directly relevant to the second limb of the consultation: moving the lower threshold to 4g creates a new step rather than raising a rate, and the UK evidence is about what manufacturers do when faced with a step. It is worth being clear that none of the UK studies isolates the effect of the threshold level itself, so this is an inference from how firms responded, not a measured elasticity.

On household purchasing, the position is more complicated than our previous briefings have allowed. The controlled interrupted time series that we have historically led with was retracted by the BMJ in 2023 after an error was found in the analysis, and it should not be cited. The authors reanalysed the data and published the corrected estimates in BMJ Open (Rogers et al., 2023b), which remains a real and usable result: sugar purchased from levy-eligible drinks fell in the year after implementation, though by less than the retracted paper reported. Our response should cite the reanalysis and nothing else on this point.

## Health outcomes

One study bears directly on health outcomes in the UK. Rogers et al. (2023a) applied an interrupted time series to National Child Measurement Programme data covering more than a million children a year. Nineteen months after implementation, obesity prevalence in year 6 girls was 1.6 percentage points lower in absolute terms than the counterfactual (95% CI 1.1 to 2.1), with the largest reductions in the two most deprived quintiles, where the absolute reduction reached 2.4 percentage points. There was no corresponding change in year 6 boys, and none in reception children. This is a single study of an unrandomised policy, and its authors are careful about attribution, but it is the best health-outcome evidence that exists anywhere for this levy, and its distributional pattern is the strongest argument we have.

The figure of 144,000 fewer cases of obesity that has appeared in our previous letters is not an outcome. It comes from Briggs et al. (2017), a comparative risk assessment model published in 2017, before implementation, which projected 144,383 fewer people with obesity under a best-case scenario in which industry responded to the levy entirely by reformulating. It is a projection of what might happen under assumptions, not a measurement of what did. We should stop using it as though it were the latter, because a Treasury analyst will recognise the number and discount everything around it.

Dental outcomes, which the Faculty of Dental Surgery will ask about, are a genuine gap. The modelled work projects a reduction in decayed teeth, and hospital admissions for tooth extraction in children have fallen since 2018, but no published evaluation links that fall to the levy with a design capable of supporting the attribution. The response should say that the dental case is plausible and untested rather than borrowing the modelled figure, which would repeat the error described above in a second outcome.

## International evidence

The international evidence is about purchases, and mostly about prices. Colchero et al. (2016) found that purchases of taxed beverages in Mexico fell by an average of 6% across 2014 relative to the pre-tax trend, with the decline growing through the year to reach 12% in December 2014, and larger falls among low-income households. The follow-up (Colchero et al., 2017) found the reduction sustained into a second year. The US city taxes tell a consistent story about pass-through and sales: prices rose in Berkeley, Philadelphia and Seattle, and the volume of taxed drinks sold fell (Silver et al., 2017; Roberto et al., 2019; Powell and Leider, 2020), with Berkeley pass-through incomplete (Cawley and Frisvold, 2016). Two systematic reviews pool these results and agree on the direction and rough size (Teng et al., 2019; Andreyeva et al., 2022).

Two qualifications matter for the consultation. Cross-border shopping absorbed a meaningful share of the Philadelphia effect, which is a warning about narrow tax bases rather than about taxes. And almost all of this evidence measures what was bought, not what was drunk.

## From purchases to intake

The steer note is right that we are thin here, and the honest answer is that the field is thin. Cawley et al. (2019) is the most direct test: it pairs purchase data with household surveys in Philadelphia and finds that the fall in reported adult consumption was smaller and less precisely estimated than the fall in purchases. Falbe et al. (2016) found lower self-reported consumption of taxed drinks in low-income Berkeley neighbourhoods after the tax, but from repeated street-intercept surveys rather than from a panel, so it carries the usual self-report and sampling caveats. The meta-analysis by Teng et al. (2019) reports pooled estimates for dietary intake alongside purchases, and the intake estimates carry substantially wider intervals. Our response should claim a purchasing and reformulation effect, which the evidence supports, and should not claim an intake effect, which it does not yet support.

## Equity

The regressivity objection will be put to us and it has a serious answer. Allcott, Lockwood and Taubinsky (2019) show that whether an SSB tax is regressive depends on how the health benefits are distributed, not only on who pays: because consumption and the associated harm are concentrated in lower-income groups, so are the benefits, and the net effect can be progressive. Dubois, Griffith and O'Connell (2020) qualify this, finding that soda taxes are poorly targeted at the individuals who consume the most sugar, since heavy consumers are spread across the income distribution. Both belong in the response, because presenting only the first invites the second.

## Milk-based drinks

There is no evaluation of a levy on milk-based drinks anywhere in the literature, so nothing here can be said to be evidence about the proposed extension. The argument for extension is an argument from mechanism: the UK evidence shows reformulation is the dominant industry response to a tiered threshold, milk-based drinks were exempted on calcium grounds rather than sugar grounds, and there is no reason in the evidence to expect manufacturers to behave differently in a category that faces the same threshold structure. That argument should be made as an argument, not dressed up as a finding.

Two features of the category weaken the transfer and should be acknowledged rather than left for a reviewer to raise. Sweetened milk drinks carry nutrients that the drinks already in scope do not, so reformulation that cuts sugar by diluting or by substituting sweeteners has a different nutritional consequence here than it does in cola. And the category is more fragmented, with a larger share of volume in own-label and food-service products that face weaker reputational pressure to reformulate. Neither undermines the case, but the response is stronger for having answered them first.

## Limitations

The UK evidence rests on interrupted time series designs without a true control group, which are vulnerable to anything else that changed in 2016 to 2019. One study addresses health outcomes, and it addresses one sex in one age group. Almost all outcome evidence is purchasing, not intake. Publication and reporting incentives in this field are strong in one direction, and the retraction described above is a reminder that the evidence base can move.

Two further limits are worth stating. The international evidence comes from tax designs that differ from ours in rate, base and structure, so it establishes that beverage taxes change prices and purchases rather than that this levy will behave a particular way in a new category. And several of the UK evaluations share authors, data sources and a funding stream, which is normal in a small policy literature but means the studies are not independent replications of one another.

## Note on sources

Pell et al. (2021), which the steer note identifies as the key UK purchasing study, was retracted by the BMJ in 2023 and has been replaced here by the authors' corrected reanalysis in BMJ Open. The hand-typed Berkeley pass-through entry was dated 2019 in the starter file; the registered publication year for that article is 2016 and the entry has been corrected. Five further studies have been added, covering the second Mexican year, the Seattle and Philadelphia evaluations, the intake question and the most recent international meta-analysis.
