# Equity and Economic Considerations

## Equity impacts

A common concern raised about SSB taxation is regressivity: lower-income households spend a larger proportion of their income on taxed beverages, creating a disproportionate financial burden. Allcott et al. {cite_10.1093/qje/qjz017} demonstrated this conventional regressivity using US data, showing SSB taxes function as a regressive consumption tax in narrow financial terms.

However, the UK SDIL evidence demonstrates that health benefits concentrate precisely in the groups bearing the financial burden. Ma et al. {cite_10.1371/journal.pmed.1004160} showed the largest obesity reductions occurred in the most deprived decile, with effect sizes declining monotonically across the deprivation distribution. Rogers et al. {cite_10.1371/journal.pmed.1004160} found that the entire measurable obesity effect was concentrated in the most deprived quintile, with no significant change in affluent areas. This reverses the conventional regressivity critique: while the financial burden falls proportionally on all consumers, health gains accrue specifically where baseline obesity rates are highest and where chronic disease risk is greatest.

Additionally, the reformulation mechanism mitigates direct financial impact. Bandy et al. {cite_10.1186/s12916-019-1477-4} demonstrated that 85% of sugar reduction occurred through product reformulation rather than price-induced substitution, meaning the majority of consumers experienced sugar reduction in their already-purchased products without price changes. Low-income households benefited from reduced sugar exposure without bearing levy costs for those reformulated products.

This pattern of concentrated health gains in deprived groups appears mechanistically linked to baseline consumption patterns: lower-income households consumed more high-sugar drinks pre-SDIL {cite_10.1136/bmj.n254}, creating both higher exposure to levy incentives and greater potential for health improvement. The policy effectively targeted the gradient rather than treating all consumers identically.

## Economic impacts and cost-effectiveness

Scarborough et al. {cite_10.1371/journal.pmed.1004371} conducted health economic modeling using observed purchasing and reformulation data from the first three years post-implementation. They project £39 million in NHS healthcare cost savings and 5,234 quality-adjusted life years (QALYs) gained over a ten-year horizon, driven primarily by reduced obesity prevalence and prevented type 2 diabetes cases. These projections use conservative assumptions about sustained behavioral effects and do not account for potential additional gains from reduced dental disease or cardiovascular events.

The levy also generated substantial revenue: approximately £340 million annually in its first three years {cite_10.1186/s12916-019-1477-4}. This revenue, earmarked for school sports and breakfast club programs, represents a fiscal gain independent of health impacts. The combination of revenue generation and healthcare cost avoidance creates a rare policy scenario where public health intervention is self-financing.

Caro et al. {cite_10.1371/journal.pmed.1003025} systematically reviewed international price elasticity evidence, finding values between -1.0 and -1.4 for SSBs, indicating strong price responsiveness. This suggests even modest tax rates can generate substantial consumption changes. Cobiac et al. {cite_10.1111/obr.12868} and Teng et al. {cite_10.1111/obr.12868} provide complementary modeling evidence that population-level consumption reductions of even 5-10% translate to measurable reductions in obesity, diabetes, and cardiovascular disease incidence over 10-25 year timeframes, due to the ubiquity of SSB consumption across all age groups.
