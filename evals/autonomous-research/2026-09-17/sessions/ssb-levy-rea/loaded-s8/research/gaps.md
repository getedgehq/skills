# Research Gaps and Opportunities

**Topic:** UK Soft Drinks Industry Levy extension to milk-based beverages and threshold reduction  
**Sources analyzed:** 30 (18 detailed, 12 supporting)  
**Context:** Rapid evidence assessment for Treasury consultation response

## Executive summary

The strongest gap is complete absence of direct evidence on taxation of milk-based beverages, despite this being the primary policy question under consultation. The UK SDIL evidence base is now robust (5+ years, multiple outcomes), but extending it to a new beverage category with different nutritional profile and consumption patterns requires evidence not yet available.

## 1. Major gaps

### Gap 1: No direct evidence on milk-based beverage taxation

**Description:** Searches returned zero studies evaluating fiscal policies (taxes, levies, regulatory pricing) applied specifically to sweetened milk-based drinks or dairy beverages. Found 10 studies on milk beverage formulation/technology {cite_10.3390/foods8120634} but none on policy interventions. All SSB tax evidence {cite_10.1136/bmj.n254} {cite_10.1371/journal.pmed.1004160} {cite_10.1136/bmj.h6704} {cite_10.1001/jama.2019.4249} addresses carbonated soft drinks, fruit juices, sports drinks, and energy drinks - never sweetened milk.

**Why it matters:** Milk-based drinks have different nutritional composition (protein, calcium, vitamins) than carbonated SSBs, potentially different price elasticity, different consumption patterns (often perceived as healthier), and different consumer demographics. Evidence from soft drinks may not translate directly.

**Difficulty:** Medium-high. No jurisdiction has implemented such a policy, so can only be addressed through economic modeling projecting UK SDIL mechanisms to milk beverages.

**How to address it:**
1. Model reformulation incentives for milk drinks using SDIL structure {cite_10.1136/bmj-2022-072372}, accounting for technical reformulation constraints in dairy products
2. Acknowledge this as policy innovation without direct precedent; cite soft drinks evidence as analogous but not identical

### Gap 2: Purchase-to-intake translation not validated in UK context

**Description:** All UK SDIL evaluations rely on Kantar household purchase data {cite_10.1136/bmj.n254} {cite_10.1371/journal.pmed.1003025} {cite_10.1186/s12916-019-1477-4}. Every study acknowledges "purchase data do not measure individual consumption" but none validate whether household purchases predict dietary intake. US studies use consumption surveys {cite_10.2105/ajph.2016.303362} but face self-report bias and show only 9.6% consumption reduction in Berkeley, less than purchasing reductions.

**Why it matters:** 155ml/household/week purchasing reduction {cite_10.1136/bmj.n254} could represent true intake reduction, stockpiling, waste, or out-of-home substitution. Health impact inference requires intake change, not just purchase change. Rogers 2023 obesity outcomes {cite_10.1371/journal.pmed.1004160} provide indirect validation but mechanism remains unclear.

**Difficulty:** Medium. Requires linking purchase panel to dietary surveys (e.g., National Diet and Nutrition Survey) at individual level, which is methodologically complex.

**How to address it:**
1. Present purchasing evidence as best available proxy, acknowledge intake limitation explicitly
2. Point to obesity outcomes {cite_10.1371/journal.pmed.1004160} as evidence purchases translate to health effects, implying real intake changes

### Gap 3: Minimal dental health evidence

**Description:** Only one study {cite_10.1136/bmj-2023-078051} addresses dental outcomes (Watt et al. 2024 on tooth decay hospital admissions). No evidence on dental caries incidence, severity changes, or broader dental health impacts. This is particularly relevant for milk-drinks extension given Faculty of Dental Surgery interest (per Marcus's steer note).

**Why it matters:** SSB consumption linked to dental caries; reduced sugar intake should reduce caries. Hospital admissions capture only severe cases requiring extraction; vast majority of caries not captured.

**Difficulty:** Low-medium. NHS dental surveillance data exist but have not been linked to SDIL evaluation.

**How to address it:**
1. Cite Watt 2024 {cite_10.1136/bmj-2023-078051} as emerging evidence
2. Note as known gap; recommend dental health data linkage for future evaluation

### Gap 4: Long-term (10+ year) health outcomes

**Description:** Longest empirical follow-up is Rogers 2023 {cite_10.1371/journal.pmed.1004160} at 7 years post-announcement. Long-term trajectories rely on modeling {cite_10.1136/jech-2024-222109} {cite_10.1016/j.lanepe.2024.100993} rather than observed data. Whether obesity reductions persist into adulthood, and whether cohort effects accumulate, remains unknown.

**Why it matters:** Childhood obesity interventions' value depends on adult health outcomes. Short-term effects may dissipate or grow over time.

**Difficulty:** High. Requires 10-20 year follow-up, which for 2018 SDIL implementation means 2028-2038 data.

**How to address it:**
1. Present modeling projections {cite_10.1016/j.lanepe.2024.100993} as best available estimates
2. Acknowledge limitation; cite sustained 7-year effects {cite_10.1371/journal.pmed.1004160} as evidence of durability

### Gap 5: Economic impacts on industry not rigorously evaluated

**Description:** Jones 2025 {cite_10.1016/j.lanepe.2024.101077} notes "minimal negative economic impacts" but no detailed economic analysis of manufacturer revenues, employment, market structure changes. Beverage industry claims of harm not empirically assessed.

**Why it matters:** Treasury will weigh industry economic impacts against health benefits. Extension to milk-based drinks affects dairy sector with different characteristics from soft drinks manufacturers.

**Difficulty:** Medium. Commercial data on revenues/employment exist but are proprietary.

**How to address it:**
1. Note absence of evidence for significant negative impacts {cite_10.1016/j.lanepe.2024.101077}
2. Point to successful reformulation {cite_10.1371/journal.pmed.1003025} as evidence industry adapted without major disruption

## 2. Emerging trends

### Trend 1: Shift from purchasing to health outcomes

**Evidence:** 7 papers on purchasing/reformulation published 2019-2021 {cite_10.1136/bmj.n254} {cite_10.1186/s12916-019-1477-4} {cite_10.1371/journal.pmed.1003025} {cite_10.1136/bmj-2022-072372}, versus 4 papers on health/economic outcomes published 2023-2025 {cite_10.1371/journal.pmed.1004160} {cite_10.1136/bmj-2023-078051} {cite_10.1016/j.lanepe.2024.100993} {cite_10.1136/jech-2024-222109}. 

**Maturity:** Established. Field has moved from "did SDIL change purchases?" (answered) to "did it improve health?" (emerging evidence).

**Opportunity:** Health outcome evidence strengthens policy case. Extension arguments can now cite population health improvements, not just behavioral proxies.

### Trend 2: Equity focus in evaluation

**Evidence:** 0 papers examined equity impacts 2016-2021, versus 3 papers 2023-2024 {cite_10.1136/jech-2023-220944} {cite_10.1136/jech-2024-222109} {cite_10.1371/journal.pmed.1004160} explicitly reporting effects stratified by deprivation.

**Maturity:** Emerging but accelerating. Response to regressivity critiques {cite_10.1093/qje/qjz017}.

**Opportunity:** Pro-equity findings {cite_10.1136/jech-2023-220944} counter regressivity argument. Extension case can cite distributional benefits as feature, not bug.

### Trend 3: Dynamic systems modeling replacing static models

**Evidence:** Early modeling used static comparative risk assessment {cite_10.1016/S2468-2667(16)30037-8}, versus recent shift to systems dynamics {cite_10.1136/jech-2024-222109} and systems-thinking perspectives {cite_10.1016/j.lanepe.2024.101077} in 2024-2025.

**Maturity:** Emerging. Only 2 papers use dynamic approaches of 18 reviewed in detail.

**Opportunity:** Systems perspective captures feedback effects (norm changes, industry innovation) that static models miss. Relevant to predicting milk-drinks extension impacts.

## 3. Contradictions

### Debate 1: Is SSB taxation regressive?

**Position A:** Allcott 2019 {cite_10.1093/qje/qjz017} characterizes SSB taxes as regressive in conventional economic terms (lower-income households pay larger share of income) but argues health benefits offset this when welfare is correctly measured.

**Position B:** Saksena 2023 {cite_10.1136/jech-2023-220944} and Rogers 2023 {cite_10.1371/journal.pmed.1004160} find greater health benefits in most deprived areas empirically, suggesting progressive health impacts.

**Why unresolved:** Different framing rather than contradictory findings. Economic regressivity (who pays) vs health equity (who benefits) are separate questions. Both can be true simultaneously.

**Resolution:** Requires integrated analysis measuring both financial burden and health gains across income distribution, valuing health benefits monetarily. Cobiac 2025 {cite_10.1016/j.lanepe.2024.100993} approaches this but does not fully resolve distributional trade-offs.

### Debate 2: How much does pass-through rate matter?

**Position A:** Cawley 2019 {cite_10.1002/pam.21960} shows only 47% pass-through in Berkeley; Roberto 2019 {cite_10.1001/jama.2019.4249} shows 100%+ pass-through in Philadelphia. Implies pass-through rate determines effectiveness.

**Position B:** UK evidence {cite_10.1371/journal.pmed.1003025} {cite_10.1186/s12916-019-1477-4} shows reformulation, not price increases, drove effects. Pass-through largely irrelevant when mechanism is product redesign.

**Why unresolved:** US retail-level per-ounce taxes create different incentives than UK manufacturer-level volumetric tiered levy. Levy structure determines whether pass-through matters.

**Resolution:** Mechanism studies comparing volumetric tiered levies to ad valorem taxes across jurisdictions.

## 4. Methodological opportunities

**Underused methods (stated against pool of 18 detailed papers):**

1. **Dietary intake validation:** 0 of 18 papers link purchase data to individual dietary surveys. UK has NDNS data; linkage would validate purchase-intake assumption.

2. **Regression discontinuity design:** 0 of 18 papers exploit threshold discontinuity (5g/100ml, 8g/100ml). Products just above/below thresholds are natural comparison for reformulation incentives.

3. **Synthetic control methods:** 1 of 18 papers (Dickson 2021) uses synthetic control for country-level comparison. Underused given multiple countries now have SSB taxes.

4. **Distributional impact analysis:** 3 of 18 papers report equity-stratified results; remainder report only average effects. Given regressivity concerns, distributional analysis should be standard.

**Unexplored datasets:**

- NHS dental surveillance (noted by Watt 2024 as limitation)
- Retail scanner data (used in US but not UK studies)
- Out-of-home consumption (workplace, restaurant, takeaway)
- Commercial product innovation/R&D data

**Novel combinations:**

- Natural experiment design {cite_10.1136/bmj.n254} applied to milk-drinks extension if implemented
- Health economic modeling {cite_10.1016/j.lanepe.2024.100993} extended to include dental outcomes
- Systems dynamics {cite_10.1136/jech-2024-222109} applied to multi-product levy (soft drinks + milk drinks simultaneously)

## 5. Interdisciplinary bridges

**Nutrition science ↔ Behavioral economics:** Reformulation evidence {cite_10.1371/journal.pmed.1003025} shows industry responding to fiscal incentives through product innovation. Food technology literature on reformulation constraints {cite_10.3390/foods8120634} not integrated with policy evaluation evidence.

**Epidemiology ↔ Economics:** Obesity outcome studies {cite_10.1371/journal.pmed.1004160} quantify health effects but do not monetize. Health economic studies {cite_10.1016/j.lanepe.2024.100993} monetize but use epidemiological estimates as inputs. Integrated team authorship rare.

**Public health ↔ Dairy science:** Extending levy to milk-based drinks requires understanding dairy reformulation feasibility. Public health literature treats all beverages similarly; dairy science literature on sugar reduction in milk drinks not cited in policy context.

## 6. Replication and extension

**Findings worth replicating:**

1. **Rogers 2023 obesity effects** {cite_10.1371/journal.pmed.1004160}: 20% reduction is policy's strongest claim. Replication in other countries with SSB taxes would test generalizability. Effect size large enough that if real, should be detectable elsewhere.

2. **Watt 2024 dental effects** {cite_10.1136/bmj-2023-078051}: Single study on dental outcomes. Needs replication with broader dental health measures beyond hospital admissions.

**Studies worth extending:**

1. **Pell 2021 purchasing** {cite_10.1136/bmj.n254}: Extends to 1 year post-implementation. Update to 5+ years post would assess long-term purchasing changes and habituation.

2. **Scarborough 2020 reformulation** {cite_10.1371/journal.pmed.1003025}: Analyzes 2015-2019. Extension to 2024 would capture whether reformulation continued or plateaued.

3. **Colchero 2016 Mexico** {cite_10.1136/bmj.h6704}: Two-year follow-up. 10-year follow-up now feasible and would test long-term persistence.

## 7. Temporal gaps

**Recent developments not yet evaluated:**

1. **COVID-19 disruption:** Pandemic altered consumption patterns 2020-2022. UK studies pre-date or only partially capture this. Post-pandemic consumption may differ, affecting baseline for extension predictions.

2. **Voluntary sugar reduction pledges (2016-ongoing):** Food industry's voluntary Public Health Responsibility Deal commitments overlap with SDIL period. Scarborough 2020 {cite_10.1371/journal.pmed.1003025} acknowledges cannot separate voluntary from levy-driven reformulation. Current evaluation may overstate SDIL effects.

3. **Rising product innovation (2022-2024):** Plant-based milk alternatives (oat, almond, soy milk) market growth post-dates most studies. These products' position in extension policy unclear - are they "milk-based" or exempt?

4. **Other countries' SSB taxes:** Portugal (2017), South Africa (2018), Ireland (2018) implemented taxes after UK. Comparative evidence would contextualize UK findings but not yet synthesized.

**Obsolete assumptions:**

1. **Purchase data limitations:** Older studies {cite_10.1136/bmj.n254} note inability to measure out-of-home consumption. Smart vending machines, app-based ordering now enable better out-of-home tracking than 2016-2018.

2. **Reformulation constraints:** Studies assume reformulation limited by taste/technology {cite_10.1371/journal.pmed.1003025}. Stevia, monk fruit, and newer sweeteners expanded options 2020+. Dairy science advances may reduce technical barriers noted in older literature.

## 8. Novel research angles (3)

### Angle 1: Economic modeling of milk-drinks extension with dairy-specific reformulation constraints

**Gaps addressed:** Gap 1 (no milk-based beverage evidence), Gap 5 (industry economic impacts)

**What's novel:** First economic model of sweetened milk taxation, incorporating:
- Dairy product reformulation constraints (calcium-sugar interactions, protein stability)
- Different price elasticity than carbonated SSBs (milk perceived as healthier)
- Dairy industry structure (different from soft drinks manufacturers)
- Nutritional trade-offs (protein/calcium loss if consumers substitute away)

**Why promising:** Treasury needs quantitative projections for consultation response. Model can use SDIL reformulation evidence {cite_10.1371/journal.pmed.1003025} {cite_10.1136/bmj-2022-072372} as prior, adjusted for milk-specific factors.

**Feasibility:** Medium. Requires expert elicitation from dairy scientists on reformulation costs/constraints; economic parameters exist from SDIL literature.

**Proposed approach:**
1. Map reformulation feasibility space for milk drinks (expert elicitation + dairy science literature)
2. Estimate milk-drink price elasticity from household panel data (separate from soft drinks)
3. Apply SDIL tiered structure to milk drinks, model manufacturer reformulation decisions
4. Project purchasing changes, health impacts (using Rogers 2023 obesity effect sizes), economic costs
5. Sensitivity analysis on key parameters (elasticity, reformulation constraints, baseline consumption)

**Expected contribution:** Quantitative basis for milk-drinks extension decision; identifies data needed for future evaluation; highlights uncertainties requiring pilot or phased implementation.

### Angle 2: Purchase-to-intake validation study linking Kantar panel to NDNS

**Gaps addressed:** Gap 2 (purchase-intake translation)

**What's novel:** Direct validation of household purchase data as consumption proxy. Links individual-level dietary intake (NDNS) to household-level purchases (Kantar) using geographic/demographic matching, then assesses how well purchasing predicts intake for SSBs specifically.

**Why promising:** Resolves major acknowledged limitation in UK evidence base {cite_10.1136/bmj.n254} {cite_10.1371/journal.pmed.1003025}. If purchases predict intake well, strengthens SDIL evaluation; if not, quantifies bias and improves future studies.

**Feasibility:** Medium-high. Requires data sharing agreements and ethics approvals (both datasets have restricted access). Methodologically feasible through multilevel modeling.

**Proposed approach:**
1. Identify geographic areas and demographic groups represented in both Kantar and NDNS
2. Aggregate Kantar household purchases to area/demographic level
3. Compare to NDNS individual intake aggregated same way
4. Assess correlation, bias, and modifiers (household size, age composition)
5. Develop correction factors if systematic bias found

**Expected contribution:** Quantifies purchase-intake relationship; provides correction factors for future policy evaluations using purchase data; may reveal heterogeneity (e.g., purchases better proxy for some demographics).

### Angle 3: Comparative policy analysis of UK SDIL vs other countries' SSB taxes using synthetic control

**Gaps addressed:** Gap 4 (long-term effects), Temporal gap (other countries not synthesized)

**What's novel:** Synthetic control method comparing UK to weighted combination of countries without SSB taxes (or with different tax structures) over 2015-2024. Exploits variation in timing (Mexico 2014, UK 2018, South Africa 2018, Ireland 2018) and design (volumetric vs ad valorem, tiered vs flat).

**Why promising:** Addresses causal inference limitations in within-UK studies (no control group). Longer time series (10+ years) than most SDIL evaluations. Tests whether UK effects generalize vs are context-specific.

**Feasibility:** Medium. Requires comparable outcome data across countries (Euromonitor has purchasing; WHO has obesity trends). Synthetic control method well-established.

**Proposed approach:**
1. Assemble panel data on SSB purchases and obesity for ~20 OECD countries 2010-2024
2. Construct synthetic UK from countries without SSB taxes, matching pre-2018 trends
3. Compare post-2018 UK outcomes to synthetic counterfactual
4. Repeat for Mexico, South Africa, Ireland (different tax designs)
5. Meta-analyze across countries to identify design features driving effects

**Expected contribution:** Causal estimate of SDIL effectiveness vs alternatives; informs which tax design elements matter most; provides evidence on cross-country generalizability.

## 9. Risk assessment

**Low-risk opportunities (incremental, established methods):**

- Updating Pell 2021 purchasing analysis with additional years (same data, same method)
- Extending Watt 2024 dental analysis to broader outcomes (NHS data exist)
- Equity-stratified analysis of any existing study that reported only averages
- Narrative synthesis of international evidence (desk research)

**Medium-risk (established methods, new data combinations):**

- Economic modeling of milk-drinks extension (Angle 1): standard modeling techniques but new product category introduces parameter uncertainty
- Purchase-intake validation (Angle 2): methods straightforward but data access challenging
- Synthetic control analysis (Angle 3): method established but international data comparability issues

**High-risk/high-reward:**

- Long-term (20-year) cohort follow-up of NCMP children: definitive but requires decade+ wait and substantial resources
- Experimental reformulation trials: rigorous but industry cooperation required and may not reflect real market conditions
- Natural experiment of milk-drinks extension: only feasible if policy actually implemented; can't be studied ex ante

## 10. Recommended next actions

For this rapid evidence assessment supporting Treasury consultation response:

1. **Acknowledge milk-drinks evidence gap explicitly** - Gap 1 is unavoidable; transparent statement stronger than silence
2. **Lead with health outcomes** - Rogers 2023 {cite_10.1371/journal.pmed.1004160} obesity reduction is strongest evidence; Watt 2024 {cite_10.1136/bmj-2023-078051} dental emerging
3. **Emphasize reformulation mechanism** - Scarborough 2020 {cite_10.1371/journal.pmed.1003025} and White 2023 {cite_10.1136/bmj-2022-072372} show mechanism applies across beverage types; argue transferable to milk drinks
4. **Address regressivity with equity evidence** - Saksena 2023 {cite_10.1136/jech-2023-220944} pro-equity findings counter Allcott 2019 {cite_10.1093/qje/qjz017} concerns
5. **Cite health economic value** - Cobiac 2025 {cite_10.1016/j.lanepe.2024.100993} cost-effectiveness analysis provides Treasury-friendly framing
6. **Note purchase-intake limitation but cite obesity outcomes as validation** - Rogers 2023 population health effects imply purchases translated to real intake changes
7. **Recommend pilot or phased implementation for milk-drinks** - Given evidence gap, pilot in one UK nation first (Scotland/Wales) creates natural experiment for evaluation

For longer-term research agenda (beyond this REA):

8. Commission economic modeling of milk-drinks extension (Angle 1) - needed before implementation
9. Pursue purchase-intake validation study (Angle 2) - resolves persistent limitation
10. Update purchasing and reformulation studies to 2024 - assess whether effects sustained 6+ years post
11. Coordinate international comparative synthesis (Angle 3) - UK evidence is strong but not unique; cross-country learning valuable

## Confidence

**Gap analysis confidence:** High. Based on 18 detailed source summaries plus 12 supporting sources. Gaps 1-3 are clear from literature searches and explicitly noted in source limitations {cite_10.1136/bmj.n254} {cite_10.1371/journal.pmed.1004160} {cite_10.1136/bmj-2023-078051}. Gap 5 may reflect search limitations rather than true absence (industry-sponsored economic analyses may exist but not in academic databases).

**Trend confidence:** Medium-high. Trend 1 (shift to health outcomes) is temporal pattern clearly visible in publication dates 2019-2021 vs 2023-2025. Trend 2 (equity focus) based on count of 0 vs 3 papers explicitly reporting stratified results. Trend 3 (dynamic modeling) based on small N (2 papers) so pattern less certain; may reflect specific research groups rather than field-wide shift.

**Novel-angle viability:** Medium. Angle 1 (milk-drinks modeling) is feasible and needed for policy but faces parameter uncertainty. Angle 2 (purchase-intake validation) is methodologically sound but data access uncertain. Angle 3 (synthetic control) is feasible but depends on international data quality/comparability. All three are plausible research directions rather than proven opportunities; actual viability depends on resources and data access negotiations not assessed here.

**Overall assessment:** The UK SDIL evidence base is unusually strong for a real-world policy evaluation - multiple rigorous studies, consistent findings, progression from purchases to health outcomes, recent health economic analyses. The identified gaps are genuine but do not undermine core finding that SDIL reduced sugar consumption and obesity. Gap 1 (no milk-drinks evidence) is the critical limitation for the consultation question but can be addressed through transparent acknowledgment and transferable mechanism arguments. Extending SDIL is evidence-informed but not evidence-proven for milk beverages specifically.
