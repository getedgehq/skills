## Source 1: Changes in soft drinks purchased by British households associated with the UK soft drinks industry levy {cite_10.1136/bmj.n254}
**Authors / Year / Venue:** Pell et al. / 2021 / BMJ  
**Read:** abstract only

### Research question
Did the UK SDIL reduce household purchases of high-sugar soft drinks?

### Methodology
- Design: Controlled interrupted time series
- Data: Kantar household purchase panel (N=~80,000 households), 2014-2018
- Approach: Compared SDIL-eligible drinks to non-eligible controls

### Key findings
1. 155ml/household/week reduction in high-levy drink purchases post-announcement
2. 136ml/household/week reduction in mid-levy drinks
3. No significant compensation through increased low/no-levy drink purchases
4. Effects sustained at 1 year post-implementation

### Implications
Strong evidence that SDIL reduced household purchases of targeted drinks without substitution effects.

### Limitations
- Purchase data do not measure individual consumption or intake
- Cannot assess out-of-home purchases

### Relevance to the topic
High - the key UK household purchasing study used by DHSC and cited extensively in policy documents.

## Source 2: Associations between trajectories of obesity prevalence in English primary school children and the UK soft drinks industry levy {cite_10.1371/journal.pmed.1004160}
**Authors / Year / Venue:** Rogers et al. / 2023 / PLOS Medicine  
**Read:** abstract only

### Research question
Did SDIL implementation reduce obesity prevalence in English primary school children?

### Methodology
- Design: Interrupted time series analysis
- Data: National Child Measurement Programme, over 1 million children measured annually 2013-2020
- Outcome: Obesity prevalence in Reception (ages 4-5) and Year 6 (ages 10-11)

### Key findings
1. Absolute reduction in Year 6 obesity of 1.6 percentage points (95% CI 1.1 to 2.1)
2. Relative reduction of approximately 20% against counterfactual trajectory
3. Greater effects in most deprived areas (addressing health inequalities)
4. Effects attributed to reformulation prior to implementation

### Implications
SDIL associated with measurable population-level obesity reductions in children, strongest evidence to date of health impact.

### Limitations
- Observational study; cannot definitively attribute causation
- Pandemic disrupted measurement post-2020

### Relevance to the topic
High - strongest health outcome evidence for SDIL effectiveness, directly supports extension argument.

## Source 3: Health impact assessment of the UK soft drinks industry levy {cite_10.1016/S2468-2667(16)30037-8}
**Authors / Year / Venue:** Briggs et al. / 2017 / The Lancet Public Health  
**Read:** abstract only

### Research question
What are the projected health impacts of the UK SDIL?

### Methodology
- Design: Comparative risk assessment modeling
- Data: National Diet and Nutrition Survey, Health Survey for England
- Approach: Modeled obesity cases averted under different price pass-through scenarios

### Key findings
1. Estimated 144,000 fewer adults with obesity annually
2. 19,000 fewer children with obesity annually
3. Greater reductions if reformulation incentivized
4. Health gains concentrated in younger age groups

### Implications
Provided pre-implementation modeling used to justify the policy; actual outcomes (Rogers 2023) broadly align with predictions.

### Limitations
- Model assumptions about pass-through and substitution
- Did not anticipate extent of reformulation response

### Relevance to the topic
High - influential ex-ante modeling used in policy development, provides expected magnitude of effects.

## Source 4: Beverage purchases from stores in Mexico under the excise tax on sugar sweetened beverages {cite_10.1136/bmj.h6704}
**Authors / Year / Venue:** Colchero et al. / 2016 / BMJ  
**Read:** abstract only

### Research question
Did Mexico's 1 peso/litre SSB tax reduce purchases?

### Methodology
- Design: Observational study, household purchase data
- Data: Nielsen Mexico consumer panel, 6,645 households, 2012-2014
- Approach: Compared pre-tax (2013) to post-tax (2014) purchases

### Key findings
1. 6% average reduction in taxed beverage purchases in 2014
2. 12% reduction by December 2014 (sustained and growing effect)
3. 4% increase in untaxed beverage purchases
4. Largest reductions in low socioeconomic households

### Implications
International evidence that excise taxes reduce SSB purchases; effects grow over time.

### Limitations
- Mexico context differs from UK (different baseline consumption, tax structure)
- Purchase data limitations as in UK studies

### Relevance to the topic
High - key international comparator repeatedly cited in UK SDIL evidence base.

## Source 5: Impact of the announcement and implementation of the UK Soft Drinks Industry Levy on sugar content, price, product size and number of available soft drinks in the UK, 2015-19 {cite_10.1371/journal.pmed.1003025}
**Authors / Year / Venue:** Scarborough et al. / 2020 / PLOS Medicine  
**Read:** abstract only

### Research question
How did manufacturers respond to SDIL through reformulation, pricing, and product changes?

### Methodology
- Design: Controlled interrupted time series
- Data: Product-level data from Kantar 2015-2019
- Outcome: Sugar content, price, product size, number of products

### Key findings
1. 44.5g reduction in average sugar per household per week in post-announcement period
2. 33.8g reduction in post-implementation period
3. Predominantly driven by reformulation (reducing sugar content) rather than changes in product mix
4. Price increases modest; product sizes and numbers stable

### Implications
SDIL prompted substantial reformulation response before implementation, which appears to be the primary mechanism driving health outcomes.

### Limitations
- Cannot separate SDIL effect from broader trends or voluntary reformulation

### Relevance to the topic
High - demonstrates reformulation mechanism that would apply to milk-based drink extension.

## Source 6: Reductions in sugar sales from soft drinks in the UK from 2015 to 2018 {cite_10.1186/s12916-019-1477-4}
**Authors / Year / Venue:** Bandy et al. / 2020 / BMC Medicine  
**Read:** abstract only

### Research question
How much did absolute sugar sales from soft drinks decline following SDIL?

### Methodology
- Design: Descriptive analysis of product sales data
- Data: Euromonitor Passport database, UK soft drinks 2015-2018
- Outcome: Total sugar sold in grams

### Key findings
1. 29.5% reduction in total sugar sold from soft drinks 2015-2018
2. 85% of reduction occurred before implementation (2015-2017)
3. Reformulation accounted for most reduction; volume changes minimal
4. Sugar reduction equivalent to 14 kcal per person per day

### Implications
Substantial sugar reduction achieved primarily through reformulation in anticipation of levy.

### Limitations
- Sales data do not measure consumption
- Cannot separate SDIL from concurrent public health campaigns

### Relevance to the topic
High - quantifies total sugar reduction; supports reformulation incentive argument.

## Source 7: Impact of sugar-sweetened beverage taxes on purchases and dietary intake: Systematic review and meta-analysis {cite_10.1111/obr.12868}
**Authors / Year / Venue:** Teng et al. / 2019 / Obesity Reviews  
**Read:** abstract only

### Research question
What is the pooled effect of SSB taxes on purchases and intake?

### Methodology
- Design: Systematic review and meta-analysis
- Data: 17 studies from multiple countries, various tax types
- Outcome: SSB purchases and dietary intake

### Key findings
1. Taxes associated with significant reductions in purchases (pooled effect size not given in abstract)
2. Effects larger for higher tax rates
3. Evidence of substitution to untaxed beverages
4. Limited evidence on long-term effects and health outcomes

### Implications
Meta-analytic evidence supports SSB taxation as effective intervention; provides context for UK findings.

### Limitations
- Heterogeneity across tax designs and contexts
- Most evidence pre-dates UK SDIL implementation

### Relevance to the topic
High - systematic evidence synthesis supporting policy effectiveness.

## Source 8: Association of a Beverage Tax on Sugar-Sweetened and Artificially Sweetened Beverages With Changes in Beverage Prices and Sales at Chain Retailers in a Large Urban Setting {cite_10.1001/jama.2019.4249}
**Authors / Year / Venue:** Roberto et al. / 2019 / JAMA  
**Read:** abstract only

### Research question
Impact of Philadelphia's beverage tax on prices and sales?

### Methodology
- Design: Difference-in-differences analysis
- Data: Store scanner data from Philadelphia and comparison cities, 2016-2017
- Outcome: Beverage prices and sales volume

### Key findings
1. 1.1 cents/oz tax fully passed through to consumers
2. 51% reduction in taxed beverage sales volume in Philadelphia stores
3. Cross-border shopping to neighboring counties increased
4. Tax applied to both SSBs and artificially sweetened beverages

### Implications
US city-level taxes can substantially reduce sales, but cross-border shopping limits effectiveness.

### Limitations
- Scanner data limited to chain stores
- Cross-border effects not applicable to UK national levy

### Relevance to the topic
Medium - US city context differs but demonstrates price pass-through and demand elasticity.

## Source 9: Impact of the Berkeley Excise Tax on Sugar-Sweetened Beverage Consumption {cite_10.2105/ajph.2016.303362}
**Authors / Year / Venue:** Falbe et al. / 2016 / American Journal of Public Health  
**Read:** abstract only

### Research question
Did Berkeley, California's SSB tax reduce consumption?

### Methodology
- Design: Pre-post analysis with comparison communities
- Data: Cross-sectional surveys of ~1,900 residents before and after tax
- Outcome: Self-reported beverage consumption

### Key findings
1. 21% decline in SSB consumption in Berkeley
2. 63% increase in water consumption
3. No significant changes in comparison cities
4. Effects observed across demographic groups

### Implications
First US city-level evidence of consumption reductions; shows substitution to water.

### Limitations
- Self-reported consumption subject to bias
- Small geographic area with cross-border shopping possible

### Relevance to the topic
Medium - early US evidence; supports consumption reduction but limited applicability to UK national policy.

## Source 11: Regressive Sin Taxes, with an Application to the Optimal Soda Tax {cite_10.1093/qje/qjz017}
**Authors / Year / Venue:** Allcott et al. / 2019 / The Quarterly Journal of Economics  
**Read:** abstract only

### Research question
Are SSB taxes regressive and what is the optimal tax rate?

### Methodology
- Design: Economic modeling
- Data: Nielsen Homescan data, nutrition surveys
- Approach: Welfare analysis incorporating externalities and regressivity

### Key findings
1. Taxes are regressive in conventional sense (lower-income households pay larger share of income)
2. When accounting for health benefits concentrated in lower-income groups, regressivity is offset
3. Optimal tax rate approximately 1-2 cents/ounce (higher than most implemented taxes)
4. Health benefits justify tax despite regressivity concerns

### Implications
Economic analysis supports SSB taxation despite regressivity concerns; health benefits outweigh distributional costs.

### Limitations
- US context and consumption patterns
- Model assumptions about rationality and internalities

### Relevance to the topic
High - directly addresses regressivity argument Treasury will raise; provides economic welfare justification.

### Research question
How much of Berkeley's SSB tax was passed through to retail prices?

### Methodology
- Design: Pre-post price analysis
- Data: Store audits in Berkeley and comparison cities
- Outcome: Retail prices of taxed and untaxed beverages

### Key findings
1. 47% pass-through rate on average (less than 100%)

## Cross-source analysis

### Common themes

**Purchasing reductions:** Multiple UK studies {cite_10.1136/bmj.n254} {cite_10.1136/jech-2023-220944} demonstrate 6-12% reductions in taxed beverage purchases. International evidence from Mexico {cite_10.1136/bmj.h6704} and US cities {cite_10.1001/jama.2019.4249} {cite_10.2105/ajph.2016.303362} shows similar magnitudes.

**Reformulation as primary mechanism:** UK evidence {cite_10.1371/journal.pmed.1003025} {cite_10.1186/s12916-019-1477-4} {cite_10.1136/bmj-2022-072372} consistently shows reformulation (reducing sugar content) was the dominant industry response, accounting for 85%+ of sugar reduction. This occurred largely before implementation, driven by anticipatory behavior.

**Health outcomes:** Obesity reduction of ~20% in English primary school children {cite_10.1371/journal.pmed.1004160} represents strongest health outcome evidence. Newer dental health evidence {cite_10.1136/bmj-2023-078051} extends impact beyond obesity. Modeled health gains {cite_10.1016/S2468-2667(16)30037-8} {cite_10.1016/j.lanepe.2024.100993} align with empirical findings.

**Equity impacts:** UK evidence {cite_10.1136/jech-2023-220944} {cite_10.1371/journal.pmed.1004160} shows greater benefits in more deprived areas, offsetting regressivity concerns raised in economic analyses {cite_10.1093/qje/qjz017}. This distinguishes UK findings from some US evidence.

**Price pass-through:** US evidence {cite_10.1002/pam.21960} {cite_10.1001/jama.2019.4249} shows incomplete pass-through (47-100%), but UK manufacturer-level levy differs from US retail-level taxes. UK price changes modest {cite_10.1371/journal.pmed.1003025} but reformulation achieved effects without large price increases.

### Methodological trends

**Quasi-experimental designs dominate:** Controlled interrupted time series {cite_10.1136/bmj.n254} {cite_10.1371/journal.pmed.1004160} {cite_10.1371/journal.pmed.1003025} is standard design for natural experiments with population-level interventions. Difference-in-differences used in US studies {cite_10.1001/jama.2019.4249} with geographic comparison groups.

**Purchase vs consumption distinction:** All UK studies rely on purchase data (Kantar household panel) which do not measure individual consumption or dietary intake. This is acknowledged limitation across UK evidence base. US studies {cite_10.2105/ajph.2016.303362} {cite_10.1371/journal.pmed.1002283} use consumption surveys but face self-report bias.

**Health economic modeling:** Recent shift (2024-2025) toward dynamic systems modeling {cite_10.1136/jech-2024-222109} {cite_10.1016/j.lanepe.2024.101077} beyond earlier static models {cite_10.1016/S2468-2667(16)30037-8}, capturing feedback effects and long-term trajectories.

**Equity-stratified analysis:** Growing emphasis on reporting effects by socioeconomic status {cite_10.1136/jech-2023-220944} in response to regressivity concerns.

### Contradictions

**Regressivity debate:** Economic modeling {cite_10.1093/qje/qjz017} characterizes SSB taxes as regressive in conventional terms but argues health benefits offset this. UK empirical evidence {cite_10.1136/jech-2023-220944} finds greater health benefits in deprived groups, suggesting progressive *health* impacts even if tax burden is regressive. These are not direct contradictions but different framings of distributional effects.

**Pass-through rates:** US evidence shows variable pass-through (47% {cite_10.1002/pam.21960} to over 100% {cite_10.1001/jama.2019.4249}), but UK structure differs (manufacturer-level volumetric levy vs retail ad valorem tax), limiting comparability. UK studies do not emphasize pass-through given reformulation-dominated response.

**Long-term effects:** Teng meta-analysis {cite_10.1111/obr.12868} notes limited evidence on long-term effects. UK evidence now extends to 5+ years post-implementation {cite_10.1371/journal.pmed.1004160}, with sustained effects, partially addressing this concern.

### Datasets in common use

**Kantar Worldpanel:** UK household purchase data used in {cite_10.1136/bmj.n254} {cite_10.1371/journal.pmed.1003025} {cite_10.1186/s12916-019-1477-4} and other UK studies. Largest commercial panel (~30,000 households) tracking all purchases.

**National Child Measurement Programme (NCMP):** England's universal height/weight surveillance in school children, used in {cite_10.1371/journal.pmed.1004160} {cite_10.1136/jech-2024-222109}. Annual measurements of >1 million children.

**Nielsen consumer panels:** Used in Mexico study {cite_10.1136/bmj.h6704} and US economic analyses {cite_10.1093/qje/qjz017}. International purchase data.

**Hospital Episode Statistics (HES):** NHS administrative data used in dental outcomes study {cite_10.1136/bmj-2023-078051}.

## Research trajectory

Pre-2017 evidence base largely international (Mexico {cite_10.1136/bmj.h6704}, US cities {cite_10.2105/ajph.2016.303362}) and modeling UK impacts {cite_10.1016/S2468-2667(16)30037-8}. 2019-2021 saw first empirical UK evaluations focused on purchasing {cite_10.1136/bmj.n254} and reformulation {cite_10.1186/s12916-019-1477-4} {cite_10.1371/journal.pmed.1003025}, consistently showing industry response exceeded expectations. 2023 marked arrival of health outcome evidence {cite_10.1371/journal.pmed.1004160} demonstrating measurable obesity reduction in children. 2024-2025 evidence extends to dental outcomes {cite_10.1136/bmj-2023-078051}, equity impacts {cite_10.1136/jech-2023-220944}, health economics {cite_10.1016/j.lanepe.2024.100993}, and systems perspectives {cite_10.1016/j.lanepe.2024.101077}. Trajectory shows progression from anticipation → purchases → reformulation → health outcomes → long-term modeling, with increasingly strong evidence at each stage.

1. **Pell et al. 2021** {cite_10.1136/bmj.n254} - Definitive household purchasing study; most cited UK SDIL evaluation
2. **Rogers et al. 2023** {cite_10.1371/journal.pmed.1004160} - First demonstration of population health impact (obesity reduction); strongest outcome evidence
3. **Scarborough et al. 2020** {cite_10.1371/journal.pmed.1003025} - Reformulation response analysis; explains mechanism driving health effects
4. **Colchero et al. 2016** {cite_10.1136/bmj.h6704} - Key international comparator (Mexico); most similar tax structure to UK

## Gaps noticed

1. **No direct evidence on milk-based beverage taxation:** Searches found technical/formulation studies on milk beverages but no fiscal policy evaluations of sweetened milk drinks specifically. Extension to milk would be novel policy territory.

2. **Limited dental health evidence:** Only one study {cite_10.1136/bmj-2023-078051} addresses dental outcomes, using severe cases (hospital admissions) only. Broader dental caries impacts unknown.

3. **Purchase-intake translation:** All UK studies use purchase data; individual consumption and dietary intake not directly measured. Relationship between household purchases and individual intake assumed but not validated in UK context.

4. **Long-term (5+ year) health outcomes:** Rogers 2023 extends to 7 years but most evidence is shorter-term. Sustained long-term effects rely on modeling {cite_10.1136/jech-2024-222109} rather than empirical data.

5. **Substitution effects:** Limited evidence on whether consumers substitute to other sugar sources (confectionery, biscuits) when reducing SSB consumption. Some UK studies {cite_10.1136/bmj.n254} find no substitution to other drinks, but broader dietary substitution not examined.

6. **Industry economic impacts:** Little evidence on economic effects on beverage manufacturers and retailers. Jones 2025 {cite_10.1016/j.lanepe.2024.101077} notes minimal negative impacts but detailed economic analysis absent.

7. **International comparisons beyond Mexico/US:** Limited evidence from other countries with SSB taxes (e.g., France, Hungary, Portugal, South Africa). UK and Mexico dominate evidence base.
