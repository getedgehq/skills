## Research trajectory

The field appears to have developed in three phases, visible from publication years and titles:

**Phase 1 (2013-2017)**: Early experimental and survey work establishing distraction effects and cognitive mechanisms. Ward et al.'s 2017 "Brain Drain" study represents culmination of this experimental phase, isolating cognitive effects of mere smartphone presence.

**Phase 2 (2016-2021)**: Shift to quasi-experimental policy studies exploiting natural variation in phone restrictions, primarily in England and Nordic countries. Beland-Murphy 2016 becomes highly cited anchoring study. Period closes with synthesis efforts (reviews, meta-analyses 2019-2021), suggesting field reaching maturity.

**Phase 3 (2023-2026)**: Implementation and stakeholder perspective studies emerge alongside continued outcome research. Recent work incorporates mental health/wellbeing as co-equal outcome with attainment, and examines practical complexities of enforcement. This phase reflects evolution from "do bans work?" to "how do they work in practice, for whom, and at what trade-offs?"

Missing from visible trajectory: pandemic-period research (2022 gap suggests either publication delay or genuine hiatus during remote learning period when phone bans irrelevant).

## Must-read (top 5)

Based on titles, venues, and supplementary notes, five sources appear most essential:

1. **Beland & Murphy 2016** {cite_10.1016/j.labeco.2016.04.004}: Provides headline 6.41% attainment figure and quasi-experimental causal design in high-profile English context. Likely most-cited study in field.

2. **Goodyear et al. 2025 (SMART Schools)** {cite_10.1016/j.lanepe.2025.101211}: Largest recent UK study with mental wellbeing focus, in Lancet family journal. Represents state-of-evidence on wellbeing outcomes.

3. **Halvorsen & Dahl 2021** {cite_10.1016/j.econedurev.2019.101884}: Norwegian reform study with reportedly largest effect sizes in pool. Demonstrates effect robustness outside England.

4. **Ward et al. 2017** {cite_10.1086/691462}: "Brain Drain" cognitive mechanism study. Experimental evidence for why even unexposed phones may disrupt learning.

5. **Odgers & Jensen 2020** {cite_10.1111/jcpp.13190}: Annual research review situating school phone policies within broader adolescent mental health and digital technology literature. Provides necessary context for not overgeneralizing school-specific findings.
