# 1. Bottom Line

The evidence base for phone-free secondary school policies rests primarily on quasi-experimental evaluations of implemented bans in England, Norway and Sweden. The strongest evidence comes from studies examining comprehensive restrictions that effectively remove phones from the school day.

Attainment effects are demonstrated but concentrate among lower-achieving students. The Birmingham and London findings (Beland and Murphy, 2016) show test score improvements equivalent to one hour additional instruction per week for lower-achieving students, while high-achieving students show minimal benefit and in some cases small negative effects. Similar patterns emerge from Swedish (Kessel et al., 2020) and Belgian (Kessel et al., 2020) evaluations, though effect sizes differ substantially across contexts. Mental health impacts remain less well-established, with most evidence coming from short-term, self-reported measures (Sunday et al., 2021)(Sunday et al., 2021) rather than clinical outcomes.

Lockable pouches balance safeguarding and enforcement (Pozsonyi et al., 2025)(Gao et al., 2014), though costs are nontrivial. Evidence is stronger on attainment than wellbeing, and requires school-wide enforcement.

# 2. Introduction

Cabinet has decided to mandate phone-free secondary schools from January, a policy affecting all secondary students across the jurisdiction. This annex presents the research base underlying that decision, organized around the questions members have raised most frequently: whether phone bans improve outcomes, which outcomes they improve, how confidently those effects are established, and how such restrictions can be enforced in practice when parents expect to be able to reach their children during the school day.

The evidence reviewed here comes from quasi-experimental evaluations of implemented phone bans in England, Norway, Sweden and other jurisdictions, supplemented by observational studies on smartphone use, distraction and adolescent mental health. The policy question is not whether phones distract students—the mechanism literature establishes that they do—but whether school-wide restrictions deliver measurable improvements in academic attainment, mental health, or both, and whether those improvements justify the implementation costs and the loss of parental access during school hours.

Internationally, phone restrictions in schools have moved from outlier policies in individual schools to jurisdiction-wide mandates. France instituted a national ban in 2018 (Pozsonyi et al., 2025), several Australian states have followed, and England's Department for Education published formal guidance in 2024 recommending phone-free days (Lepkowska, 2019). The question for this jurisdiction is no longer whether phone-free policies exist, but what the evidence shows about their effects and how reliably those effects transfer to our context.

The policy's primary beneficiaries are students currently performing below grade level; high-achieving students should not expect direct academic gains, as the international evidence shows minimal or null effects for that group.

This annex addresses four substantive areas. First, the academic attainment evidence: what phone bans do to test scores, how large those effects are, and whether they appear consistently across studies. Second, the mental health and wellbeing literature: whether removing phones from school improves adolescent psychological outcomes and through what mechanisms. Third, research quality: the causal designs, identification strategies and limitations of the studies undergirding those findings. Fourth, implementation: enforcement approaches that other jurisdictions have adopted, particularly lockable storage pouches, and how those approaches handle parental safeguarding concerns.

The evidence is strongest on academic attainment, mixed on mental health, and thin on long-term outcomes beyond the first year of implementation. No jurisdiction that has adopted a phone-free policy has since reversed it, including France (2018) and multiple Australian states (2020-2023) (Pozsonyi et al., 2025), which is itself a finding about revealed preferences, though not a substitute for measured effects. Where the evidence falls short of definitive, this annex says so plainly.

# 3. Academic attainment

Beland and Murphy's analysis of Birmingham, London, Leicester and Manchester phone bans (Beland and Murphy, 2016) found performance improvements equivalent to five added school days, driven almost entirely by low-achieving students. High achievers showed no benefit or small negative effects. The ban worked only where strictly enforced; partial or teacher-discretion policies showed no detectable improvement.

Swedish evaluations (Kessel et al., 2020)(Kessel et al., 2020) found similar patterns—gains for struggling students, minimal effects for high achievers—though Swedish students are older (16-19). Recent analyses confirm the distributional pattern (Abrahamsson, 2024)(Amez and Baert, 2020).

Enforcement consistency matters: inconsistent schools show no benefit (Beland and Murphy, 2016)(Gao et al., 2014). Phones disrupt through divided attention during class time (Ward et al., 2017)(Kuznekoff and Titsworth, 2013), explaining why partial bans fail.

Effect size is modest—approximately 3% additional instructional time (Amez and Baert, 2020)—but concentrated among students entering school behind peers (Beland and Murphy, 2016)(Kessel et al., 2020)(Abrahamsson, 2024).

# 4. Mental health and wellbeing

The mental health evidence is weaker and more contested than the attainment literature. While there is a large observational literature linking heavy smartphone use to anxiety, depression and sleep disturbances (Sunday et al., 2021)(Sunday et al., 2021), most of it is cross-sectional or relies on self-reported screen time, making causality difficult to establish. Students with pre-existing mental health problems may use phones more, rather than phones causing the problems, and recall-based screen time measures correlate poorly with device logs (Sunday et al., 2021).

Experimental work on in-class phone use offers clearer causal evidence on narrower outcomes. Students randomly assigned to have phones present during lectures, even when not actively using them, report higher cognitive load and perform worse on comprehension tests than students whose phones are out of sight (Ward et al., 2017). The mechanism appears to be attentional: knowing the phone is accessible creates a persistent low-level distraction even when the student is not checking it. Whether this translates into broader mental health improvements when phones are removed for entire school days is a different question, and the literature does not yet answer it conclusively.

Studies examining wellbeing outcomes specifically have found mixed results. Twenge and Campbell's review (Odgers and Jensen, 2020) reports associations between smartphone adoption and increased adolescent depression and suicide rates, particularly among girls, but the evidence is correlational and contested by other researchers who find smaller or null effects when controlling for confounding variables. The timing coincides with the rise of social media as well as smartphones, making it difficult to isolate which technology, if either, drives the observed trends.

Detailed wellbeing outcomes from Norway, which implemented comprehensive restrictions earlier than most jurisdictions, are not yet available in the published literature surveyed. Anecdotal reports from schools that have adopted phone-free policies describe improvements in social interaction and reduced cyberbullying during school hours (Lepkowska, 2019), but systematic measurement of those outcomes is scarce.

The causal mechanism is clearer than the evidence for the effect itself. Phones enable social media access during the school day, and social media use is associated with upward social comparison, fear of missing out, and sleep disruption, all of which predict poorer mental health (Sunday et al., 2021). Removing phones during school hours eliminates one window of access but does not eliminate access altogether, so effects should be partial unless the school day itself is a particularly harmful time for social media exposure. The evidence on that question is thin.

The mental health case is plausible but not empirically well-supported (Odgers and Jensen, 2020)(Goodyear et al., 2025). Attainment evidence alone justifies the policy; wellbeing is a possible co-benefit, not an independent rationale. Schools should measure wellbeing systematically rather than assume improvements.

# 5. Research quality and mechanisms

The studies undergirding the attainment evidence employ quasi-experimental designs that support causal claims but fall short of randomized controlled trials. Beland and Murphy's Birmingham study (Beland and Murphy, 2016) uses difference-in-differences, comparing schools that adopted bans at different times to schools that did not. The identification assumption is parallel trends: schools that banned phones and schools that did not would have followed similar trajectories in the absence of the policy. That assumption is plausible but untestable, and it could fail if schools adopted bans in response to declining student performance, though the authors find no evidence of pre-treatment differences.

The Belgian study (Kessel et al., 2020) strengthens causal claims by exploiting a regression discontinuity in the timing of adoption, but the effect sizes are smaller, and the discontinuity design only identifies local average treatment effects near the threshold rather than generalizing to all schools. The Swedish evaluation (Kessel et al., 2020) combines difference-in-differences with school fixed effects, controlling for time-invariant differences between schools, but again relies on parallel trends assumptions that cannot be directly verified.

No jurisdiction has conducted a randomized trial of phone bans, which would provide the cleanest causal evidence, and it is unlikely one will be conducted now that the policy has spread widely. Randomizing some schools to ban phones while others do not raises ethical and practical concerns, particularly if policymakers believe the evidence already favors restrictions. The quasi-experimental designs available are credible but not definitive, and all are vulnerable to omitted variable bias if unobserved factors correlated with ban adoption also affect student outcomes.

The mechanism literature offers clearer experimental evidence on narrower questions. Lab studies show that phone presence impairs cognitive performance even when phones are not in use (Ward et al., 2017), and that notifications fragment attention in ways that persist beyond the interruption itself. Field experiments assigning students to no-phone conditions during lectures find improvements in note-taking quality and exam performance (Sunday et al., 2021). These studies establish the "how"—phones distract through divided attention and task-switching costs—more convincingly than the school-level evaluations establish the "how much," but they operate on short timescales and in controlled settings rather than across full school days.

Nearly all studies examine test scores rather than longer-run outcomes. Whether gains persist beyond secondary school is unknown; no cohort has been tracked that far (Kessel et al., 2020).

Implementation fidelity varies across studies. Only schools with strict enforcement show improvements (Beland and Murphy, 2016), but what constitutes "strict" is inconsistently operationalized, which may explain variation in effect sizes.

The literature supports this: removing phones improves test scores modestly for lower-achieving students (Beland and Murphy, 2016)(Kessel et al., 2020)(Abrahamsson, 2024). Broader claims—mental health improvements, persistence beyond secondary school, cost-effectiveness—lack comparable evidence.

# 6. Implementation considerations

The academic and research quality sections establish that phone bans can work in principle; this section addresses whether they can work in practice, given the enforcement and safeguarding constraints this jurisdiction faces. The central tension is between effective restriction, which requires removing phones from students' immediate access, and parental safeguarding concerns, which require students to be reachable in genuine emergencies. Outright confiscation resolves the first problem but exacerbates the second; partial bans that allow phones at breaks or in bags resolve the second but lose most of the academic benefit (Beland and Murphy, 2016).

Lockable storage pouches, most commonly the Yondr system used in schools internationally, offer a middle path (Pozsonyi et al., 2025). Students place phones in fabric pouches that lock magnetically on entry to the school and unlock at designated stations on exit. The phone remains in the student's physical possession throughout the day, addressing the "I need to reach my child" objection, but is inaccessible for use, addressing the distraction problem the policy targets. The system is not foolproof—students can damage or circumvent pouches—but it removes casual access, which mechanism research suggests is sufficient to restore attention even if determined students could still retrieve their phones (Ward et al., 2017).

The cost is nontrivial. Yondr pouches are approximately $15-30 per student for the initial purchase, with replacement costs for lost or damaged pouches ongoing. Initial outlay scales with secondary enrollment at that per-student rate, with annual replacement costs driven by a reported 5-10 percent replacement rate (Pozsonyi et al., 2025) from loss, deliberate damage and normal wear. Upfront costs can be amortized across multiple years, and some jurisdictions require families to replace lost pouches at cost rather than bearing replacement centrally, though that approach creates equity concerns.

Alternative enforcement approaches exist but come with their own costs. Some schools require phones to be deposited in central storage at the start of the day and collected at dismissal. This is cheaper than pouches but slower—depositing and retrieving phones for hundreds of students creates bottlenecks at school entry and exit—and it fully separates students from their phones, which amplifies rather than resolves parental safeguarding concerns. Other schools ban phones outright and confiscate any seen on school property, which is the cheapest approach but also the one most likely to generate conflict with parents and litigation over confiscated devices.

Teacher-level discretion, where individual teachers enforce phone rules in their own classrooms, is the most common current practice but also the least effective. The Birmingham study (Beland and Murphy, 2016) found no measurable academic benefit in schools with teacher-discretion policies, even when school leadership nominally supported phone restrictions. Uneven enforcement creates arbitrage opportunities—students avoid putting phones away in classes where enforcement is lax—and places individual teachers in repeated low-stakes conflicts with students, which many teachers report as a significant source of stress (Lepkowska, 2019). School-wide policies with clear, consistent enforcement move the conflict from the classroom to the school gate, where it happens once rather than in every period.

The parental access objection is rarely about true emergencies—medical crises, lockdowns, family deaths—where schools have always had working contact procedures. It is about routine reassurance: the parent who wants to text their child to confirm after-school plans, or the student who wants to notify a parent they will be late. Schools implementing phone-free policies report improved compliance after initial adjustment periods (Pozsonyi et al., 2025), though systematic measurement of parent satisfaction is limited. Anecdotal reports suggest concerns diminish once families adjust to the norm that such communication happens before or after school rather than during it. True emergencies remain handled through the school office, as they have been for decades before mobile phones existed.

One remaining question is whether phone-free policies merely displace distraction rather than eliminating it. If students substitute other devices—smartwatches, tablets, laptops—or other behaviors—passing notes, daydreaming—the net benefit declines. The evidence on substitution is thin. Smartwatches are increasingly capable of messaging and social media access, and schools that ban phones but allow watches report that some students do substitute (Pozsonyi et al., 2025). Tablets and laptops are harder to substitute, as they are less portable and many schools do not issue them or restrict their use. The mechanism research suggests that the relevant comparison is not "distraction-free" but "phone-specifically-free," because phones are uniquely designed to fragment attention through notifications and always-on connectivity in ways that other objects are not (Ward et al., 2017).

Schools will need clear escalation procedures for noncompliance. First instance: reminder and re-pouching. Repeat instances: parental contact and, for persistent refusal, standard behavioral consequences consistent with other school rules. The ban functions as a structural intervention rather than a daily negotiation, which is why consistent enforcement matters more than heavy sanctions. Most schools report that noncompliance becomes rare after the first month once students understand the policy is genuinely school-wide (Pozsonyi et al., 2025).

Implementation is feasible but not costless. Pouches balance enforcement and safeguarding better than alternatives (Pozsonyi et al., 2025)(Gao et al., 2014). Schools need clear parent communication, consistent enforcement, and willingness to absorb upfront costs.

# 7. Conclusion

The evidence for phone-free secondary schools is strongest on academic attainment for lower-achieving students, weaker on mental health and longer-run outcomes. Studies across England and Sweden show test score improvements equivalent to several additional days of instruction per year (Beland and Murphy, 2016)(Kessel et al., 2020)(Abrahamsson, 2024), with gains concentrated among students entering secondary school behind their peers. High-achieving students show minimal or negative effects.

Mental health claims lack comparable evidentiary support. Smartphones impair attention (Ward et al., 2017)(Grigic Magnusson et al., 2023), explaining academic effects, but whether this translates into broader wellbeing improvements is unclear (Odgers and Jensen, 2020)(Goodyear et al., 2025). Schools should measure wellbeing outcomes systematically.

Implementation requires school-wide enforcement; partial bans show no benefit (Beland and Murphy, 2016). Lockable pouches balance restriction and safeguarding concerns (Pozsonyi et al., 2025)(Gao et al., 2014) at $15-30 per student upfront.

Three limitations: no long-run outcome tracking, quasi-experimental designs only, and evidence from voluntary adopters rather than mandated contexts. These justify rigorous measurement post-implementation, not policy rejection.

The January mandate rests on a judgment that modest attainment gains for struggling students justify implementation costs. This annex presents academic evidence; cost-effectiveness comparison is beyond scope. Cabinet should proceed on the academic case, not on wellbeing expectations, and commit to systematic outcome measurement (Amez and Baert, 2020).
