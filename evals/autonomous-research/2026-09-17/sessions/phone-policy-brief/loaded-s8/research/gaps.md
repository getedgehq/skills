# Research gaps and opportunities

**Topic:** Phone-free secondaries — evidence base for cabinet policy annex  
**Sources analyzed:** 20 (15 abstract only, 5 metadata only)

## Executive summary

The literature provides strong quasi-experimental evidence on attainment and emerging evidence on mental health, but has a critical implementation gap: no empirical evaluation of enforcement mechanisms (lockable pouches, collection systems) or safeguarding protocols (parental emergency contact). For a cabinet annex supporting January rollout, the biggest opportunity is synthesising the existing causal evidence while explicitly naming implementation questions as open, rather than claiming the enforcement evidence exists.

## 1. Major gaps

### Gap 1: Enforcement mechanism effectiveness
**Description:** No source in the pool empirically compares lockable pouches, phone lockers, collection systems, or other enforcement methods. {cite_10.1080/07380569.2023.2211062} discusses implementation complexity qualitatively but does not evaluate which mechanisms work. This is the question school heads raise most frequently in consultations ("what does the research say about lockable pouches before I sign up").

**Why it matters:** A policy mandating phone-free schools without evidence on enforcement mechanisms leaves schools to solve the hardest implementation problem individually. Failure modes (students keeping a second phone, pouches not locked, teacher time spent policing) would undermine the policy's attainment and wellbeing goals.

**Difficulty:** Medium to high. Would require controlled comparison across schools using different methods, with fidelity monitoring.

**Ways to address:**
1. Commission rapid evaluation of schools already using pouches vs. lockers vs. teacher collection, measuring compliance rates and teacher burden.
2. Name this as an open implementation question in the annex, with commitment to evaluation within first year of rollout.

### Gap 2: Parental safeguarding concerns and emergency contact protocols
**Description:** No study addresses parent concerns about reaching their child during the school day, or compares communication protocols in phone-free vs. phone-permissive schools. This is a dominant theme in consultation responses but absent from the academic literature.

**Why it matters:** Parental resistance can undermine policy uptake and compliance. Emergency contact is a safeguarding issue that cannot be dismissed as preference. Schools need evidence-based protocols, not invented-on-the-spot compromises.

**Difficulty:** Low. Survey existing phone-free schools on their communication protocols and parent satisfaction.

**Ways to address:**
1. Rapid survey of phone-free schools on emergency contact procedures and parent feedback.
2. Include in annex as a procedural consideration requiring clear school policies, not an evidence-based objection to bans.

### Gap 3: Special educational needs and disabilities (SEND)
**Description:** No source examines whether phone restrictions affect SEND pupils differently, or whether accommodations are needed for specific disabilities (e.g., pupils who use phones for medical monitoring, assistive technology, anxiety management).

**Why it matters:** Blanket policy without SEND consideration invites legal challenge under equality law and may harm vulnerable pupils who rely on phones for legitimate support.

**Difficulty:** Medium. Requires consultation with SEND specialists and evaluation of existing accommodations.

**Ways to address:**
1. Explicitly name SEND accommodation in policy guidance (medical exceptions, assistive tech distinction from social media access).
2. State in annex that policy requires SEND-specific implementation guidance, currently under development.

### Gap 4: Sixth form (ages 16-18) differentiation
**Description:** Evidence base focuses on ages 11-16 {cite_10.1016/j.lanepe.2025.101211}, {cite_10.1016/j.labeco.2016.04.004}. {cite_10.1016/j.chb.2014.05.011} notes policies become less restrictive at higher ages, but no study evaluates optimal sixth form policy. Cabinet decision applies to all secondaries, but sixth formers have different legal status and developmental needs.

**Why it matters:** Developmental appropriateness and student buy-in differ between 13-year-olds and 17-year-olds. Uniform policy may be unnecessarily restrictive for older students, undermining the rationale.

**Difficulty:** Medium. Requires separate evaluation of sixth form outcomes.

**Ways to address:**
1. State in annex that policy applies to ages 11-16, with sixth form guidance to follow.
2. Pilot differentiated sixth form policies and evaluate within first year.

### Gap 5: Partial vs. complete ban comparison
**Description:** Studies compare phone bans {cite_10.1016/j.labeco.2016.04.004}, {cite_10.1016/j.econedurev.2019.101884} to no restrictions, but do not compare "locked away all day" vs. "switched off in bags" vs. "allowed at break." {cite_10.1086/691462} suggests complete removal is necessary for cognitive benefits, but this has not been tested in schools.

**Why it matters:** Schools will ask whether they must confiscate phones or whether "switched off in bags" suffices. Evidence gap means policy either imposes strictest option without justification or permits looser option without knowing it works.

**Difficulty:** Medium. Requires study design distinguishing policy categories and compliance measurement.

**Ways to address:**
1. Treat Ward et al.'s "brain drain" mechanism {cite_10.1086/691462} as rationale for complete removal, acknowledge school-based comparison is absent.
2. Policy specifies "not accessible during school day" and leaves collection method to schools, with evaluation planned.

### Gap 6: Cost-effectiveness and resource comparison
**Description:** No source compares cost per achievement gain for phone bans vs. other interventions (tutoring, reduced class size, extended day). {cite_10.1016/j.labeco.2016.04.004} notes phone ban is "low-cost" but does not quantify cost per effect-size unit.

**Why it matters:** Cabinet allocates limited resources. Phone ban may deliver 6.41% test score improvement {cite_10.1016/j.labeco.2016.04.004}, but if small-group tutoring delivers 15% at three times the cost, the comparison matters.

**Difficulty:** Low to medium. Cost data on enforcement mechanisms (pouches, lockers) is available; effect size comparison to other interventions exists in education literature.

**Ways to address:**
1. Include cost-effectiveness estimate in annex if pouch costs and rollout costs are known.
2. Frame phone ban as complement to other interventions, not substitution, if cost-effectiveness data unavailable.

## 2. Emerging trends

### Trend 1: Shift from correlational to quasi-experimental methods (2016-2024)
**Evidence:** 0 of 15 reviewed sources from 2013-2015 used quasi-experimental designs. From 2016 onward, 5 of 8 post-2016 sources with attainment outcomes exploit policy variation for causal inference {cite_10.1016/j.labeco.2016.04.004}, {cite_10.1016/j.econedurev.2019.101884}, {cite_10.1016/j.econedurev.2020.102009}, {cite_10.2139/ssrn.3617386}, {cite_10.2139/ssrn.4735240}.

**Key sources:** Beland & Murphy (2016) {cite_10.1016/j.labeco.2016.04.004} established the difference-in-differences approach; Nordic researchers replicated with administrative data access.

**Maturity:** Established for attainment outcomes, emerging for mental health.

**Opportunity:** Strong causal evidence base exists for attainment claims in annex. Mental health evidence is newer and more limited (1 cross-sectional UK study {cite_10.1016/j.lanepe.2025.101211}, 1 Norwegian registers study {cite_10.2139/ssrn.4735240}), so claims must be more cautious.

### Trend 2: Mental health outcomes entering the evidence base (2023-2025)
**Evidence:** 0 of 12 sources published before 2023 measure mental health outcomes directly. 2 of 3 sources from 2023-2025 include wellbeing or mental health measures {cite_10.1016/j.lanepe.2025.101211}, {cite_10.2139/ssrn.4735240}.

**Key sources:** Goodyear et al. (2025) {cite_10.1016/j.lanepe.2025.101211} is the first large UK study linking school phone policies to mental wellbeing; Abrahamsson (2024) {cite_10.2139/ssrn.4735240} bridges attainment and mental health in Norwegian data.

**Maturity:** Emerging. Sample size is still limited and causal identification weaker than for attainment.

**Opportunity:** Annex can cite mental health benefits but must note evidence is recent and less robust than attainment findings. This is the direction the field is moving, so policy is slightly ahead of the evidence strength.

### Trend 3: Implementation research emerging (2023)
**Evidence:** 1 of 15 reviewed sources addresses implementation challenges {cite_10.1080/07380569.2023.2211062}, published 2023. 0 sources before 2023 examine enforcement or teacher experience.

**Maturity:** Emerging. Enough schools now have bans that implementation can be studied, but literature is thin.

**Opportunity:** Gap 1 (enforcement mechanisms) reflects that implementation research is just beginning. Annex should acknowledge this and frame January rollout as requiring implementation guidance informed by early adopters, not by published research.

## 3. Contradictions

### Debate 1: General technology use vs. school-specific phone restrictions
**Position A:** {cite_10.1038/s41562-018-0506-1} and {cite_10.1111/jcpp.13190} find tiny effect sizes (under 1% of variance) for general adolescent technology use on wellbeing, describing effects as smaller than "wearing glasses or eating potatoes." They emphasise context-dependence and argue most research overstates impact.

**Position B:** {cite_10.1016/j.lanepe.2025.101211} and {cite_10.2139/ssrn.4735240} find meaningful mental health improvements from school phone restrictions specifically.

**Why unresolved:** Position A papers (2019-2020) predate the phone ban studies (2024-2025) and measure general technology use, not school-day interventions. They may be addressing different phenomena: population-level screen time vs. intensive school-day phone access. Or Position A's small effects reflect that most adolescents are not harmed by technology, while school bans disproportionately help the vulnerable tail.

**Study design to resolve:** Heterogeneity analysis within a school phone ban study, testing whether mental health improvements concentrate among high baseline phone users, vulnerable pupils, or spread across the distribution. If effects are uniform, Position B's findings challenge Position A's framework. If effects concentrate in the tail, both can be correct.

### Debate 2: Evidence strength and policy caution
**Position A (Implicit in review literature):** {cite_10.1038/s41562-018-0506-1}, {cite_10.1111/jcpp.13190}, {cite_10.1016/j.ijer.2020.101618} call for evidence-based policy, caution against moral panic, and note that most effects are small and context-dependent.

**Position B (Implicit in quasi-experimental studies):** {cite_10.1016/j.labeco.2016.04.004}, {cite_10.1016/j.econedurev.2019.101884}, {cite_10.2139/ssrn.4735240} find consistent, policy-relevant effect sizes across multiple countries and designs, suggesting evidence is strong enough to act on.

**Why unresolved:** This is partly a methodological disagreement (correlational reviews vs. causal policy studies) and partly a values question (how strong must evidence be before mandating school policy?). Position A reviews predate the strongest quasi-experimental work, so they are not directly refuting Position B's findings.

**Implication for annex:** Cabinet has decided to proceed, so the annex's role is to present the evidence honestly, including that some researchers urge caution while the quasi-experimental studies support the direction. The annex should not pretend the debate does not exist, but also should not overstate Position A's applicability to school phone bans specifically (as opposed to general technology panic).

## 4. Methodological opportunities

**Underused methods:**
- **Implementation science frameworks:** Used in 0 of 15 reviewed sources. {cite_10.1080/07380569.2023.2211062} is qualitative but does not employ implementation science taxonomy (fidelity, reach, adoption). Phone ban rollout is an implementation question as much as an effectiveness question.
- **Cost-effectiveness analysis:** Used in 0 of 15 sources despite policy relevance. One sentence in {cite_10.1016/j.labeco.2016.04.004} calls phone ban "low-cost" but does not compute cost per effect size.
- **Mediation analysis:** {cite_10.1086/691462} proposes cognitive mechanism, {cite_10.1016/j.lanepe.2025.101211} measures social media use as potential mediator, but no study explicitly tests mediation pathways (does phone ban improve attainment *through* reduced distraction? *through* better sleep? *through* less social comparison?).

**Unexplored datasets:**
- **Schools already using pouches or lockers:** Early adopters provide natural variation for Gap 1 (enforcement mechanisms). No study has exploited this.
- **Consultation response data:** Parent and teacher concerns are data, not just noise. No study analyses stakeholder objections systematically.

**Novel combinations:**
- Apply {cite_10.1086/691462} Ward et al.'s "brain drain" experimental paradigm to classroom setting: do students perform better on in-class tasks when phones are locked in pouches vs. switched off in bags vs. in lockers outside the room? Tests Gap 5 (partial vs. complete ban) directly.
- Combine Goodyear et al.'s mental health measures {cite_10.1016/j.lanepe.2025.101211} with Beland & Murphy's quasi-experimental design {cite_10.1016/j.labeco.2016.04.004}: run a difference-in-differences study using administrative attainment data *plus* repeated wellbeing surveys. Would provide causal evidence on mental health, not just association.

## 5. Interdisciplinary bridges

**Implementation science → education policy:** Implementation science has frameworks for measuring fidelity, reach, and adoption {not in pool, but well-established in health policy}. These could be applied to phone ban rollout to measure whether policy-as-written becomes policy-as-practiced. {cite_10.1080/07380569.2023.2211062} shows implementation varies but does not measure fidelity systematically.

**Behavioural economics → enforcement design:** Behavioural nudges (default options, commitment devices) could inform enforcement mechanisms. Lockable pouches where students lock their own phones may increase buy-in compared to teacher collection. No source in pool draws on this literature.

**Cognitive psychology → classroom practice:** {cite_10.1086/691462} is cognitive psychology, not education research, but directly informs classroom phone policy. Further bridge: working memory research could predict which lessons benefit most from phone removal (complex problem-solving vs. rote practice).

## 6. Replication and extension

**Findings worth replicating:**
- {cite_10.1016/j.labeco.2016.04.004} Beland & Murphy's 6.41% test score improvement: Single UK study, now widely cited in policy. Needs replication in other UK regions or other countries to confirm generalisability.
- {cite_10.1086/691462} Ward et al.'s "brain drain" effect in classroom setting: Laboratory findings should be tested in schools with actual lessons, not just cognitive tasks.

**Studies worth extending:**
- {cite_10.1016/j.lanepe.2025.101211} Goodyear et al. to longitudinal design: Cross-sectional association between phone policies and wellbeing needs within-student change measurement (same students before and after policy introduction).
- {cite_10.1016/j.econedurev.2019.101884} Halvorsen & Dahl's Norwegian reform to UK context: Strongest effects in the pool, but Norway-specific. Do the same effect sizes appear in English reform context?

## 7. Temporal gaps

**Recent developments not yet in literature:**
- **SMART Schools study (2025):** {cite_10.1016/j.lanepe.2025.101211} is the newest UK mental health evidence, published months ago. No subsequent work has built on it yet, so mental health claims in the annex are at the frontier rather than consensus.
- **Lockable pouch vendors and school adoption:** Anecdotal reports suggest pouch use is spreading rapidly in UK schools (2023-2024), but no academic study has documented uptake rates or evaluated effectiveness. Literature lags practice by at least two years.
- **Post-pandemic phone use patterns:** Sources published before 2020 {cite_10.1016/j.chb.2014.05.011}, {cite_10.1080/03634523.2013.767917} predate pandemic-era changes to adolescent phone use. Whether pre-pandemic findings hold is unknown.

**Obsolete assumptions:**
- Early sources {cite_10.1016/j.chb.2014.05.011} discuss whether phones can be used for learning, weighing educational benefits vs. distraction. Current debate has moved on: social media access during school day is now the primary concern, not calculator apps. Literature has not caught up to this framing shift.

## 8. Novel research angles (3)

### Angle 1: Enforcement mechanism effectiveness study
**Gaps addressed:** Gap 1 (enforcement), Gap 5 (partial vs. complete ban)

**What's novel:** First empirical comparison of lockable pouches, lockers, and teacher collection systems on compliance, teacher burden, and student outcomes.

**Why promising:** School heads' most frequent question; rapid survey of early adopters is feasible before January rollout.

**Feasibility:** High. Observational comparison of existing schools using different methods, with phone use tracking and teacher surveys. No randomisation required.

**Proposed approach:**
1. Identify 30 phone-free schools using pouches (10), lockers (10), teacher collection (10)
2. Survey teachers on time burden, compliance rates, and student pushback
3. Measure student phone use during school day (self-report or tracking app data where consented)
4. Compare attainment data if available (secondary outcome)
5. Produce rapid evidence note for schools implementing policy in January

**Expected contribution:** Fills largest implementation gap; provides practical guidance for schools; identifies which mechanisms work.

### Angle 2: Parental safeguarding protocol evaluation
**Gaps addressed:** Gap 2 (parental communication), stakeholder concerns

**What's novel:** First study addressing parent emergency contact concerns systematically rather than dismissing them as preference.

**Why promising:** Low-cost survey; addresses most common consultation objection; produces implementable protocols for schools.

**Feasibility:** Very high. Survey existing phone-free schools on emergency contact procedures, parent satisfaction, and frequency of actual emergencies requiring phone contact.

**Proposed approach:**
1. Survey 50 phone-free schools on emergency contact protocols (office phone, email, parent-teacher communication)
2. Measure parent satisfaction and frequency of emergency contact requests
3. Document range of approaches (some schools allow office calls, some have parent emergency line, some prohibit all contact)
4. Identify best practice and common failure modes
5. Produce guidance for schools on implementing safeguarding-compliant communication protocols

**Expected contribution:** Removes parental resistance based on evidence that emergency contact systems work; provides schools with tested protocols.

### Angle 3: SEND accommodation framework
**Gaps addressed:** Gap 3 (SEND pupils), legal compliance

**What's novel:** First study examining phone ban impacts on SEND pupils and developing accommodation framework.

**Why promising:** Legal necessity; affects vulnerable pupils; prevents policy failure from unintended consequences.

**Feasibility:** Medium. Requires SEND specialist input and consultation with schools that have SEND populations.

**Proposed approach:**
1. Consult SEND specialists on legitimate phone uses for disabled pupils (medical devices, assistive technology, anxiety management)
2. Survey phone-free schools with SEND pupils on accommodations made and challenges encountered
3. Develop decision framework distinguishing medical/assistive exceptions from social media access
4. Pilot framework in 10 schools with diverse SEND populations
5. Include accommodation guidance in policy rollout materials

**Expected contribution:** Ensures policy is lawful under equality legislation; protects vulnerable pupils; provides schools with clear accommodation criteria.

## 9. Risk assessment

**Low-risk opportunities (incremental, established methods):**
- Angle 1 (enforcement mechanism study): Observational comparison using existing variation, established survey methods, rapid turnaround. Main risk is recruitment of enough schools using each mechanism.
- Angle 2 (parental protocol evaluation): Survey-based, non-controversial, directly applicable. Very low risk.
- Synthesising existing quasi-experimental evidence for annex: Evidence base is strong {cite_10.1016/j.labeco.2016.04.004}, {cite_10.1016/j.econedurev.2019.101884}, findings replicate across countries. Low risk to cite this as supporting policy.

**Medium-risk opportunities:**
- Angle 3 (SEND framework): Requires specialist input, consultation time, and pilot testing. Risk is that framework is too restrictive (harms SEND pupils) or too permissive (undermines policy). Mitigation: involve SEND specialists and disability rights groups from the start.
- Claiming mental health benefits in annex: Evidence is newer {cite_10.1016/j.lanepe.2025.101211}, {cite_10.2139/ssrn.4735240} and one UK study is cross-sectional. Risk is overstating certainty. Mitigation: cite as "emerging evidence" or "associated with" rather than "proven to improve."

**High-risk/high-reward opportunities:**
- Randomised controlled trial of phone ban: Would provide gold-standard causal evidence but faces ethical issues (can you randomise schools to keep phones when evidence suggests bans help?), long timeline (2-3 years), and high cost. Not feasible for January rollout, possibly useful for evaluation afterward.
- Mediation analysis testing mechanisms: Requires detailed data on potential mediators (distraction, sleep, social media use, cognitive capacity) collected before and after policy change. High inferential value but complex design. Medium-term opportunity (years 2-3 of rollout).

## 10. Recommended next actions

1. **Write attainment section around three strong quasi-experimental studies:** {cite_10.1016/j.labeco.2016.04.004} for 6.41% figure and equity effects, {cite_10.1016/j.econedurev.2019.101884} for largest effect sizes and scalability, {cite_10.2139/ssrn.4735240} for Norwegian causal evidence. Lead with Beland & Murphy as it is UK-based and most cited.

2. **Write mental health section cautiously around Goodyear et al. (2025):** {cite_10.1016/j.lanepe.2025.101211} is the UK evidence but cross-sectional. Support with Abrahamsson's Norwegian registers {cite_10.2139/ssrn.4735240}. Use language like "emerging evidence suggests" and "associated with" rather than "proven."

3. **Frame implementation section around open questions, not settled evidence:** Gaps 1 (enforcement), 2 (parental contact), 3 (SEND) are all absent from literature. Annex must say "research on enforcement mechanisms is limited; schools will need clear guidance informed by early adopters" rather than citing non-existent evidence.

4. **Include Ward et al. (2017) "brain drain" as mechanism explanation:** {cite_10.1086/691462} provides cognitive rationale for complete phone removal vs. switched-off-in-bags. Useful for explaining why policy should require phones to be inaccessible, not just switched off.

5. **Name the technology-wellbeing debate explicitly:** One short paragraph acknowledging that {cite_10.1038/s41562-018-0506-1} and {cite_10.1111/jcpp.13190} found small general effects, but school-specific phone bans show larger impacts {cite_10.1016/j.lanepe.2025.101211}, {cite_10.2139/ssrn.4735240}, possibly because school-day access is concentrated exposure or because vulnerable pupils benefit most. Do not pretend the debate does not exist; demonstrate awareness and move on.

6. **Plan word budget carefully:** 2,000-word annex must cover attainment (600-700 words), mental health (400-500 words), implementation (500-600 words), and conclusion (200-300 words). Bottom Line box is 150 words and separate. Cannot afford tangents or methodological detail; focus on findings and their policy relevance.

7. **Verify all DOIs before compiling:** Legal bounced last annex for untraceable references. Run `citations.py verify` and `compile` before delivery; exit nonzero on any unresolved DOI.

8. **Consider rapid evidence notes for Angles 1 and 2 alongside annex:** If timeline allows, commission enforcement mechanism survey and parental protocol survey to run in parallel with annex drafting. Even preliminary findings (N=15 schools) would strengthen implementation section. If not feasible by Tuesday, name as follow-up actions in annex.

## Confidence

**Gap analysis confidence:** Medium to high. 15 sources read at abstract level plus 5 metadata-only is enough to identify major gaps, and the gaps (enforcement, parental contact, SEND) are glaringly absent across the pool. Lower confidence on whether we missed a domain-critical gap outside the pool (e.g., a recent enforcement study published after our search).

**Trend confidence:** Medium. Dated trend claims (0 pre-2023 sources on implementation, 2 of 3 post-2023 sources on mental health) are backed by counts from the pool. Confidence limited by pool size (20 sources) and read depth (abstract only). A full-text review might reveal more nuance.

**Novel-angle viability:** High for Angles 1 and 2 (enforcement and parental protocols), medium for Angle 3 (SEND framework). Angles 1 and 2 are low-cost surveys of existing practice, feasible within months. Angle 3 requires specialist consultation and pilot, feasible within a year. All three address real gaps that no source in the pool has filled.
