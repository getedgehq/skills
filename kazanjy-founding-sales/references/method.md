# The Founding Sales Method

Based on Peter Kazanjy's public method. Not affiliated with or endorsed by Peter Kazanjy.

## Reading map and attribution

Use sections 1 and 2 for stage or targeting questions, sections 3 and 4 for customer-facing assets, section 5 for diagnosis, and section 6 for hiring and scale. Section 7 covers exceptions. Sources are registered in [sources.md](sources.md).

Kazanjy's public method treats founder-led sales as a sequence of experiments that reduce uncertainty about a business. The founder's proximity to the problem and solution makes them the initial owner of learning how to sell. A successful motion includes getting customers to value, not merely collecting signatures. [S1] [S2] [S3]

The packet structure, evidence labels, experiment format, and asset layouts below are this package's operational conventions. They make the method executable and reviewable without presenting an invented template as the expert's own prescribed format.

## 1. Diagnose maturity

Assess demonstrated behavior rather than fundraising stage, calendar age, or the founder's confidence. Find the earliest unresolved question. Later-stage activity can continue, but it cannot substitute for missing earlier proof. [S2]

| Stage | Question | Relevant evidence | Next work |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Problem discovery | What problem should we solve, and how? | Interviews describing pain, current approaches, and willingness to pay; a coherent initial solution | Interview across relevant organizations and personas, then narrow the product hypothesis |
| Value creation | Does the solution improve the intended result? | Implemented customers with measured improvement in the relevant business indicators | Onboard suitable beta customers, instrument outcomes, and validate with them |
| Value exchange | Will a customer pay for that improvement? | Payment, completed procurement, and subsequent successful implementation | Present a business case, ask for money, and carry the agreement into delivery |
| Repeated exchange | Can the founder repeat this beyond friendly beta users? | Multiple suitable paying customers, documented execution, and delivered value | Repeat prospecting through onboarding, recording resonance and failure modes |
| Seller transfer | Can another person execute the motion? | Non-founder sellers independently progressing and closing suitable accounts that succeed | Hire a small number, train, inspect, coach, and compare performance |
| Cohesive revenue unit | Can the functions work together predictably? | Balanced prospecting, selling, onboarding, and retention with reliable handoffs | Establish a complete operating unit and its management cadence |
| Initial team maximization | Can more units operate under one management system? | Predictable production and effective orchestration across the team | Refine management, tooling, and analytics before adding complexity |
| Management transfer and scale | Can managers reproduce successful teams? | Productive, utilized sellers, functioning management, and sustainable economics | Replicate the managerial playbook while monitoring constraints |

The stage sequence and evidence categories come from the public presentation. [S2]

Its repeated-exchange example involves 30 to 50 or more non-beta prospects and approximately 10 to 20 successful paying customers. Treat these as contextual reference ranges, not a statistically validated universal gate. Its staffing ratios, ramp assumptions, and booking targets are likewise illustrations. The practical decision is whether there is sufficient demonstrated repeatability and a documented motion for another person to test. [S2]

**Package rule:** Record the evidence for each relevant stage as observed, reported, hypothesized, or unknown. Select a next experiment with an owner, target segment, proposed action, review date, and observable exit condition. If the condition fails, identify the uncertainty to revisit rather than silently relabeling the stage as complete.

## 2. Define fit and timing

An ideal customer profile describes both the organization and the people involved in a transaction. Industry alone is insufficient. Organizational size, number of relevant users, existing processes, and observable indicators of the problem help identify a narrow initial market. Interview different sizes and personas before deciding where the unmet need is strongest. A narrow entry market can focus engineering effort and improve the relevance of references and word of mouth. [S3]

Distinguish the user, the person advocating change, the operational decision maker, and the person releasing budget. They may be the same individual, but do not assume they are. Research public organizational and attendee facts before the call; spend live time investigating matters that require their account of the situation. [S3]

Fit and timing are different. A suitable account can lack an immediate reason to act. A genuine event, such as rapid hiring that exposes management deficiencies, can move an existing pain up the buyer's priorities. An attractive logo is not evidence of that event or of urgency. [S3]

**Package rule:** Qualify accounts with observable inclusion conditions, exclusions, pain evidence, relevant contacts, and a timing hypothesis. Label public signals as clues until discovery confirms the pain. Use a manageable prospect batch sized to founder capacity and sales-cycle length; the package imposes no fixed batch size.

## 3. Build the value argument

Buyers need to understand why their future state will be materially better than their present state. That argument has business and personal dimensions. A manager may care about team output, but also about succeeding in their role. Discover those stakes rather than inventing emotional pressure. [S3]

Build the commercial argument around the current process, unmet need, consequences, limitations of present solutions, and the mechanics of the proposed improvement. Evidence must connect the product promise to actual customer outcomes. For an existing category, replacing an inadequate tool may draw on an established budget. For a new category, the seller often needs to expose a cost that is currently carried as labor or lost opportunity. [S2] [S3]

A new-category business case should make that hidden cost visible and usable by a champion with the economic buyer. Customer studies or a bounded deployment can support confidence, but estimates should not become exaggerated financial claims. Kazanjy specifically emphasizes a credible economic case rather than convenient sales arithmetic. [S3]

**Package calculation rules:** Retain the baseline, units, time period, price, adoption assumptions, and measurement source. Distinguish released capacity from cash savings. Multiply hours saved by a labor rate only as a capacity-value estimate unless a credible staffing or expenditure change converts it into cash. Avoid double counting labor savings and revenue increases from the same time. Include relevant implementation burden and uncertainty. These are accuracy conventions that operationalize the interview's demand for a defensible business case.

**Package asset rules:** Draft the central argument first, then adapt it to the requested medium. An outreach draft should connect a verified fact or clearly stated hypothesis to a likely pain and ask for a conversation. A presentation outline can cover current state, consequences, alternatives, proposed change, evidence, price, and next step. A demo plan should select a workflow that makes the claimed improvement observable. These layouts are package recommendations, not source-prescribed email or deck formulas.

## 4. Run the conversation

Pre-call planning includes the organization's fit, the attendee's role, the desired meeting outcome, and researched rapport. Kazanjy contrasts preparation with generic small talk and discovery that merely collects easily available facts. Preparation should enable a better conversation, not establish false certainty about a prospect. [S3]

Discovery has two jobs: learning the current state and helping the prospect recognize the consequences of that state. Start with how the relevant work happens today. Follow answers into a specific failure, its downstream effect, cost, and priority. Prepare branches rather than reading every question in a rigid list. In customer development, ask about the process and problem before presenting a mockup or asking for product reactions. [S3]

Examples of package branches:

- **Current tool works well:** Ask where it falls short, if anywhere. If there is no meaningful gap, do not manufacture one.
- **Manual workaround:** Ask who performs it, how often, and what happens when the work is delayed or omitted.
- **Pain acknowledged but low priority:** Ask what would change its priority and whether a real event is expected.
- **Champion lacks budget authority:** Ask how decisions are made and what evidence the economic buyer would need.

After discovery, summarize the acknowledged problem and frame the solution before displaying product screens. Demonstrate only what is relevant to the discovered need. If discovery consumes the meeting, obtain agreement on the problem and schedule a specific presentation rather than rushing an unexplained screen tour. [S3]

Ask for a commitment suited to the maturity stage. The presentation's value-exchange stage requires agreement about the expected result, movement through procurement, payment, and implementation. A friendly conversation is not equivalent to that exchange. [S2]

## 5. Record and diagnose results

High quantities of high-quality selling behavior provide repetitions for learning. Instrument first meetings, subsequent meetings, opportunity progression, proposals, and wins. Founder capacity also includes appointment setting, procurement, and customer success; full-time seller activity examples should not become mandatory founder quotas. [S3]

The public presentation includes CRM records, qualification, messaging, pricing, implementation checklists, and a documented process among the tools for repeatable sales. Record enough information that temporary call knowledge can support future execution and coaching. [S2]

**Package record:** Capture account fit, meeting date, narrative version, problem acknowledged, current solution, buyer roles, objections, opportunity value, agreed action, owner, and due date. After purchase, add implementation progress, baseline, outcome measure, and customer validation.

Diagnose where the process breaks rather than changing everything at once. The following are working hypotheses, not automatic conclusions. [S3]

| Observation | Initial hypothesis | Evidence to inspect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Most prospects lack the pain | Targeting or problem hypothesis | Interview answers by organization type and persona |
| Suitable buyers acknowledge pain but do not progress | Discovery, explanation, priority, or buying process | Call responses, misunderstood claims, next-step requests, authority |
| Sales close but adoption or results disappoint | Product, implementation, or expectation mismatch | Usage, onboarding completion, promised and measured outcomes |
| Outcomes are strong but wins remain weak | Commercial explanation or presentation | How the same successful use case is described to prospects |
| Trained sellers lag despite comparable opportunities | Execution, training, supervision, or transfer weakness | Call quality, process adherence, lead comparability, ramp period |

The fit, explanation, and product distinction is explicit in the interview; the added inspection fields are package conventions. [S3]

Sparse data still helps. Record qualitative engagement and concrete progression, but do not mistake nodding or enthusiasm for purchase intent. Always retain numerator, denominator, observation period, and segment when reporting conversion. Review a focused change across additional comparable conversations; avoid claiming causality from a handful of calls. [S3]

## 6. Transfer and scale

Once the founder has documented a minimally viable motion, the next experiment is whether one or two additional sellers can execute it. Their job includes improving the motion, but they should not be expected to invent the initial recipe in the founder's absence. The founder becomes a player-coach who trains, inspects, and corrects execution. [S2] [S3]

For first sellers, prioritize comfort with early product selling. Relevant stage experience, buyer-persona familiarity, and sales-motion complexity matter. A seller accustomed to short, simple transactions may be poorly matched to complex technical buying. Seniority at a large established company does not itself establish readiness for an incomplete early-stage environment. [S3]

**Package hiring and training rules:** Define the work to reproduce, evaluate a realistic call or account scenario, and ask the candidate to respond to feedback. Build training around customer context, the narrative, product, tools, qualification, procurement, and implementation handoffs. Use rehearsal and supported execution, then remove support as evidence warrants. These practices implement the presentation's hiring, onboarding, coaching, and inspection requirements; the exact exercise and curriculum are package choices. [S2]

Judge transfer by seller efficiency and suitable customers reaching success, not by headcount or a founder rescuing every close. Then prove a balanced unit across prospecting, selling, and success. Leadership handoff becomes reasonable when there is a functioning unit and managerial process to transfer. Monitor opportunity supply, ramp costs, productivity, utilization, handoffs, and economics as complexity increases. [S2]

## 7. Edge cases and explicit warnings

### Evidence and operating exceptions

- **Free pilots:** They can help prove value creation. They cannot prove payment. A paid deal also does not prove successful delivery by itself. [S2]
- **Friendly early buyers:** Useful for initial learning, but validate repeated exchange with non-beta prospects. The presentation warns that selling within friendly founder circles can create misleading traction. [S2]
- **New category:** Expect more education and potentially lower initial win rates or multiple trips through the funnel. Maintain contact with qualified pain-bearing prospects whose timing is wrong. Support successful early advocates and make their outcomes visible. [S3]
- **Product-led entry:** A self-serve account or assisted trial can help demonstrate value. It does not erase the separate questions of business outcome, payment, or transfer. [S2] [S3]
- **Limited founder time:** Appointment-setting or customer-success help may increase founder selling repetitions while the founder still owns discovery of the motion. Such support differs from outsourcing initial sales development to a leader. [S2]
- **Incomplete evidence for a narrow asset:** Produce a usable draft with explicit hypotheses and a validation plan. Do not require a whole sales transformation before answering a limited request. This is a package scope rule.

### What Kazanjy explicitly warns against

Do not build features without understanding pain and current solutions. Do not assume the product will sell itself. Do not hire a seller or VP to establish the earliest proof of value and repeatability. Do not count numerous acquired customers as success when few reach value. Do not hire too many sellers before proving transfer, or remain so occupied closing personally that the first sellers receive inadequate coaching. Do not scale selling capacity out of balance with prospecting and customer success. Do not hand off or replicate an unproven management system. These warnings appear in the public presentation. [S2]

The interview additionally warns against leading customer-development conversations with a product pitch, treating discovery as a static fact-gathering checklist, relying on generic rapport, showing screens without framing the value, and choosing first sellers whose stage or motion experience does not fit. It also stresses making a credible case to the budget authority. [S3]
