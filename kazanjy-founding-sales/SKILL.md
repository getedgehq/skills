# Founding Sales for Founder-Led B2B Selling

Based on Peter Kazanjy's public method. Not affiliated with or endorsed by Peter Kazanjy. Sources: [references/sources.md](references/sources.md).

## Output contract

Read this first. It decides what the user receives.

- **The deliverable is the thing they asked for, ready to use:** a sales plan, a playbook, an email, a call plan, a demo outline, a hiring plan. Not a "sales working packet", not a report about the method.
- **Length and format:** match the request and what a strong founder-operator would hand a peer. A 90-day plan or playbook fits on one to two pages (roughly 500 to 1,000 words); an email is under 120 words; a call plan fits on one page. Keep the format the user asked for. If you save a file, the chat reply is two or three lines pointing to it, not a second copy.
- **What the method changes:** the decisions inside the deliverable, not its length. Kazanjy's method shows up as: the plan targets the earliest unproven proof (problem, value, payment, repeatability, transfer) for this company; the target customer is narrow and observable; discovery comes before demo; each step asks for a concrete commitment; success counts customers reaching value, not signatures; no hiring a seller to invent the motion.
- **Facts:** use only facts from the request and the files provided. Never invent product features, customers, pilots, prices, conversion rates, quotas, market sizes or named people. If a fact the plan depends on is missing, write the step so it works either way; avoid [placeholders] except in an email's personalization slot. Numeric targets you propose are your recommendation; say so in a few words, never present them as benchmarks.
- **Never output:** a recap of the framework or its stage table, evidence labels (observed/reported/hypothesized), assumption registers, "what I know vs. what I don't" tables, validation plans, review checklists or their results, source tags like [S2], or the expert's name used as decoration. Do the method's thinking silently; show only its conclusions.
- **Open questions:** at most three, at the end, only if the answer would change the plan. Otherwise none.

## The method, as decisions

Use these to make the choices in the deliverable. Detail and edge cases are in [references/method.md](references/method.md).

1. **Aim at the earliest missing proof.** Founder-led sales is a sequence of experiments: problem discovery, value created for a customer, value exchanged for money, the exchange repeated beyond friendly beta users, then another seller repeating it. Funding, enthusiastic pilots and logos do not prove a stage. Pick the stage from the facts given and make the plan's milestones about closing that gap; if the stage is unknown, sequence the plan so the first weeks establish it while still selling. [S2]
2. **Narrow, observable customer.** Define fit by organization conditions (size, how many people do the relevant work, current tools, visible signs of the pain) and by people: user, champion, budget owner. Industry alone is not a fit test. Fit is not timing; a trigger event (hiring surge, new leader, failed tool) is what makes it urgent. [S3]
3. **One commercial argument, reused everywhere.** Current workflow, what it costs, why today's alternatives fall short, how the product changes it, the proof. Emails, decks and demos all derive from it. In a new category, make the hidden cost (labor, lost opportunity) visible so a champion can defend it to the budget owner. Keep economics honest: released time is capacity, not cash, unless spending actually changes. [S3]
4. **Discovery before demo.** Open with how the work happens today, follow the answer into a specific recent failure, its consequence and priority. Branch on what you hear; never manufacture pain when the current tool works. Summarize the problem the buyer confirmed, then show only the workflow that addresses it. [S3]
5. **Ask for the next real commitment.** Every meeting ends with a specific next step suited to the stage: a scoped evaluation, a paid pilot, a procurement step, a purchase. Enthusiasm is not progress. [S2]
6. **A sale is done when the customer gets value.** Carry the deal into implementation with a baseline and an outcome the customer confirms. Free pilots can prove value but not willingness to pay; payment does not prove delivery. [S2]
7. **Write things down so the motion can be taught.** A simple record per deal (pain, roles, objections, next step, outcome) is what later lets someone else sell. [S2]
8. **Transfer only a proven motion.** Do not hire a VP or seller to discover how to sell. Once the founder has repeated sales that succeed, test one or two sellers comfortable with early products, with the founder as player-coach. [S2] [S3]

## Shapes that work

- **Sales plan (30/60/90 or by week):** the goal for the period in one line; phases with concrete actions, weekly volumes the founder can actually sustain, and the exit signal for each phase; the core message; the discovery questions; what to track; the decision at day 90.
- **Playbook:** who to sell to and how to spot them; the commercial argument; outreach; discovery questions with branches; demo flow; commitment asks per stage; deal record fields; when to hire.
- **Email or call:** see the short skeletons in [templates/output-template.md](templates/output-template.md).

Worked illustrations are in [examples/worked-examples.md](examples/worked-examples.md); they show the reasoning, not the format to hand over.

## Before you hand it over (silent)

Check, without writing the results down: every fact traces to the request or input; no invented features, customers or rates; nothing the user must delete before using it (framework recap, labels, process notes); length is no longer than the job needs; the plan names a concrete next action for this week. Fuller criteria: [checklists/review-checklist.md](checklists/review-checklist.md).
