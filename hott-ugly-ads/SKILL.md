# Barry Hott's Ugly Ads

Based on Barry Hott's public method. Not affiliated with or endorsed by Barry Hott.

## When to use

Use this skill when asked to develop, revise, brief, or diagnose paid social creative for a scrolling feed, especially Instagram, Facebook, TikTok, or short video placements, and the work needs to feel like content the intended audience already consumes. [S1][S3][S7]

Apply the same reasoning to premium products, but calibrate the degree of roughness to the product and placement instead of assuming that an expensive offer requires a studio ad. [S1][S3]

For television, YouTube, or another placement where the audience expects polished production, choose the style that fits that placement; Hott explicitly allows beautiful, high production advertising there. [S3]

## Procedure

### 1. Define the viewing moment

1. Write down the specific person, their situation, the placement, the offer, and the action the ad should prompt before choosing a visual format. [S1][S3][S4]
2. Assume a cold viewer does not know the brand, has little time, and will care only if the opening is immediately about something in their own life. [S8]
3. State the human moment in one sentence, such as an irritation, hope, result, or small everyday scene that the audience would recognize without product knowledge. [S4][S8]
4. If the brief starts with an interface screenshot, feature list, or product demonstration, translate it into the person's problem or experienced change first; introduce the product as the answer to that moment. [S4]
5. Reject a concept whose first frame merely announces the company or shows a handsome product unless a cold viewer can instantly see why it matters to them. [S4][S8]

### 2. Learn the audience's feed language

1. Spend focused time in the organic feeds the audience actually watches, including relevant TikTok, Reels, or Shorts communities; save examples and notes before drafting ads. [S7]
2. Record the observable grammar of those examples: who speaks, camera distance, lighting, edit pace, captions, setting, opening move, and what makes the content feel natural in that feed. [S2][S7]
3. Capture the audience's own descriptions of frustrations and wins, then select a real human moment as the ad's premise rather than treating an unpolished look as the premise. [S4][S7]
4. Use organic content as the primary style reference when competitors' ads or an internal mood board would produce another recognizable ad. [S2][S7]
5. If AI helps generate ideas or variants, first provide it the current audience research and saved examples; do not let a generic prompt stand in for watching what people consume. [S2][S7]
6. Match the content mode the audience already accepts: human made content for a feed of human made posts, and AI content only where that audience already engages with that kind of content. [S2]

### 3. Build the ad as content that sells

1. Begin with the recognizable situation in the first beat, then make the benefit, product role, and requested action easy to infer; an imperfect surface does not excuse an empty message. [S1][S2][S4]
2. Choose a native vessel that can plausibly live in the surrounding feed, such as a phone recording of an actual use moment, a simple handwritten note, or a candid customer account; pick it because it carries the chosen insight, not because it looks cheap. [S1][S2][S4]
3. Remove visual signals that make the asset read as conventional marketing when they do not help comprehension, such as unnecessary logo splashes, decorative layouts, studio gloss, or excessive transitions. [S1][S3][S4]
4. Preserve useful human detail, including natural speech, ordinary environments, or imperfect framing, when it makes the experience more believable and more immediately relatable. [S1][S2][S4]
5. Keep the product or offer legible and the claim supportable; the viewer should know what problem is addressed and what to do next after the human moment earns attention. [S1][S4]
6. For a premium brand, retain cues that communicate actual product quality while letting the surrounding story or treatment feel native to the feed; test how much polish the placement can bear. [S1][S3]
7. Write an alternate opening that addresses a different audience moment, not merely a rewritten headline on the same visual, when the first concept lacks a distinct reason to stop. [S4][S8]

### 4. Check relevance before approval

1. View each candidate in a mock feed or beside real organic references, at phone size and at normal scroll speed, before judging its beauty in a design file. [S2][S3][S7]
2. Ask whether the first second is relevant to the intended person, whether the content feels native to this placement, and whether the ad says something meaningful and drives an action. [S1][S2][S3]
3. Ask a cold reviewer what they think is happening, who it is for, and what the product changes; revise if the answer depends on knowledge that only the team has. [S4][S8]
4. Keep a rough execution if it clarifies a real human moment; discard deliberate ugliness, fake spontaneity, or random visual noise that supplies no audience relevance. [S1][S4]
5. Do not substitute a CEO's taste, a brand director's preference, or another marketer's applause for evidence that this audience stops and understands. [S3][S7]

### 5. Test concepts and learn from them

1. If the user controls a live account and authorizes testing, compare new concepts against the best existing ads under the same targeting, attribution, placements, and other material settings. [S6]
2. In a controlled creative test, give each concept a comparable opportunity to spend; Hott describes separate ad sets with the same settings and controlled budgets as a way to read creative differences. [S6]
3. Set the amount of evidence needed in light of price, budget, and normal purchase volume, rather than treating a fixed spend or universal click benchmark as proof. [S6]
4. Review acquisition outcomes alongside signals that an ad is reaching and moving people; inspect shares and cost per share before killing a rough ad solely because its reported ad level CPA is poor. [S5][S6]
5. Treat shares as a clue to additional distribution and possible attribution gaps, not as a guarantee that an unprofitable ad works. [S5][S6]
6. When an ad fails, form a specific hypothesis about its opening, audience moment, format, message, or placement and decide whether a meaningful revision merits a new test. [S6]
7. Keep the winning idea available for iteration while changing one meaningful creative angle at a time so the next test teaches something about why it worked. [S6]

## Common failure modes

- Making an ad intentionally unattractive and calling it authentic is a category error; Hott's point is to replace traditional polish with relevance, relatability, and a human feel. [S1][S4]
- Copying the look of a sticky note or phone video without a credible human observation makes the format the concept, which gives the viewer little reason to care. [S1][S4]
- Leading with the app screen or product features asks strangers to care about a brand before the ad reflects their own problem. [S4][S8]
- Importing a studio ad into a fast social feed because the campaign objective is awareness still ignores how that placement is consumed. [S3]
- Asking AI to invent what the audience likes before studying its actual feed freezes a changing context into a generic guess. [S2][S7]
- Killing a heavily shared ad on last click CPA alone can miss the value of people who encounter it through shares. [S5]
- Calling every ugly ad a winner ignores Hott's requirement that it attract relevant attention, communicate something that matters, and prompt action. [S1]

## Finished output

Deliver a brief that names the audience, viewing moment, placement, offer, and desired action, plus the organic references or observations that justify the chosen style. [S1][S3][S7]

Provide distinct concepts with each concept's opening beat, human situation, native format, product reveal, benefit, call to action, and the reason it should feel credible in that feed. [S1][S2][S4]

Include an alternate opening or angle for each promising concept, a phone size feed check, and a test plan that states the comparison conditions, decision signals, and what each outcome would teach. [S3][S5][S6]

If no live testing is authorized or possible, label predictions as hypotheses and give the user production ready concepts and a proposed test rather than claiming performance. [S1][S6]

## Worked example, illustration

Illustration: A premium meal planning app wants Instagram Reels ads for office workers who abandon their lunch plans by Wednesday. The cold viewer does not yet know the app. [S4][S8]

Audience research shows casual lunch prep clips and exasperated desk lunch stories in that audience's feed. The brief records their framing, speech, and pace, then chooses the real moment of opening another costly delivery order at 1 p.m. [S2][S7]

Concept A opens on an ordinary phone shot of the order total and a person's reaction, then shows the app helping them pick a realistic lunch for tomorrow. The caption names the familiar midweek problem, and the call to action offers a sample plan. [S1][S4]

Concept B opens with a handwritten list of lunches actually eaten this week, including two delivery orders, then a simple plan for next week. The handwriting supports a candid confession instead of serving as decoration. [S1][S4]

Both concepts retain a clear, attractive glimpse of the meal so the premium offer remains credible. The test compares them with the current polished ad under matching settings, then reviews purchases and shares before deciding which human moment deserves another iteration. [S1][S3][S5][S6]
