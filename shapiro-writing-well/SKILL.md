# Writing Well with Julian Shapiro's Method
Based on Julian Shapiro's public method. Not affiliated with or endorsed by Julian Shapiro.

## When to use

Use this skill to develop a nonfiction idea, turn notes into an article, or revise an existing draft for substance and reader engagement. Treat writing as a way to discover and clarify thinking, rather than merely transcribe a finished argument. [S1]

For an existing draft, identify its objective and strongest insight before entering the revision steps. Treat the procedure as a reliable approach rather than a rigid formula; departures are justified when the result remains useful and interesting. [S6]

## Operational procedure

### 1. Find the objective and the unresolved pull

Identify what the piece should accomplish, such as overturning a belief, solving a problem, simplifying a subject, or telling a story that teaches something. Pair that objective with the author's reason for caring. Ask what question keeps returning, what experience needs explaining, or what belief feels wrong. If the objective is unclear, explore the notes in a rough, diary-like passage and then choose an objective from what emerges. [S2]

Do not substitute a publishing schedule for something worth saying. Look for an idea the author keeps returning to and at least one worthwhile insight. If neither exists, mine their observations, disagreements, or explanations to friends before expanding the piece. A passionate disagreement still needs reasoning and evidence. [S6]

### 2. Test for worthwhile novelty

List candidate insights and identify which provide an unexpected mechanism, challenge an accepted story, reveal something startling, articulate an idea unusually well, or express a feeling readers recognize but struggle to name. Mere unfamiliarity is insufficient. Each candidate should offer something significant that readers would struggle to infer unaided. [S2]

Use the author's informed curiosity as a proxy for the intended reader. Learn the subject's basics, then investigate what still surprises that informed perspective. Avoid guessing at fashionable reader interests when the author has no meaningful curiosity about them. Retain questions that genuinely invite further exploration. [S3]

### 3. Draft the introduction before the full outline

Write an exploratory introduction containing only enough context for readers to understand the territory and care about a question. Generate several possible hooks by asking which unanswered question would make the author excited to read someone else's piece. Rank them by interest and turn the strongest into a partial narrative, finding, or argument whose explanation follows later. [S2]

Connect the hook to a meaningful reader problem. A question without stakes is weak, even if it sounds mysterious. Treat each hook as an obligation the body must fulfill and as a source of talking points. Seek early reactions to the introduction; use relevant objections and more compelling questions to revise its direction. [S2] [S6]

### 4. Outline prerequisites and consequences

Translate the objective and hook questions into talking points. Separate what readers must understand or accept from what follows if the argument is true. Usually establish supporting points before implications, but choose the path that serves this particular objective. The outline should orient exploration without fencing it in. [S3]

Fill it rapidly with incomplete thoughts from experience, research, experiments, and brainstorming. Use placeholders for unresolved details. Do not demand originality or polished explanations yet. Most discoveries should be allowed to emerge while drafting. When stuck, ask what would strengthen the current claim and what interesting consequence follows from it. [S3]

### 5. Generate through weak ideas

If early attempts are obvious or clumsy, develop them far enough to identify why they fail rather than abandoning the session. Contrast weak output with better alternatives and iterate toward a more original direction. Shapiro's proposed mechanism is a creative heuristic, not a guarantee that endless generation will produce insight. [S7]

Try combining approaches from unrelated domains when the combination meaningfully fits. Ask what the author wishes someone would finally explore, rather than assembling familiar pieces from admired work. Do not let the demand for total originality prevent a deeply relatable articulation of something already known. [S7]

### 6. Rewrite the thinking for clarity

For each section, state privately what it is really saying and which point readers must grasp. Rewrite abstract phrasing into concrete language and reduce the number of ideas carried by each sentence. Use the logic-following ability of a thirteen-year-old as the test, while preserving distinctions and causal explanations. Simpler language should reveal complex ideas, not erase them. [S4]

When plain wording still leaves uncertainty, add an example and a contrasting failure or before-and-after pair. Use familiar situations to make the boundary of the concept visible. Consider a diagram when explaining the example in prose requires readers to juggle too many moving parts. [S4]

### 7. Compress from sections down to paragraphs

Reconstruct an overgrown section from its essential argument before trimming words. Shapiro recommends reading it, taking roughly an hour away, and rewriting from memory, or rebuilding from a friend's brief spoken summary. For immediate agent work, use a separately written essentials summary as an adaptation, without claiming it reproduces human forgetting. [S4]

Then remove unnecessary words and rephrase each paragraph from scratch around what survives. Judge succinctness by the proportion of meaningful content, not a fixed length. Preserve useful complexity, but remove material that contributes neither understanding nor resonance. [S6]

Try compressing the entire argument into one short social post. If nothing important disappears, a longer article may be unnecessary. When the assignment requires the longer form, identify what additional reasoning or examples actually earn that space instead of padding the short idea. [S4]

### 8. Shape the reader's curiosity

Design a compelling opening, one concentrated peak of insight or surprise, and an ending that makes the reading feel worthwhile. Identify the strongest discoveries in the draft and arrange them into a climax. The ending should crystallize the piece's wisdom and, when useful, indicate how readers can continue exploring. [S6]

For narrative passages, decide in advance which answers arrive later. Raise further questions as earlier ones develop, and slow down important moments enough for readers to experience their stakes. Withholding should create anticipation that receives a meaningful payoff, rather than obscure the information readers need to follow the story. [S8]

### 9. Make the ideas resonate in the author's voice

Replace borrowed literary mannerisms with language that fits the author's way of speaking and seeing. Choose details that evoke a recognizable experience, rather than adding descriptive inventory. Where useful, describe an effect on the surroundings instead of naming its cause directly, allowing readers to infer the meaning. Keep the inference recoverable. [S5]

Use a story, analogy, anecdote, or image when it helps the idea take hold. Do not decorate every passage. For a story, select a consequential change, establish empathy and obstacles, and arrive at a meaningful discovery. Emphasize why the experience mattered to this author rather than copying another storyteller's persona. [S5] [S8]

### 10. Gather reactions and revise toward a stopping point

Prepare a feedback request for intended readers that asks where attention faded, where understanding broke, and where they felt delight or insight. Aggregate the highlighted moments of interest as counts beside sentences. Shorten stretches with few such reactions or strengthen them with worthwhile discoveries. Do not manufacture respondent counts from the agent's own impressions. [S4]

Ask several readers for a satisfaction score from 1 to 10. Shapiro's suggested stopping target is an average of at least 7.5, rather than chasing universal scores above 9 and bloating the piece. If outside feedback is unavailable, deliver the revision with that check pending. Reformatting the draft can help a fresh reread, but it does not substitute for actual reader reactions. [S4]

### 11. Copy edit and deliver

After substantive revision, favor short paragraphs, precise verbs, and modifiers that contribute necessary information. Remove pompous vocabulary used to perform intelligence. Read for awkward transitions and retain the author's natural voice. [S6]

Check punctuation for meaning. Prefer separate sentences when a comma joins complete thoughts without helping comprehension, and use a final list comma where it prevents ambiguity. Natural speech patterns can remain, but punctuation must make them intelligible on the page. [S9]

Deliver the revised piece with a fulfilled hook, sufficient supporting reasoning, a clear peak, and a satisfying ending. When the task includes editorial explanation, briefly identify its objective, principal insight, major cuts, and pending reader checks. Treat that note as an agent reporting adaptation rather than a template Shapiro prescribes. [S3] [S4] [S6]

## Worked example, invented illustration

Scenario: A manager wants an essay about meetings. Their initial thesis is that meetings waste time. Exploration produces a more specific possibility: recurring meetings may conceal who has authority to decide.

An illustrative hook is: "We canceled the weekly meeting. The decision still stalled. What had the meeting been hiding?" The objective is to help managers diagnose stalled decisions. The outline first distinguishes discussion from decision ownership, then examines consequences.

A rough sentence, "Coordination deficiencies create systemic organizational latency," becomes "Nobody knew who could approve the change." A contrasting example shows that removing a useful review meeting can also delay action.

The peak reveals that a crowded calendar may be a symptom of missing authority. The ending asks the reader to identify the decision owner before canceling the next meeting. This is an invented application, not a claim about actual workplace research or measured reader satisfaction.
