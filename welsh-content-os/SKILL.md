# The Content Operating System
Based on Justin Welsh's public method. Not affiliated with or endorsed by Justin Welsh.

## When to use

Use this skill when a creator, founder, or solo business owner needs a repeatable way to turn expertise into a newsletter or article and a set of useful social posts. It fits a request for a weekly content plan, an issue plus derivatives, or a diagnosis of a content workflow that depends on daily inspiration. It is a synthesis of Welsh's public descriptions and essays, not a reconstruction of lessons inside his paid course. [S1]

## Procedure

### 1. Define the reader and the destination

Record the intended reader, the specific problem they are trying to solve, the writer's relevant experience, the main platform, and the owned destination, usually an email list or a website article. If the request lacks these details, infer what the available material supports and label the assumptions. Keep the first distribution plan centered on one platform until there is evidence that it works, then adapt to additional channels. Welsh describes social attention as a route to deeper content and says he first concentrated on LinkedIn before expanding. [S7][S10]

Decide what the content is meant to do this cycle. An educational issue can earn a subscription, a social post can start a conversation, and a product related article can lead to an offer. Give each asset one primary next step so its promise and destination agree. For a social post that points to a deeper resource, link to the relevant article or issue rather than an undifferentiated homepage. [S7][S9]

### 2. Build an idea inventory before drafting

Collect candidate ideas from audience questions, comments, recurring objections, industry reading, and observed discussions. Capture the reader's wording, the question behind it, the writer's firsthand evidence, and a possible useful answer. Welsh describes reading industry material and engaging with his audience to find ideas, and his public course page describes curation as a way to keep ideas flowing. [S1][S2]

Study strong content for its structure, such as the opening promise, order of ideas, example type, and ending. Save the reusable pattern as a blank template, then fill it with the writer's own knowledge and evidence. Do not transplant another writer's claims or voice. Welsh's public description emphasizes curating and templatizing effective structures; his AI essay treats generated hooks and outlines as material to revise, reject, and personalize. [S1][S8]

Select one idea that passes three gates. The audience must have a recognizable problem or question, the writer must have a defensible lesson or example, and the idea must contain several angles that could stand alone after the main piece is written. If an idea offers only a catchy headline, keep it in the inventory and choose a stronger one. Welsh puts one substantial idea at the center of his content leverage model and recommends a pillar that addresses a major audience pain point. [S2][S3]

### 3. Make the substantial piece the hub

Draft the newsletter, article, or other substantial piece first. State the problem in the reader's language, show why it matters, deliver a clear method or lesson, support it with a concrete example, and end with an appropriate next step. Make it useful even if the reader never buys anything. Welsh describes a weekly newsletter as the hub from which social pieces are drawn, and his public course page names ideation, writing, promotion, and multiplication as the broad work. [S1][S2][S3]

Before extracting posts, test the hub for substance. Can a reader repeat the main lesson? Is each claim supported by a reason, observation, or example? Can at least three sections be understood independently? Repair a thin hub before multiplying it. Reformatting a weak idea produces more weak assets, while Welsh's 1-3-5 method depends on three substantial core ideas within the pillar. [S3]

### 4. Extract distinct spokes, then write each for its setting

Mark three independent lessons in the hub. For each, list possible treatments such as a practical tip, a short story, a mistake, a question, or a compact sequence. Use Welsh's 1-3-5 pattern as an optional expansion map: one pillar, three core ideas, and five small pieces per idea. For a smaller assignment, choose the strongest six to twelve social pieces rather than manufacturing every possible format. The public course page gives six to twelve weekly pieces as its target; the later essay shows how one pillar can yield fifteen additional assets. [S1][S3]

Write each spoke so it gives value on its own. Preserve the central lesson, but change the entry point, example, length, and presentation for the platform and audience. A LinkedIn post might explain a professional consequence, while a short X post might isolate one decision rule. If video or graphics are outside the request or available inputs, substitute written posts and questions instead of pretending those assets were produced. Welsh explicitly permits tailoring the 1-3-5 formats and describes adapting a winning short post with added professional context for LinkedIn. [S3][S10]

Apply the 5-12-3 filter to candidates when the goal is an evergreen library: can the opening make sense within five seconds, can the idea plausibly stay relevant for twelve months, and can the concept be adapted across three platforms? Treat it as a selection heuristic, not a promise of performance. Set time sensitive ideas aside for timely distribution rather than forcing them into the evergreen library. [S10]

### 5. Draft the post body before the hook

Write the useful material first: the lesson, evidence, specific steps, or advice. Then write an opening that interrupts the scroll and makes a truthful promise about that body. On LinkedIn, treat the text before the expand control as a short trailer whose lines lead the reader toward the substance. Finish with a compact recap and, when discussion is useful, a specific call to conversation. Welsh describes this order as body, trailer, then summary and conversation prompt. [S4]

Run a context check on every opening. A new reader should immediately understand who it concerns, what the topic is, and why that audience should care. Replace unexplained acronyms and insider phrasing with recognizable terms or a quick explanation. Welsh attributes a stark difference between two similar posts to one opening providing the necessary context. [S5]

Make the promised payoff visible in the body. Avoid a provocative opening that resolves into generic advice. For a conversation prompt, ask about a concrete experience or decision rather than adding a vague request for opinions. If a post's goal is a click to an article, use a clear, relevant link invitation instead of asking for comments as a competing primary action. [S4][S6][S9]

### 6. Distribute as a connected system

Prepare a sequence around the hub: a useful preview before publication, the complete newsletter or article, then selected spokes after publication. A preview should reveal enough value to earn interest, not withhold the answer. Give some spokes a route to the matching article or issue so social readers can explore the deeper explanation and subscribe if they choose. Welsh's public course description includes promotion before and after the newsletter, and his engagement loop essay connects short posts with newsletter issues. [S1][S7]

Where the publishing setup allows it, connect related articles and social posts so a reader can follow another useful idea and return to a subscription opportunity. Keep links relevant to the specific question at hand. If there is an offer, place it after the article has taught something, as a quiet continuation for readers who need more help. Welsh describes this sequence of short attention, relevant long form content, and a related offer as passive selling. [S7][S9]

Schedule approved assets for the chosen channel and record their destination and publication status. Automation can handle scheduling and repetitive handoffs, but only after the content and links have been reviewed. Welsh advocates simplifying a repeatable process before automating it, and his public course description treats distribution as a distinct system component. [S1][S11]

### 7. Measure components and revise the system

Maintain a simple log for each published asset: topic, opening type, body structure, conversation prompt or link invitation, optional visual, channel, date, impressions, meaningful comments, clicks, and subscriptions where available. Look at the top posts by impressions to inspect openings, read reactions to inspect the body, and compare comment rates or clicks according to the chosen next step. These are diagnostic signals, not proof that one component alone caused the result. [S6][S9]

Review patterns in the writer's own results and revise the template inventory. Test one component at a time when practical, keep the structures that repeatedly work for this audience, and revisit them as platform behavior changes. Do not assume Welsh's preferred post length, visual style, or platform mix will transfer unchanged. He explicitly advises readers to run their own tests and adjust over time. [S6]

At the end of the cycle, remove outputs with no clear job, simplify cumbersome steps, then automate or delegate stable repetitive work. Protect the time needed to interact with readers, collect new questions, and write the next hub. Welsh's productivity review uses elimination, simplification, automation, and delegation in that order. [S11]

## Failure checks and finished output

Reject a batch if the spokes merely paste sections of the hub, if the opening needs insider context, if several posts promise the same payoff in the same form, or if a call to action sends a reader to an unrelated page. Fix those failures by choosing a new angle, clarifying the reader's stakes, and linking the closest relevant destination. [S3][S5][S9]

The finished deliverable should contain the reader and objective, an idea inventory or selection rationale, one substantial hub draft or outline, a labeled set of distinct spokes, channel and publication order, the next step for each asset, and a small measurement plan. When the user requests only part of the system, produce that part plus the minimum context needed to use it. The system's purpose is repeatable production of helpful writing and connected distribution, not a fixed post count for its own sake. [S1][S2][S6]

## Worked example (illustration)

An independent bookkeeper serves local restaurant owners who struggle to predict cash shortfalls. She chooses the recurring question, "Why did a profitable month leave me short of cash?" Her hub newsletter explains the timing gap between recorded sales, card deposits, payroll, and supplier bills, then walks through a small example ledger. The three standalone ideas are profit versus cash, a weekly cash calendar, and a warning sign in payment terms. [S2][S3]

She drafts six spokes: two concise explanations, one story about a payroll week, one three-step calendar post, one question asking owners which bill timing surprises them, and one post pointing to the full newsletter. Each opening names restaurant owners or a recognizable cash problem. The link post goes to that issue, whose ending offers a relevant cash planning worksheet. Her tracking sheet separates impressions, useful replies, clicks, and worksheet requests, so the next issue responds to actual reader confusion. [S4][S5][S7][S9]
