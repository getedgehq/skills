# Worked examples

Both scenarios are invented illustrations. Names, business situations, figures, and draft content demonstrate decisions; they are not client evidence or Welsh's results. Each example uses a compact hub and three spokes to show the method without manufacturing a full weekly quota. [S3]

## Illustration 1: A bookkeeper explains restaurant cash timing

### Brief and selection

Reader: Independent restaurant owners who see accounting profit but worry about making payroll.

Writer: A bookkeeper with permission to discuss a fictional ledger, without claiming it represents a client.

Objective: Teach a weekly cash check and invite readers to the full issue.

Primary channel: LinkedIn. Owned destination: A planned newsletter issue about cash timing.

Idea inventory:

| Candidate | Evidence available | Decision |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Become financially successful | No defined problem or method | Reject as too broad. |
| Why profit does not mean cash is available for payroll | Illustrative dated receipts and payments | Select: recognizable problem and three usable lessons. |
| How much cash every restaurant should keep | No context-specific basis | Hold for research; avoid a universal reserve claim. |

### Weak first draft

**Hub:** Restaurant finance is complicated. Profit is important, but cash flow is even more important. Track your KPIs, optimize your working capital, and plan ahead. Subscribe for more tips.

**Social post:** This cash-flow secret will transform your business. Every owner needs better KPIs. Agree? Follow me, subscribe, and book a call at my homepage.

The draft gives no usable process, relies on unexplained terms, promises an unsupported transformation, and asks for several unrelated actions. There is too little substance to multiply.

### Improved hub draft

**Title: A profitable month can still leave a restaurant short on payroll day**

Monthly profit and Friday's bank balance answer different questions. Profit describes income and costs for a period. The cash available for payroll depends on when money actually arrives and leaves.

Consider this invented week: Your restaurant starts Monday with £4,000 available. A £3,000 supplier bill is due Tuesday, £2,000 in card receipts arrives Thursday, and £4,000 payroll leaves Friday. With no other movements, Friday's balance is £1,000 short. The expected receipts do not cover the dated payments.

Before the week begins:

1. Write down the cash available now.
2. Put expected receipts on their likely arrival dates.
3. Put payroll, supplier bills, and other payments on their due dates.
4. Calculate the running balance after each movement.
5. Investigate any shortfall early, including whether receipt dates are dependable and which payment terms can be discussed.

This calendar is a planning aid, not a guarantee. Unexpected costs and delayed receipts still matter. Start with the dates you know, then update the calendar as the week changes.

Try the check before your next payroll run. The aim is to see a timing problem while there is still time to address it.

### Improved spokes

**A. Explanation, LinkedIn, standalone education**

Restaurant owners: A profitable month does not tell you whether Friday's payroll is covered.

Check when receipts reach the bank and when bills leave it. Put both on a dated calendar and calculate the balance after each movement. A monthly total can hide the day that creates a shortfall.

**B. Process, LinkedIn, conversation**

Before payroll week, give your restaurant's cash forecast five rows: starting cash, expected deposits, supplier payments, payroll, and other bills.

Add dates, then calculate the balance after each payment. That turns a vague worry into a specific day to investigate.

Which arrival or payment date is hardest for you to predict?

**C. Resource invitation, LinkedIn, article visit**

A restaurant can expect enough receipts for the month and still run short on Friday.

In my cash-timing issue, an invented week starts with £4,000 and ends £1,000 short after supplier payments, deposits, and payroll. The useful part is seeing the shortfall before Friday.

Read the dated walkthrough here: [cash-timing issue URL pending].

### Distribution and measurement

Use B as a pre-release insight, publish the hub, then A and C on separate planned slots. C stays in draft until its exact issue URL exists. Record impressions and useful questions for A and B, and article clicks for C. Collect subscriptions only if the destination provides that metric. No results are claimed.

### Rules that changed the result

- A recognizable cash problem replaces a broad aspiration, applying the context test. [S5]
- A worked ledger gives the hub enough teaching to support independent spokes. [S2][S3]
- Bodies contain the promised value before openings are polished. [S4]
- The conversation question asks about actual experience; the resource post has a different primary job. [S6]
- The link continues the same topic rather than sending readers to a homepage. [S9]

## Illustration 2: A freelance designer reduces revision confusion

### Brief and selection

Reader: Freelance designers whose clients send scattered feedback.

Writer: A designer sharing a proposed process, with no quantified results supplied.

Objective: Teach a simple review brief and learn where readers encounter friction.

Primary channel: X. Secondary adaptation: LinkedIn for an already established professional audience.

Inventory: Tool recommendations lack relevant testing; a review brief has a concrete procedure; a story about cutting revisions by 80% has no evidence. Select the review brief and discard the unsupported number.

### Weak first draft

**Hub:** My revolutionary review system eliminates endless revisions. Just communicate clearly, manage expectations, and use the right tools. Download my secret framework.

**Posts:** Stop endless revisions. / Communication is everything. / Want fewer revisions? Use my framework.

These are three versions of one slogan. The hub gives no instructions, the result is invented, and the offer does not exist.

### Improved hub draft

**Title: Give clients a decision to make before asking for design feedback**

When a review link arrives without instructions, clients have to guess what kind of feedback is useful. A layout review may turn into a debate about final copy or colors before either is ready.

Try adding a short review brief above the link:

- Purpose: This page should help visitors understand the service and find the enquiry form.
- Review stage: Today we are reviewing layout and information order.
- Question: Where would a first-time visitor struggle to find the next step?
- Boundary: Final photography and copy will be reviewed later.
- Response route: Please consolidate comments in the review document before the agreed deadline.

For example, instead of sending a homepage draft with only a request for thoughts, name the decision: Does the order of these sections explain the service clearly enough to reach the enquiry form?

This does not remove every revision. New stakeholders, changed requirements, and genuine design problems still need attention. The brief gives this review a shared focus so disagreements are easier to interpret.

For your next review, write the purpose, stage, and question before sending the link.

### Improved spokes

**A. Practical tip, X, standalone education**

Freelance designers: Before sending a review link, name the decision you need.

For a layout review: Can a first-time visitor find the enquiry form?

Add what is out of scope today, so placeholder photos do not become the main discussion.

**B. Mistake, LinkedIn, conversation**

A design review link needs more than a request for thoughts.

Without a review stage, a client can discuss final copy while you are testing the page structure. Put the purpose, current decision, and later decisions above the link. Then give everyone one place to collect feedback.

Which review decision do your clients most often mix with another stage?

**C. Compact process, X, standalone education**

A design review brief can be five lines:

1. What the page must achieve.
2. What we are reviewing now.
3. The decision we need.
4. What comes later.
5. Where and when to send feedback.

Try writing those before your next review link.

### Distribution and measurement

Publish the hub on the writer's existing newsletter, then space A, B, and C as different treatments. No invented download is offered. A future social-to-hub invitation needs the actual issue URL. Compare useful replies to B with previous experience questions; retain reader objections for the next issue. Record publication dates and equal observation windows before comparing reach.

### Rules that changed the result

- A focused problem replaces an unsupported outcome and nonexistent offer.
- Three lessons emerge: name the decision, separate review stages, and consolidate the response route. These support variation rather than duplicate slogans. [S3]
- LinkedIn adds professional context while X isolates a practical action. [S10]
- The recap and experience question make discussion easier without generic engagement bait. [S4][S6]
- The process is small enough to repeat; a new tool or automation is unnecessary until the workflow is stable. [S11]
