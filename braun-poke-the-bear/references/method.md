# Method and decision rules

## Scope and attribution

Braun's method starts a conversation about the prospect's existing way of doing a job. Its central move is a question that makes a possible overlooked exposure visible without deciding in advance that the prospect needs help. His public examples include direct openings, permission openings, contextual stories, and questions without a preceding story. Use the underlying intent to choose among them, rather than treating any example as a mandatory script. [S1][S2][S3][S7]

The output schema, evidence labels, branch coverage, and pass/fail tests in this package are operational conventions. They translate the public guidance into a repeatable drafting and review process; they are not claimed to be Braun's named framework. Invented examples illustrate reasoning rather than establish results.

## Principles

### Discover whether there is a problem

Sales pressure produces a protective response. Braun describes this as the Zone of Resistance. An opening that tries to secure agreement to an attractive benefit can reinforce that response because the prospect recognizes the intended destination. Curiosity instead examines the current job without assuming a defect. The practical objective is to learn whether a gap exists and whether the person wants to discuss it. [S2][S3][S5]

Detachment must affect the branches, not just the tone. If every answer leads to a demo, the script still carries a predetermined agenda. Satisfaction should end the diagnostic thread unless the prospect introduces another concern. A person can make adequate progress with a less capable solution because their resources and priorities differ from the seller's. [S8][S12]

### Make the exposure specific

An existing provider may perform its main job successfully while a separate issue remains unseen. Generic promises to save money or improve efficiency make the offer sound interchangeable with that provider. The useful question examines a mechanism: coverage between checks, completeness of a record, reconciliation between systems, or verification that an apparent success holds up. [S2][S4]

Internally map four elements:

- Job: the outcome the prospect owns.
- Current approach: what is known, or a clearly marked assumption.
- Possible blind spot: the condition that could escape that approach.
- Consequence: what could follow if the blind spot exists.

Braun emphasizes the potential cost of continuing unchanged and discusses loss aversion and resistance to change. Treat those as his explanations of the approach, not proof of a conversion rate or permission to amplify fear. A consequence must follow from a credible mechanism. Specific does not mean catastrophic. [S4][S6]

### Preserve the possibility of a satisfactory answer

A question is neutral when it reveals how the prospect thinks or works, rather than forcing agreement with the seller. An open question can still be leading if it embeds a failure as a fact. Ask about how they check, handle, or verify a process without claiming that they are failing to do so. [S2][S3]

Use a practical test: can the prospect describe a complete, effective current process without having to dispute the premise? If not, remove the accusation or establish the premise first. This test is the package's synthesis of Braun's distinction between exploration and leading questions. [S2][S3]

## Evidence and framing decisions

Separate known facts, plausible hypotheses, and unknowns before writing. A title may suggest ownership without proving it. A seller's observation about a group does not establish an individual prospect's loss. Braun's numerical examples belong to those examples; do not transfer their amounts, prevalence, or geography to unrelated outreach. This evidence discipline is a package convention for applying his concrete framing responsibly. [S4][S5][S12]

Use a documented peer pattern if the seller can substantiate it. Otherwise state a possible process gap or ask how the job is done. Do not invent a customer story or imply that the seller works with the prospect's community. Braun's familiarity examples rely on actual shared context, and his ethics guidance criticizes omission and self-serving pressure. [S7][S8]

When framing a problem, keep it impersonal and correctable. A brief situation involving comparable people can let the listener consider a risk without feeling blamed. Braun specifically recommends putting an unexamined problem into a similar customer's story to reduce personal attack. This is an option, not a requirement to manufacture social proof. [S6]

If there is no credible blind spot, ask a straightforward current-process question. Do not create a dramatic problem merely to satisfy a template. Curiosity about the job is itself consistent with Braun's permission-based examples. [S3][S5][S7]

## Opening decisions

### Cold calls

Select one opening suited to the assignment:

- Direct: identity, a short conversational greeting, then a responsibility check if needed. [S1]
- Permission: identity, acknowledge the unexpected contact, and ask whether a brief exchange is possible. [S3][S5]
- Familiarity-supported: use genuine work with a comparable group to explain relevance, then move quickly to the prospect's process. [S7]

Do not concatenate all three into a long preamble. Where research supports ownership, use a tentative observation and invite correction instead of adding another qualification question. When ownership is unclear, ask who handles the job. Avoid collecting team size, title confirmation, workflow detail, and buying authority before making the reason for the conversation intelligible. [S11]

A permission opening is a real decision point. If the person declines, end the exchange. A time promise also constrains the call: do not treat initial consent as permission for an extended presentation. [S8][S10]

### Cold email

Carry over the process examination, not the spoken ceremony. Use enough identity and job context for the question to make sense, then provide one clear reply point. A greeting exchange, a simulated pause, and every call branch do not belong in the email body. Keep branch notes separately for handling replies. This adaptation is the package's channel convention; Braun explicitly applies neutral questions to cold email. [S2]

Avoid a benefit question that bundles desirability with agreement to hear a pitch. A process question should allow a reply about success, another approach, or low relevance. Use a short subject that accurately reflects the process under discussion, without implying a verified incident.

## Question and menu decisions

Ask one main question about the current job or the specific verification gap. A how or what opening is useful, but grammar alone does not guarantee neutrality. Remove assumed losses and product-shaped answers. Stop after the question so the prospect can supply their own reality. [S1][S2][S3]

A small menu can help when an open question requires too much effort. Include plausible ways the job is currently done and allow correction. Braun uses menus both to demonstrate workflow familiarity and to make responding easier. Some of his examples end with an attractive alternative; this package applies the neutrality test to prevent that alternative from becoming the only respectable answer. [S5][S12]

Use no menu when the prospect can easily explain the process or when options would introduce unsupported assumptions. Do not make the list a disguised ranking from incompetent to intelligent.

## Listening and progression

After the main question, pause. After the person finishes, allow about two beats before responding. Braun's listening sheet recommends a roughly two-to-one listening-to-speaking balance, short mirrors, tentative labels, and invitations to elaborate. Treat the ratio as a practice goal, not a timing requirement for each individual call. [S1][S9]

A mirror repeats a small part of their language to invite clarification. A label offers a tentative interpretation of what they said. Ground both in the answer. Do not mechanically mirror every sentence, invent an emotion, or steer a label toward the admission you wanted. Alternate questions with observations so the call does not become an interrogation. [S9][S11]

Braun warns against multitasking, assuming the next answer, judging, and responding to someone else's story with your own. These behaviors shift attention away from understanding the prospect. A prepared talk track should therefore include listening cues and flexible branches, not a speech to recite regardless of the response. [S9]

Use the following branches:

| Response | Decision | Next move |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Adequate current process | Accept their account | Acknowledge it and close; explore further only if they introduce a concern. |
| Specific gap | Understand their experience | Reflect the gap and ask one relevant clarification, without assuming urgency. |
| Explicit refusal | End the exchange | Thank them and stop; do not add a last-chance question. |
| Conversational dismissal with continued willingness | Clarify relevance optionally | Acknowledge it, then seek permission for one clarification about satisfaction or ownership. |
| Interest or product question | Explain relevance briefly | Connect the capability to their process and offer an optional later conversation. |
| Wrong or uncertain owner | Correct the relevance assumption | Accept correction; ask about ownership only if they remain willing. |

These branches combine detached intent, Braun's optional clarification of disinterest, and his conditional next-step examples. An explicit request to stop takes precedence over the clarification technique. [S1][S5][S8][S10][S12]

Explain the product when asked why you are calling or when interest makes an explanation relevant. Detachment does not require evading a direct question. Give a truthful, short explanation tied to the job, then allow a decision. [S5]

When the conversation reaches interest, preserve the short-call promise and propose a later time to examine options. Braun warns against extending a productive unexpected call until its appeal fades. A gap alone is not consent to a meeting. [S10]

## Delivery, edge cases, and explicit warnings

Braun identifies vague promises, confusing language, wrong ownership, a good-enough current solution, overexcited delivery, and talking too long as reasons outreach fails. Keep the voice calm and conversational. His approximately fifteen-second speaking guideline is a useful edit signal: shorten a long stretch and give the conversation back, rather than speeding up to fit the script. [S12]

Respect these explicit warnings:

- Do not lead people toward an obvious benefit to trap agreement. Ask about their process instead. [S2]
- Do not assume a problem or try to talk someone into a meeting. Discover applicability. [S3][S5]
- Do not center the opening on the seller's desire to show a product. Examine the prospect's situation. [S6]
- Do not treat people as objects to collect, omit material information, refuse no, or manufacture pressure with offer deadlines. [S8]
- Do not turn nervous questioning into an interrogation. Add grounded observations and listen. [S11]
- Do not interrupt, multitask, anticipate answers, or replace their story with yours. [S9]
- Do not prolong an unexpected call after interest appears. Offer a later conversation. [S10]
- Do not use prospects as a practice audience. Rehearse and improve between calls. [S12]

For a known provider, acknowledge that it may work well and inspect a distinct exposure only if warranted. For an unknown process, ask before narrowing the hypothesis. For an emotionally charged or costly exposure, use measured language and concrete mechanisms rather than fear. For a request for evidence, disclose what is supported and what remains a hypothesis. [S2][S3][S4][S8]

Braun's Aunt Flow illustration refers to a legal requirement. The package uses that source for conversation structure, not current legal guidance. Verify any legal obligation independently before including it in actual outreach; do not reuse its jurisdictional premise automatically. [S10]
