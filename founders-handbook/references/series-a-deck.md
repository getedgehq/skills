# How to nail the Series A deck

Source: 1984 Ventures Founders Handbook, chapter "How to Nail Your Series A Deck" by
Samit Kalra (https://1984.vc/docs/founders-handbook/series-a-deck). The chapter links an example deck by the author. This file is a
derived work; credit 1984 Ventures and the author, and do not present it as original.

## General principles

1. An investor spends 30 seconds reviewing the deck before deciding whether to meet.
   Do not overload it with information.
2. The deck tells a story, and the story should build sequentially. It is not a deep
   dive on strategy, positioning and competitive advantage; write a separate memo for
   that if you want one.
3. Every slide has only one simple message, presented in the header. Heuristic: if the
   investor only reads the slide titles, do they still get the narrative?
4. Aim for 15 slides, 20 max.
5. Most investors know nothing about your space. The deck's job is to grab attention,
   not to educate; education happens in the presentation and targeted Q&A after.
6. The deck does not need professional design and slides can be minimalistic, but it
   should still be neat.

The narrative woven through the slides should focus on why you will dominate your
category: traction, team, unique insight.

## Typical structure

Reorder if it serves the narrative, but grab attention from the very beginning:

1. Cover slide
2. Overview
3. Exec Summary
4. Market Size
5. Problem
6. Solution
7. Traction
8. Customers
9. Case Study (optional)
10. Product Roadmap
11. Competition
12. Growth Plan
13. Vision
14. Team
15. Fundraising Ask (optional)
16. Closing

## Slide-by-slide guidance

- **Cover (1):** a simple one-line overview of what the business does, or something
  intriguing.
- **Overview (2):** optional if the cover already carried the message; useful to get
  the reader up to speed on what is coming.
- **Executive Summary (3):** the benchmarks to raise a Series A have gone up, so
  investors expect strong traction. If you have impressive traction, put it early to
  force the investor to pay attention for the rest of the deck.
- **Market Size (4):** investors discount top-down figures and prefer bottoms-up. They
  preemptively ignore markets they deem too small, so do not pitch anything under
  $1 to 2B. If the market is small-ish (low $Bs) but growing fast, include the speed of
  growth. Do not pitch a gigantic multi-hundred-$B number quoted from a market research
  paper either; investors are skeptical of those. Note that AI unlocks labor automation,
  so software companies can tap labor budgets, not just software budgets. Those can be
  orders of magnitude bigger and can turn categories investors felt were too small into
  something investable. It is on you to convey this clearly.
- **Problem (5):** state the problem so the investor easily understands it. Cognitive
  load increases the chance the investor will not bother to dig in.
- **Solution (6):** also convey that what you built is hard.
- **Traction (7):** up-and-to-the-right graphs are always well received. Include a few
  key metrics, but do not overload.
- **Customers (8):** logos are an easy way to provide social proof, if recognizable.
- **Case Study (9):** a slide in the deck or a standalone document.
- **Product Roadmap (10):** should convey three things: you are a product centric
  company that ships fast (point solutions get commoditized quickly), you have an
  ambitious vision (e.g. becoming the System of Record), and you are pursuing the market
  in a unique way.
- **Competition (11):** a more detailed market segmentation can go in a written document
  sent after the meeting.
- **Growth Plan (12):** founder ambition is how investors justify picking a company in a
  crowded category, so convey ambitious growth targets. Investors frequently see
  companies pitching growth from 0 to $3/5/7M. For reference, forecasting $1 to $3M is
  decent but not especially ambitious. If you are under $2M, forecast 5x growth for that
  year, then 3x the year after. 2x growth is unlikely to meet the threshold.
- **Vision (13):** a compelling vision that excites the investor. They are looking for
  fund-returning investments, not simply good companies, so make the vision play to
  that.
- **Team (14):** convey that the team is special: pedigree, unique founder-market fit,
  repeat founders. Series A investors also want a team ready for a Series A, meaning the
  company has grown beyond the founders to include a few additional hires, typically
  engineering and sometimes sales. Include LinkedIn links on the page so an investor can
  gauge the team quickly.
- **Funding Ask (15):** optional, and you do not have to state the exact dollar amount,
  since you may iterate on it with feedback. The risk of naming a number is investors
  self-selecting out if the round seems too small or too large for them. For a Series A,
  $8 to 20M is likely to fit most multi-stage funds, but $20M may be high for many, so
  you may want to say something like $15M, which can get bid up to $20M with competing
  term sheets.
