## Impact of a Series A on the cap table

Chapter: https://1984.vc/docs/founders-handbook/raising-your-series-a (by Ramy Adeeb)

Starting point (priced seed for simplicity): founders 35% each (1,000,000 shares each),
employees 7% (200,000), seed investor 20% (571,428), available pool 3% (85,714); total
2,857,142. Series A: $8M at $40M post, lead targets 20%.

| Step | New shares | Founder each | Seed | Series A | Pool | Total shares | Founder-side dilution |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A only (PPS $11.20) | 714,286 | 28% | 16% | 20% | 2.4% | 3,571,427 | 20% |
| A + refresh pool to 10% (PPS about $10.10) | 354,286 pool + 791,837 A | 25.26% | 14.43% | 20% | 10% | 3,959,183 | about 29% |
| A + pool + seed takes full pro-rata | 1,542,858 in total | 22.73% | 20% | 20% | 10% | 4,400,000 | 35% |

Why 29% and not 20% + 7%: the new shares must leave the Series A at 20% and the pool at
10%, so existing holders absorb more. The chapter says 25 to 30% total founder dilution
in a Series A is not uncommon. (The chapter's prose gives 4,400,400 total shares in one
sentence and 4,400,000 in its table; use the table and note the discrepancy if asked.)

For your own numbers, point the founder to 1984's Cap Table Worksheet
(https://startup-finance.1984.vc) rather than solving the pool recursion by hand.

## Minimizing founder dilution

Chapter: https://1984.vc/docs/founders-handbook/minimizing-founder-dilution

The best defense is execution. Beyond that, ten levers, the first six during the round:

1. **Get a second term sheet (BATNA).** Set a clear timeline; say you are in advanced
   discussions without naming firms; generate genuine interest, not shopping.
2. **Negotiate down the lead's ownership.** Leads open around 15%, some at 20%; cut the
   round size if you do not need the capital, or cut the lead's check to fit others.
3. **Offer other terms** (board structure, information rights, protective provisions,
   major investor rights) for lower ownership, but not too much board control and not
   super pro-rata.
4. **Negotiate a smaller option pool**: 5 to 8% instead of 10 to 12%; expect pushback,
   especially with a thin executive team.
5. **Renegotiate early investors' pro-rata**: get the friendliest to agree first, then
   ask all others to reduce equally.
6. **Negotiate a founder grant** of 1 to 2% post-financing when CEO ownership has fallen
   below an acceptable line (the chapter's examples: 20% at the A, 10% at the B). Tread
   carefully.
7. **Start with fewer co-founders.** Three equal founders at 33% can leave the CEO under
   20% by the Series A.
8. **Limit pro-rata at seed** to larger investors, typically $500K+ checks.
9. **Raise more earlier** (a larger seed or a small-dilution extension) to reach the A at
   a higher valuation.
10. **Build something people want.** The chapter notes some 1984 companies went from Seed
    straight to Series B.

## Selling secondaries

Chapter: https://1984.vc/docs/founders-handbook/selling-secondaries (by Ramy Adeeb)

- A secondary is a founder selling existing shares; it is not dilutive and usually
  trades at a discount because common stock has no liquidation preference. Needs a
  buyer and board consent.
- When: typically Series C or later, Series B for top performers. Best near a $500M to
  $1B valuation with a clearly oversubscribed round.
- Buyers, best first: existing investors, the new round's lead, secondaries funds, LPs
  and family offices (via your investors), investment banks for pre-IPO.
- Consent: ROFR and co-sale rights must be waived, so the lead or board must agree. Do
  not raise it until the round is clearly oversubscribed. Selling large amounts or at
  Series A or B is a red flag.
- How much: often millions, $500K is not uncommon; the chapter says most investors
  support 5 to 10% of the founder's stake at an oversubscribed Series C. Make the case
  that liquidity frees focus for the company.
- Price: negligible discount for top companies in oversubscribed rounds, 10 to 30% for
  decent ones, 50% or more for average ones.
- Watch for: tender offer rules (SEC Regulation 14E, e.g. 20 business days), 409A
  impact, the 2,000-shareholder SEC threshold, QSBS (the founder's shares qualify after
  the holding period; the buyer's do not; a redemption to convert common to preferred
  can disqualify everyone), and employee morale (keep it quiet, then open a program to
  long-tenured employees).

## Structured and down rounds

Chapter: https://1984.vc/docs/founders-handbook/structured-and-downrounds

When a company raised above what it can merit now, it has three options: a structured
round at the old valuation with dirty terms, a clean down round, or a push to
profitability. The chapter's view, citing Bill Gurley's 2016 "On the Road to Recap": a
down round is usually better for existing shareholders because it resets to market and
avoids onerous terms.

Structure terms, roughly best to worst per the chapter:

1. **Warrants**: most benign; an effective lower price that saves face.
2. **Pay-to-play**: disliked by earlier investors but company and founder friendly.
3. **Dividends / PIK**: dilutive quickly; be on a path to sell.
4. **Guaranteed IPO returns**: tolerable unless the IPO price is near the liquidation stack.
5. **Series-based M&A vetoes**: generally bad for future M&A.
6. **SAFE with a liquidation preference** (1.2 to 2x, sometimes 3 to 4x): bad; if forced,
   take it on a SAFE whose preference disappears on conversion in the next equity round.
7. **Priced round with a liquidation preference**: pretty bad; every later investor will
   want equal or higher. Consider a clean down round instead.

Down round anti-dilution (in the Certificate of Incorporation, often renegotiated):
full ratchet (very dilutive, avoid), broad-based weighted average (most common, NVCA
recommended, mild if the down round is small), narrow-based weighted average (preferred
shares only). A returning lead may ask to move its existing shares to full ratchet.

The chapter's closing warning: founders hope a structured or down round buys runway to
profitability or an exit, and it rarely does.
