## Legal considerations: preparing the company for sale

Chapter: https://1984.vc/docs/founders-handbook/mergers-and-acquisitions/legal-considerations

Six steps, ideally before seeking a buyer:

1. **Engage experienced M&A counsel**, plus tax, IP, antitrust or employment specialists
   as needed. Outside an auction, the buyer's counsel usually drafts the acquisition
   agreement first.
2. **Run sell-side due diligence** to find anything that could delay or block the deal,
   including required board, stockholder, regulatory and third-party consents, and to
   prepare the disclosure schedules. Checklist from the chapter: financial statements
   (possibly audited GAAP, possibly projections); corporate records, good standing,
   up-to-date organizational documents, equity issued properly; material contracts with
   change-of-control or anti-assignment clauses; liens on assets; ownership of key IP;
   permits and licences; pending litigation.
3. **Prepare the virtual data room**: financials, corporate records, capitalization,
   material customer and vendor contracts, IP, employees and benefits, permits,
   litigation, real estate, insurance.
4. **Structure the transaction** (below).
5. **Negotiate the terms** (below).
6. **Sign and close**, simultaneously when no significant third-party approvals are
   needed (preferred by sellers because it removes interim risk), otherwise in two
   stages, then handle post-closing filings and obligations.

## Structuring: asset, stock, or merger

Chapter: https://1984.vc/docs/founders-handbook/mergers-and-acquisitions/structuring

| Structure | What transfers | Buyer view | Seller view | Consents and process |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Asset acquisition | Listed assets and liabilities; seller entity remains and is later liquidated | Picks assets, limits unknown liabilities (successor liability can still apply), stepped-up tax basis | C-corp sellers face potential double taxation plus possible sales and transfer taxes; pass-through entities are more willing | Most complex: separate transfers and many anti-assignment consents. Common for acquihires |
| Stock acquisition | 100% of stock; target becomes a subsidiary with all liabilities | Good for a going concern with few stockholders; protects itself with indemnities and escrow | Generally preferred: single level of tax, usually no transfer taxes | Simplest; consents only under change-of-control clauses; slow with many stockholders |
| Merger | Two entities combine; survivor takes all assets and liabilities | Reaches 100% with majority stockholder approval, useful with many or reluctant holders | Depends on the form | Most statutory requirements, filings and appraisal processes |

## Terms to negotiate

Chapter: https://1984.vc/docs/founders-handbook/mergers-and-acquisitions/terms

The chapter's founder tip: counsel handles the legal terms; negotiating the commercial
terms is primarily the founder's job.

- **Consideration** (commercial): cash, notes, stock, or a mix. Sellers usually prefer
  cash; stock can make sense for tax deferral or QSBS. Consult tax advisors.
- **Purchase price adjustments and earnouts**: adjustments are common (usually working
  capital). Earnouts bridge valuation gaps; a founder staying on can accept one more
  easily than a founder who wants a clean exit.
- **Escrow**: the chapter cites 5 to 10% of the price held for 12 to 18 months; negotiate
  amount, length and release mechanics.
- **Scope of assets and liabilities** (asset deals): sellers prefer "everything except
  listed exclusions".
- **Governance and social issues**: name, headquarters, leadership, board, founder role,
  whether the brand and product survive.
- **Representations and warranties**: the longest, most negotiated section; sellers
  limit them with materiality, knowledge and time qualifiers. Review them closely; they
  drive later indemnification claims.
- **Closing conditions**: sellers want fewer.
- **Covenants**: pre-closing operating limits and post-closing non-compete and
  non-solicit. Check every one that binds the founder personally.
- **Indemnification**: sellers limit it with caps, baskets and shorter survival periods.

## QSBS for early-stage founders

Chapter: https://1984.vc/docs/founders-handbook/taxes/qsbs (by Mark Percival)

US federal only; the chapter notes that state treatment varies and California does not
recognize it. Rules as the chapter states them for stock acquired after July 4, 2025
(One Big Beautiful Bill Act): holding period tiers of 3 years (50% exclusion), 4 years
(75%) and 5+ years (100%); exclusion cap $15M (was $10M), inflation-adjusted from 2027;
gross asset threshold $75M at issuance (was $50M). Stock issued before July 4, 2025
keeps the old rules (5-year hold, $10M cap).

Qualification: C-corporation only (not an LLC), under the asset threshold at issuance,
80% of assets in qualified business activities, stock acquired at original issuance.

How to secure it: the clock starts at issuance, not vesting; use restricted common stock;
keep records of issuance dates and asset levels; file an 83(b) within 30 days of a grant
(not required for QSBS but, per the chapter, almost always beneficial); start as a
C-corp because an LLC conversion resets the clock.

The redemption trap: company redemptions above 5% of stock value over 24 months
disqualify everyone's QSBS. The chapter's case: a company buys back 45% from a departing
50/50 co-founder after 16 months and destroys QSBS for both. Alternatives it lists: the
remaining founder buys personally, unvested shares are forfeited, the departing founder
sells to investors, or they keep 5 to 10% instead of a full buyout.

Tax law changes. Present these figures as "per the 1984 chapter" and tell the founder
to confirm current rules with a tax advisor before acting.
