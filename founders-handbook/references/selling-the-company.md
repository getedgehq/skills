# How to sell your startup

Source: 1984 Ventures Founders Handbook, chapter "How to Sell Your Startup" by Ramy
Adeeb (https://1984.vc/docs/founders-handbook/mergers-and-acquisitions/how-to-sell). This file is a derived
work; credit 1984 Ventures and the author, and do not present it as original. The
handbook's own disclaimer applies: general education, not legal advice.

Startups are bought, not sold. But a founder can set the company up for a successful
exit by thinking ahead and approaching M&A with the same methodical execution applied
to product or engineering. Seven steps.

## 1. Decide on using a banker

Bankers can build the pipeline and manage negotiations, but their involvement can send
an unwanted signal, and handling it yourself beats a subpar banker.

When bankers add value:

- Series C or later with significant revenue (over $50M ARR).
- A competitive process with multiple potential acquirers, or a need to reach strategic
  buyers outside your immediate network.
- Deal complexity that requires dedicated financial modeling and transaction support.

When bankers may be a negative signal:

- Early stage (Seed to Series B), where relationships matter more than process.
- You already have strong relationships with potential acquirers.
- You are in preliminary discussions with a single interested party.

If you do use bankers, pick ones with specific expertise in your sector who bring deep
industry relationships, not just process.

## 2. Build relationships

- List potential acquirers. They fall into buckets: larger competitors, partners and
  customers of your products, and larger companies threatened by your competitors. The
  third bucket, while less obvious, can be one of the most strategic outcomes.
- Start building relationships well before you need to get acquired. Think a year or
  two. With only 3 to 6 months of cash left, you end up with a fire sale for pennies.
- Build them with the right decision makers: a CEO, GM, or CXO level executive. Do not
  waste time with Corp Dev teams; they execute M&A decisions but do not initiate them.
- Consider partnerships that could lead to acquisition.

Sizing reality check: companies typically spend at most 1 to 2% of their market cap on
an M&A transaction. A competitor publicly trading at $8B can probably spend $100 to
$150M at the larger end.

## 3. Start the conversation

- Schedule a casual 1:1 coffee or lunch with the senior executive (not Corp Dev), framed
  as a catch-up or partnership discussion, not an acquisition pitch.
- Share recent successes and growth trajectory, plus partnership success stories if you
  are partners, then transition naturally into how your solutions could complement their
  strategy.
- Keep it exploratory: "exploring strategic options" or "evaluating potential deeper
  partnerships", never a direct statement that you want to sell.
- Ask about their build-vs-buy strategy in your space and their take on recent industry
  consolidation.
- If you already have other interest: "I value our partnership and wanted you to know
  we've received some inbound interest from a strategic and/or competitor. We're still
  in the exploratory phase, but given our relationship, I wanted you to be the first to
  know." Be prepared to decline questions about who the acquirer is and at what price.

Never appear desperate or indicate that you need to sell. Frame the acquisition as a
strategic opportunity for both parties, never a necessity for either.

## 4. Position around the combined value

Talent, revenue, or product acquisitions are some of the less valuable M&A outcomes. The
highest value comes from positioning the acquisition as a strategic threat or a
defensive move.

Tell a compelling "1+1=11" story: how your product drives massive revenue through the
acquirer's existing customer base, how your technology transforms their core business,
how your team accelerates their strategic initiatives. Paint transformative potential,
not current metrics.

If your target is a large strategic, consider quietly engaging with that company's top
competitor. Letting it "slip" that you are in conversation creates urgency and FOMO.

## 5. Understand and drive the deal structure

- Prepare the company for sale, including a dataroom (see the handbook's Legal
  Considerations for M&A chapter).
- Decide whether an asset acquisition, stock acquisition, or full merger is the right
  structure (see the Structuring M&A chapter).
- Think beyond cash: timing of payments, earnouts, title and role (see the Terms to
  Negotiate in an M&A Transaction chapter).
- Always get a second bidder. Once you receive an LOI, immediately prioritize a second
  term sheet. Reach out to partners and potential acquirers and tell them you have
  received an acquisition offer. Keep the acquirer's identity confidential, though you
  may indicate a general price range or hint at competition.

## 6. Convince your investors

Investors may resist a sale they see as below the company's worth. Use a mix of fear and
greed: fear of the standalone outcome, greed about the acquirer's stock potential in a
stock acquisition.

Approval mechanics: if you have raised only through SAFEs, you can generally execute the
deal without investor approval, since you control the common shares and the board. Once
you have raised a priced round, you typically need both board approval and majority
approval from preferred shareholders.

Do not screw over your investors. Top VCs will not care whether they get 1x or 1.5x, but
they will remember how you handled the process, and you may need them to vouch for you
on your next venture.

## 7. Post-deal considerations

The stretch between term sheet and definitive documentation is typically the most
demanding phase of a founder's life: expect 4 to 8 weeks of around-the-clock work on
everything from a missing NDA for a consultant hired five years ago to replacing an open
source module the acquirer deems too risky.

Prepare the team. Their two biggest questions are how much will I make and is my job
secure. Address job security and culture change, set expectations for the new operating
world, and plan layoff conversations carefully if the transaction includes them.

Lead them through it. The acquirer may keep the team and culture intact or absorb it
entirely; most say the former and do the latter. Either way, make sure your team is well
integrated and not isolated. It is better for their careers.

## Common pitfalls

- Do not neglect the core business during the process. Ideally only your co-founder
  knows at early stage (or your CFO and a handful of key people at late stage). The news
  spreads like wildfire and effectively pauses the company's progress.
- Do not wait until you are forced to sell.
