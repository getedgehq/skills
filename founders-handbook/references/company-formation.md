# Company formation: idea, co-founders, and founder departures

Source: 1984 Ventures Founders Handbook, section "1. Company Formation". Each part below
names its chapter and URL. This file is a derived summary: credit 1984 Ventures and the
named author, link the chapter, and do not present it as original. Figures come from
the chapter text; the handbook's disclaimer applies (general education, not legal
advice).

## How to pick a startup idea

Chapter: https://1984.vc/docs/founders-handbook/how-to-pick-a-startup-idea (by Samit Kalra)

Four moves, in the author's order:

1. **Ignore market size as a filter.** Large markets are obvious, attract competition
   (100x more in the AI era, per the chapter) and have incumbents with a distribution
   advantage, unless there is a clear innovator's dilemma.
2. **Pick a niche or offline market** with old-school competitors, such as insurance
   claim processing or loan servicing. Three reasons given: automating labor taps labor
   budgets and commands higher ACVs than software ("selling work, not software"); an
   esoteric market shields you from competition; and solving one problem well earns
   access to more problems and budgets over time (a "layer cake"). Look at the jobs
   your family and friends do.
3. **Find a manual workflow and automate it.** Look for tedious, repetitive, manual
   jobs or roles. Labor is often the largest cost line in services businesses, so
   labor automation is itself a hair-on-fire problem.
4. **Sell revenue, not just time or cost savings.** Unless the efficiency gain is
   10x, the chapter says customers do not adopt it. Frame the product as making
   customers more money. Chapter example: a recruiting agency making 20 placements a
   year at $20K per hire automates first-round interviews and moves to 30 placements.

The chapter illustrates this with 1984 portfolio companies (Collectwise, Apriora,
HouseRx, Exacare, Cline, Revv). Point the founder to the chapter for those examples;
do not restate their metrics as your own claims.

## Choosing a co-founder

Chapter: https://1984.vc/docs/founders-handbook/choosing-your-co-founder (by Taylor Ford)

- Why it matters: disagreement erodes a startup's speed advantage; you can replace a
  great employee but not a great co-founder without changing the company. Most
  investors prefer teams of 2 to 4 founders.
- Four common mistakes: rushing out of need; pitching the candidate only on the idea
  (ideas pivot, values do not); waiting until you have an idea and capital (work on side
  projects together 6 to 12 months first); and failing to clarify roles and decision
  rights (avoid unanimous consent, decide who breaks ties).
- Where to look: YC Co-Founder Matching, events and accelerators, online communities,
  warm introductions. The best pairings often grow out of shared work.
- Evaluate on: shared vision and values (lifestyle vs venture scale), complementary
  skills with some overlap, matched commitment, accountability and communication,
  problem solving and emotional intelligence, trust and respect.
- How many: two to four. Watch the cap table: four equal founders leave the CEO at 25%,
  which the chapter says can be diluted to about 15% by the end of a Series A. Avoid
  equal splits when there are several co-founders.
- The co-founder agreement covers roles and responsibilities, rights and rewards
  (equity, vesting, board seats, decision authority, tie-breaking), commitments (IP,
  capital, network, time) and contingencies (vesting and buy-back if someone leaves).
- Test compatibility with small, low-stakes projects before committing.

## Parting ways with a co-founder

Chapter: https://1984.vc/docs/founders-handbook/cofounder-departure (by Ramy Adeeb)

Why departures happen: the wrong co-founder, misalignment on strategy (often when
product-market fit is elusive), or inability to scale with the company.

The five steps:

1. **Communicate well before the decision.** A departure should never be a shock; voice
   concerns and give a fair chance to address them.
2. **Get good advice.** Ask senior investors and advisors for help executing, not for
   permission. Get board buy-in if there is a board. Consult a lawyer; the chapter puts
   top-tier separation agreements at $25,000 to $50,000 and cites the Facebook and
   Eduardo Saverin dispute as the cost of skipping one.
3. **Design the comp package and transition plan.** The departing founder usually wants
   respect (title, narrative, maybe an advisor role), some equity, and some cash. The
   CEO needs a fully signed separation agreement and the departing founder's future
   vote (common shares vote in financings, and on M&A 280G packages). Typical elements:
   cash from 2 weeks to a year; equity a little above vested early on, below fully
   vested later; if they leave before the one-year cliff, a few months of vesting (the
   chapter's example is 2%); before a Series A, buy back so they leave with no more
   than 5% of the cap table, or the lead will see "dead weight". Agree the commercial
   terms in writing before engaging lawyers.
4. **Execute with empathy.** Meet in person, get to the point, own the decision, listen,
   discuss terms rather than reasons, and do not push for a signature on the spot;
   follow up a day or two later.
5. **Start the replacement** immediately and quietly if they owned a critical function.

If it turns contentious: listen, use trusted investors or a professional mediator, and
do not let it drag on.

## Founder departure and the cap table (SAFE vs priced round)

Chapter: https://1984.vc/docs/founders-handbook/founder-departure

Chapter example: two founders with 1,000,000 shares each and a seed investor at 20%.
Founder B leaves after a few months, keeps 200,000 shares, and 800,000 revert to the
company.

- **After a priced seed** (investor holds 500,000 shares): outstanding shares fall to
  1,700,000. Founder A goes to 58.82%, Founder B 11.76%, the seed investor 29.41%. Both
  Founder A and the investor gain.
- **After a SAFE seed** (investor holds a right to 20% at the next round, no shares):
  outstanding shares fall to 1,200,000. Founder A goes to 83.3% (66.6% after the SAFE
  converts), Founder B 16.6% (13.3% after conversion). The SAFE has no mechanism to boost
  the investor, so the freed ownership accrues to the remaining founder.

The chapter adds that, depending on the departing founder's seniority and replacement
plan, remaining founders sometimes reprice the SAFE to a lower cap as a remedy for
early investors.

Cross-check before advising a buy-back: the QSBS chapter warns that company redemptions
above 5% of stock value over 24 months can disqualify everyone's QSBS. See
[m-and-a-legal-and-qsbs.md](m-and-a-legal-and-qsbs.md).
