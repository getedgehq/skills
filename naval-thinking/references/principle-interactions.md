# When good principles point in different directions

This is an original decision guide synthesized from the source concepts in [wealth.md](wealth.md), [judgment.md](judgment.md), [freedom.md](freedom.md), and [values-and-growth.md](values-and-growth.md). These resolutions are not quotations or instructions attributed to Naval. Read the tension that applies rather than the entire list mechanically.

## Ownership versus staying solvent

Ownership can uncouple returns from time; dependable income can fund the time needed to build it. The decisive variable is whether the next stage can survive long enough to produce useful evidence.

When demand is weak and savings are thin, preserve income and test the opportunity. When demand, retention, and delivery are demonstrated and limited time is the constraint, reduce employment or consulting deliberately. Compare the intermediate option before assuming the choice is all or nothing. Reverse course if remaining cash no longer covers the next learning stage and an essential buffer.

## Leverage versus judgment

Replication multiplies the consequences of a decision. If quality is uncertain, improve and test the process before multiplying it. If delivery is reliable and labor is the binding constraint, reusable systems can help. If buyer access is the constraint, automation may make unused capacity cheaper without generating revenue.

Locate the bottleneck across demand, acquisition, delivery, support, and retention. Re-evaluate after changing it; the bottleneck may move.

## Compounding versus pivoting

Compounding requires an asset whose usefulness actually accumulates. Persist while evidence shows learning, repeat business, or durable trust improving. Pivot when repeated evidence contradicts the thesis and the next action cannot repair the cause affordably.

Ask what transfers: domain knowledge, customer understanding, relationships, tools, and reputation may survive a role change. A new title does not necessarily reset the important asset. Distinguish a temporary interruption from a structurally weakening trajectory.

## Specific knowledge versus learning something new

An existing advantage gives a starting position, not a lifetime boundary. If the adjacent skill unlocks a buyer's problem, learn it or partner for it. If the move is driven only by fashion and discards a demonstrated advantage, investigate before committing.

The key evidence is useful output at the intersection of old and new abilities. Avoid treating curiosity as sufficient demand or past success as immunity to change.

## Long-term people versus accountability

Repeated cooperation reduces friction when commitments remain credible. Patience can preserve valuable collaboration during an openly communicated temporary difficulty. It becomes costly when it normalizes broken promises or unsupported assurances.

Inspect the prior record, early communication, realism of agreements, willingness to repair, and effect on others. Adjust capacity and dependencies for a temporary problem; change the arrangement for repeated unresolved failure. Do not infer motive from a missed date alone.

## Focus versus exploration

Focus concentrates learning and execution after a direction deserves commitment. Exploration discovers that direction. If buyer, problem, or fit is unknown, bounded exploration can prevent years of concentrated error. If evidence is strong, adding unrelated projects may dilute the advantage.

Name the unanswered question and the cost of another branch. Stop exploring when the available evidence justifies a commitment, not only when uncertainty disappears.

## Ambition versus peace

Ambition can buy security, craft, or autonomy while also accumulating demands. A meaningful goal may be worth discomfort; a borrowed goal may not deliver the desired benefit even when achieved.

Compare ordinary life under the options and let the user choose the tradeoff. If the actual limitation is financial insecurity or inflexible work, changing circumstances may be appropriate. If the material goalpost keeps moving despite needs being met, examine comparison and expectations alongside the next achievement.

## Authenticity versus market feedback

Authenticity can sustain distinctive work; buyers still decide whether it is useful. Preserve the interests and values that matter while adapting the product, packaging, or distribution. Avoid using identity to defend a rejected offer.

If feedback consistently identifies a solvable adjacent problem, that can be a better expression of the user's expertise than their original product. If meeting demand would violate a chosen value, say so and evaluate a different market instead of pretending the conflict disappears.

## Saying no versus reversible learning

A cautious no can protect time from optional durable commitments. A reversible test can make an uncertain choice better informed. The relevant variables are irreversibility, cost, deadline, and the information a test will produce.

Do not decline every uncertain opportunity or test one that will teach nothing. Some commitments cannot be piloted; then compare plausible outcomes, required safeguards, and the cost of waiting.

## Freedom versus responsibility

External freedom concerns options and control of time; internal freedom concerns how compulsively one reacts. A business may create ownership while reducing control of daily life. Employment may finance independence while restricting hours. Either can be preferred under the user's constraints.

Define the actual freedom sought. Check agreements and dependencies before acting. Mental reframing cannot replace a schedule change that the user's obligations genuinely require.
