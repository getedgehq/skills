# Judgment under uncertainty

Source ideas below are paraphrases. Application procedures are this skill's synthesis. Printed pages refer to the [official Almanack](https://navalmanack.s3.amazonaws.com/Eric-Jorgenson_The-Almanack-of-Naval-Ravikant_Final.pdf).

## Reality before identity

**Source idea:** Desire and identity can distort perception; understanding basic mechanisms matters more than memorizing sophisticated conclusions. Book: “How to Think Clearly,” pp. 95-100; “Shed Your Identity to See Reality,” pp. 101-103.

**Apply:** Separate observations, interpretations, and preferences. Ask what a neutral observer would see in the same evidence. Identify the belief that makes the preferred story work and the observation that would overturn it. Translate labels such as founder, contrarian, or successful into actual behavior and outcomes.

Raise the evidence bar when a conclusion flatters the user or conveniently excuses an obligation. Apply the same scrutiny to your own preferred recommendation. Leave room for deliberation rather than treating a full calendar as proof of progress.

**Limits:** Disagreeing with consensus does not establish independent thought. Conviction and confidence do not substitute for evidence. A person's values are legitimate inputs, even when a choice is not financially optimal.

## Make incentives visible

**Source idea:** Principal-agent conflicts and economic incentives help explain behavior. Book: “Collect Mental Models,” especially pp. 108-109.

**Apply:** Map who decides, who pays, who receives the upside, and who bears failure. Evaluate a recommendation in light of the recommender's incentives. Ask whether success for one participant requires harm to another or whether useful value can grow for both.

**Limits:** Incentives explain possibilities, not hidden motives with certainty. Equity can align some incentives while creating concentration risk or conflict over timelines.

## Match deliberation to commitment

**Source idea:** Long-lived commitments deserve careful selection; short-term discomfort can conceal a valuable long-term choice. Book: “Learn the Skills of Decision-Making,” pp. 103-105; decision heuristics, pp. 111-113.

**Apply:** Distinguish a reversible experiment from an expensive commitment. If options look similar, isolate the missing fact before extending the debate. Investigate whether hesitation comes from poor fit, missing evidence, or fear of useful effort. Give a decision deadline when continued analysis has a cost.

**Limits:** Uncertainty does not automatically mean no. Short-term pain does not prove long-term benefit. Do not mechanically apply a slogan about saying no to medical needs, basic obligations, or decisions with unavoidable uncertainty.

## Keep the ability to try again

**Source idea:** Avoid catastrophic loss while pursuing opportunities with substantial upside. Book: pp. 66-67.

**Apply:** State the failure budget in cash, time, reputation, or health. Identify correlated risks and irreversible downside. Prefer a smaller test when it can answer the same question. Specify an exit or review condition before emotional investment grows.

Use inversion when it helps: what would reliably make this fail, and does the plan already contain that condition? Describe a plausible adverse scenario instead of relying on one forecast. These procedures are an operational synthesis of the book's mental models, not a quoted workflow.

**Limits:** A favorable upside story does not establish positive expected value. Do not assume all risk is measurable or that all losses can be recovered.

## Learn for use, not display

**Source idea:** Mental models compress experienced understanding; curiosity and strong foundations support learning. Book: pp. 106-114, 114-120.

**Apply:** Choose a model only if it explains a causal feature of this decision. State its prediction or practical implication. For learning plans, connect reading to an actual problem, worked example, or piece of work; use difficulty to expose gaps in foundational understanding.

**Limits:** Reading more is not automatically progress. A model from another field may fail here. Do not introduce economics, evolution, or game theory merely to make advice sound authoritative.
