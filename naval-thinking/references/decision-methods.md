# Methods for consequential decisions

These methods are original applications of the cited source ideas. They are not a canonical Naval algorithm. Choose the relevant method and adapt it to the stakes and facts. Calculations are descriptive tools, not forecasts or personalized investment recommendations.

## Career or business transition

1. State the ordinary-life outcome sought, not just the job title. Identify non-negotiable obligations and contractual constraints.
2. Compare present work, partial transition, and full transition when these options are available. Include learning, market access, time, and cash consequences.
3. Separate buyer evidence into interest, payment, continued use, and retention. State the actual cohort and observation period; a waitlist is not paid demand and an early cohort is not a durable trend.
4. Find the binding constraint. Extra build time matters only if time is preventing a demonstrated opportunity from advancing. More features may not repair weak acquisition or an irrelevant problem.
5. Choose the commitment level that matches the evidence and failure budget. Specify what would justify increasing or decreasing it.

If demand for an expansion is the decisive uncertainty, seek the cheapest credible commitment before substantial building: a paid pilot with clear delivery/refund terms, a purchase decision conditional on a demo, or another observable action the buyer must take. Do not treat requesting a feature as intent to purchase. When buyers need working proof to decide, build only the minimum demonstration required to obtain that evidence. Separate improving current retention from acquiring new buyers; they can justify different experiments.

Break-even income is one possible transition threshold, not a universal prerequisite for leaving employment. A deliberately funded growth period can be reasonable with credible evidence, a justified failure budget, and a concrete benefit from additional time. Explain the tradeoff instead of silently requiring profitability before every commitment.

Useful arithmetic, with consistent time units:

- Current monthly cash surplus = after-tax available income − personal spending − costs not already included.
- Transition monthly burn = personal spending + incremental business costs − reliable available income during transition.
- Approximate runway = liquid cash allocated to the transition ÷ positive monthly burn. Exclude cash reserved for essential obligations; identify taxes and costs still unknown. If burn is zero or negative, report that rather than a finite runway calculation.
- Delivery capacity must include sales, support, rework, and coordination. Do not count all hours outside employment as available build time.

Example: having proven buyers and a partial consulting option can justify reducing consulting sooner than a blanket rule to preserve employment. The right reduction depends on actual available cash, living costs, retention, and capacity.

For a bridge based on client offers, make the budget conditional on signed scope, payment, start date, cancellation, and collection assumptions. Recalculate for early termination and the stated contract end. Do not describe an offered duration as guaranteed income, even after recommending that the terms be checked.

Source concepts: ownership and leverage, pp. 53-69; focus, pp. 69-75; career alternatives, pp. 77-82; avoiding ruin, pp. 66-67.

## Compensation and ownership

Compare dependable cash, actual decision rights, learning, and uncertain upside separately. State vesting, dilution, exercise costs, deadlines, liquidity, concentration, and what is unknown when they matter. Do not convert an optimistic acquisition story into today's compensation.

An illustrative exit share = ownership fraction at exit × proceeds attributable to that class, only under the explicit assumptions about dilution, preferences, vesting, and participation. Do not substitute enterprise valuation for distributable proceeds. Subtract exercise costs when relevant; taxes and timing may materially change the result. If the needed facts are unavailable, show an explicitly simplified scenario and identify the missing terms rather than inventing them.

Distinguish cash needed to exercise from savings available today. A smaller nominal stake with credible liquidity can differ greatly from a larger illiquid claim. Employment plus independent assets may build freedom too.

Source concepts: accountability and ownership, pp. 50-54; judgment, pp. 67-69, 103-111. Contract analysis and these calculations are this skill's synthesis.

## Scale an existing offer

Map the entire customer path. Calculate current revenue and delivery work, then distinguish revenue, contribution, and profit. For the next increment of scale, include recurring service costs, acquisition, quality control, support, rework, and coordination.

- Recurring revenue = active paying customers × price per period, when pricing is uniform.
- Contribution per customer = price − variable fulfillment and support costs, before fixed costs.
- Acquisition payback = acquisition cost ÷ positive contribution per period, under stable retention and cost assumptions. Revenue alone is not a valid denominator for a profitability claim.

Do not infer lifetime value from a few months of data. Inspect dependence on one customer, platform, channel, or operator. Ask what growth does to that dependency.

If automation is the candidate intervention, test it against representative work including difficult cases. Count total reviewed time and corrections, not only machine runtime. Compare against the customer's actual standard and choose expansion based on observed capacity. If distribution is at risk, repairing acquisition can dominate cheaper delivery.

Source concepts: leverage, pp. 55-69; incentives and prediction limits, pp. 108-111.

## Partnership, team, or repeated cooperation

Describe concrete commitments and effects before judging character. Compare the prior record with the recent change. Inspect communication, capacity, incentives, authority, and willingness to repair. Distinguish emergency overload, unrealistic planning, lack of skill, and repeated unresolved unreliability; these require different actions.

Protect a threatened customer or other urgent dependency first. A fair reset names a feasible result, owner, resources, date, escalation rule, and review point. If such a reset already failed, another identical trial supplies little new information. Consider role changes, complementary support, or a transition consistent with agreements.

Source concepts: long-term cooperation, pp. 46-50; accountability, pp. 50-52; incentives, pp. 108-109.

## Personal direction and autonomy

Translate dissatisfaction into specific needs, preferences, comparisons, and constraints. Compare an ordinary week under each path. Determine whether the bottleneck is material circumstances, an agreement, a habit or environment, or an expectation. Multiple causes can coexist.

Evaluate change, acceptance, and departure without making acceptance mandatory. A personal experiment should target the actual cause: protecting time cannot repair insufficient income; reflection cannot create employer permission. Measure the behavior or experience the user cares about, not a generic happiness score unless they choose it.

Source concepts: desire and comparison, pp. 137-145; habits and acceptance, pp. 145-155; freedom, pp. 187-192.

## A reusable decision record

For a requested deep review or follow-up, offer a compact record in the conversation: choice and deadline; objective and constraints; supplied facts and missing information; serious alternatives; decisive mechanism and bottleneck; current recommendation; downside limit; next action and reversal condition.

On follow-up, distinguish genuinely new evidence from a changed preference. Recalculate affected assumptions and explain whether the conclusion changes. Do not force consistency with prior advice when the facts changed. Writing a persistent record requires the user's authorization and the appropriate storage workflow; ordinary answers need no new file.
