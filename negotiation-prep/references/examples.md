# Worked Examples and Anti-Patterns

All examples are illustrative. Names, companies and figures are invented.

## Example 1: Information Leakage (salary offer)

BAD: "I'd like $128,000. I also have an offer at $120,000 elsewhere, so anything above that works for me."
- Reveals the BATNA and, with it, the floor. The employer now only has to beat $120,000.

GOOD: "Based on the scope of the role and what I'd bring in the first year, I'm looking for $128,000."
- Anchors on value to them, not on your alternative.

## Example 2: Self-Undermining Concession Framing (vendor contract)

BAD: "We already came down from a 3-year term to 1 year, so the 12% discount is the least you can do."
- If you are also arguing that a 1-year term was always the plan, this sentence contradicts you and makes the "concession" look invented.

GOOD: "A 1-year term with a 12% discount works for us, with an option to extend on the same terms."
- States the structure without re-anchoring on a number you do not want to defend.

## Example 3: Trap Detection, "Protection until [event]" (partnership terms)

TRAP: "We can offer exclusivity protection until the first product launch."
- The launch is exactly when a competing partner would become a threat.
- Protection that ends at the moment you need it is no protection.

COUNTER: "Exclusivity that ends at launch protects us until the point where it starts to matter. Let's tie it to 12 months after launch instead."

## Example 4: Sweetener Distraction (vendor contract)

TRAP: "We'll increase the volume discount from 8% to 10%, but list prices may be adjusted annually."
- The extra 2% looks generous.
- Without a cap on list-price increases, the discount can be wiped out in the first renewal.

COUNTER: "A cap on annual price increases matters more to us than the discount. We can accept 8% with increases capped at 3% per year."
- Shows you understand the real value driver, and taking less on the headline number makes the ask hard to call greedy.

## Example 5: False Equivalence (cloud billing dispute)

TRAP: "We'll credit 20% of last month's invoice, which covers the outage."
- Merges two separate items: the outage credit owed under the SLA, and the overage charges caused by the provider's misconfigured autoscaling.

COUNTER: Separate the items. "The SLA credit and the overage are two separate issues. Here is the SLA calculation, and here are the overage line items from the misconfiguration, with the support ticket that confirms the cause."
- Only cite figures you can show from the invoice, the SLA text and the ticket.

## Example 6: "Standard" Claims (vendor contract)

TRAP: "Uncapped liability for the customer is standard in our industry."
- "Standard" without a source is an assertion.
- It is often applied selectively: standard when it helps them, not when it helps you.

COUNTER: Don't argue about what is standard. Argue the substance: "Uncapped liability exposes us to risk out of proportion to the contract value. We propose a cap at 12 months of fees."

## Example 7: Emotional Reframing (partnership terms)

TRAP: "This isn't about you or us, it's about making the partnership work."
- Recasts a legitimate commercial interest as selfishness.
- Any pushback now looks like you are hurting the partnership.

COUNTER: Accept the frame and use it: "Agreed, and a partnership works best when both sides know exactly what they are committing to. That's why I want the revenue split in writing."

## Example 8: Timing Pressure (any)

TRAP: A conciliatory message in the afternoon, then a harder position late in the evening after "checking with legal", with a request to confirm by morning.
- Late messages create urgency.
- The shift after outside consultation tells you they have been advised, not that the deadline is real.

COUNTER: Don't match the urgency. Reply when ready with a calm, structured proposal, and address the change in position directly.
