# Strategic narrative method

Based on Andy Raskin's public method. Not affiliated with or endorsed by Andy Raskin.

## Scope and attribution

Raskin treats strategic narrative as a buyer-centered story that aligns leadership, sales, marketing, product, and other functions. Its central question is how buyers win after a meaningful change in their world. The company's capabilities support the buyer's journey. Building the story around superiority claims misses this mechanism. [S1][S6][S9][S14]

This package combines his published narrative sequence with later clarifications. Older articles discuss an enemy through tools associated with an obsolete world; the later enemy post explicitly identifies the old mindset as the antagonist. Use that clarification when interpreting the earlier examples. The buyer mission is his later articulation of the desirable future earlier called the promised land. [S1][S5][S7]

Practical conventions in this package include evidence labels, a five-part storyboard, compact word targets, review gates, and discovery prompts. They operationalize the method; they are not claimed to be Raskin's official template. Public sources substantiate method claims, not the effectiveness or market claims of a future user's business.

## Discovery and evidence

Begin with buyers and the circumstances that made a purchase matter. In Raskin's Foxglove example, customer conversations revealed that robotics companies were shifting from proving a concept in a lab to scaling deployments in production. The software did not cause that shift; the shift made the software relevant. [S4]

Useful discovery prompts, written for this package:

- What happened in your business that made the previous approach inadequate?
- What used to count as winning, and what counts as winning now?
- What would happen if you continued operating as before?
- What outcome matters enough to justify changing, and what blocks it?
- Which peers demonstrate the new mindset, and what evidence can we inspect?

Record the speaker, context, date, and whether their observation generalizes beyond one account. Separate facts from interpretations. Buyer statements can suggest a shift without proving that an entire market has changed. Seek independent supporting evidence or narrow the story to the segment for which evidence exists.

Use three working labels: verified fact, hypothesis, and `[needs source]`. These are package conventions. A compelling draft with unverified foundations remains a hypothesis. Do not fabricate proof to pass the method's change test. Do not treat Raskin's historical client examples as current evidence for the user's market.

## The shift

A strong opening names a relevant change in the buyer's world. Raskin specifies three conditions: it is actually happening, it would happen without the seller, and it creates stakes. All three matter. A technological development with no consequence for this buyer is insufficient; a seller-created workflow improvement is insufficient too. [S1]

Express the shift as old winning mindset to new winning mindset. Compact names help people repeat the story. A target of roughly one to four words per name is a drafting heuristic here, not a strict requirement. The supporting explanation must say why the rules of success changed. [S4][S10]

Decision rules:

- If the new game disappears when the product is removed from the explanation, you probably have a product before/after.
- If the opener could fit any company in the sector, find the specific buyer consequence or reduce its scope.
- If the old approach still works for the chosen audience, do not pretend it is obsolete. Reconsider the audience or the proposed change.
- If a useful change name already has recognition, consider adopting it. Raskin's Uberflip analysis endorses considering established language rather than spending months inventing a term. [S2]
- Generic AI-world messaging is already undifferentiated in Raskin's 2026 commentary. Identify what changed about the buyer's winning mindset beyond the presence of AI. [S11]

Open with shared context rather than accusing prospects of operating incorrectly. Raskin describes how Uberflip's earlier pitch labeled the prospect's approach defective and put people on the defensive. The revised story opened a conversation about change. [S2]

## Stakes and enemy

Show the positive and negative paths created by the shift. What do successful adapters achieve? What becomes costly or dangerous about persisting with the old mindset? Raskin's Uberflip analysis uses winners and losers to make the cost of remaining with the status quo visible. [S2]

Choose evidence close enough to the buyer's situation to support the inference. An industry analogy can clarify the logic but cannot prove a customer's likely outcome. Describe a concrete business consequence, not an unsupported certainty of collapse. If no verified winner is available, mark the evidence gap and describe the proposed mechanism as a hypothesis.

The enemy is an old mindset. It is not the list of competing vendors and is not simply a disconnected operational problem. Naming the old game may already do the work of naming the enemy. Alternatives can be explained as designed around the old assumptions, but that factual claim must be supportable. [S5]

Raskin's positioning argument favors commitment to the new mindset over generic claims of being better. Treat his aspirational exclusivity language as positioning guidance, not permission to assert that the company is the only provider without evidence. [S6]

Where truthful, include the seller among people who previously believed in the old mindset. Raskin notes that Drift's leaders made the journey shared, reducing the accusatory quality of the pitch. Avoid invented founder histories. [S1]

## Buyer mission

Define what winning looks like for buyers. This is a goal state, not installing software, owning a platform, or choosing a vendor. It should be desirable and sufficiently difficult that outside help has a meaningful role. [S1]

Raskin's Cinderella tradeoff addresses how high to aim. The most aspirational outcome is often too vague to define a movement; the most concrete feature-level outcome can be too small. Choose the highest-level goal that still defines and differentiates the movement. There is no universal correct level. Higher-order benefits can appear in longer customer stories without bloating the core mission. [S7]

Use these decision tests:

1. Can a buyer explain the outcome without naming our product?
2. Does the outcome distinguish the mindset we champion from a plausible alternative?
3. Would it help the company prioritize features or decline an irrelevant opportunity?
4. Could a reasonable buyer reject it because they prefer another goal or philosophy?
5. Is the promise bounded by what the company can credibly help achieve?

Raskin's later discussion of a disputable purpose supports a mission narrow enough for reasonable disagreement. Do not soften it until everyone agrees and it no longer guides decisions. This is specificity, not an instruction to manufacture provocation. This package relies only on Raskin's own public application of that idea, not the book passage reproduced in the source. [S12]

A question can introduce the desired future and invite discussion; Raskin discusses this in the Uberflip analysis. Choose declarative or question form for the setting rather than treating a grammatical form as mandatory. [S2]

## Obstacles and gifts

After the mission, explain what prevents buyers from achieving it. Obstacles must make the difficulty credible. They can include existing constraints made more consequential by the shift; do not insist that every obstacle was newly invented by it. Raskin specifically critiques obstacle slides that fail to explain why reaching the future is hard without help. [S2]

Map capabilities to these obstacles. The metaphor of gifts means features receive purpose within the journey. It does not mean remove features or replace demonstrations with slogans. Raskin's Drift analysis shows product capabilities introduced as tools for overcoming specific barriers. [S1]

For each mapping, write the obstacle, its consequence, the capability, how it helps, and a boundary. If a capability serves no mission obstacle, omit it from the narrative. If an obstacle has no credible answer, narrow the promise or flag the gap. Human services, process changes, and buyer effort can be necessary alongside software. A narrative must not imply the product alone guarantees the entire outcome.

## Proof

Raskin favors customer stories in the customer's voice showing that the desirable future became real. Evidence should address the mission, not merely show that someone purchased the product. His Uberflip analysis places customer examples and then demonstrations after relevance has been established, with credentials optionally appearing there. [S1][S2]

When customer stories are unavailable, demonstrate the relevant capability or show internal use results, explicitly identified as internal. Drift's early feature launch used its own team's results. Internal experience is not equivalent to repeatable external customer success. [S1]

Maintain outcome boundaries: the audience, starting situation, achieved result, time period, and limits on attribution. A logo alone cannot establish a mission-level outcome. Unverified quotes stay placeholders; demo results stay demo results. These evidence-handling rules are package safeguards for applying the method honestly.

## Audience adaptation and edge cases

Keep the relevant external change as the common narrative anchor, then adapt the desired future, obstacles, and evidence to the audience. Raskin warns that unrelated stories for each segment leave the brand without a coherent entry message and make it harder for buyers to persuade colleagues. A cross-segment view can connect the shared change to local consequences. [S3]

- **Investors:** Keep the change, then address the prospect of returns and barriers to belief: market size, business model, and team. Add investor-required financial detail in the requested format. Do not substitute an ownership ambition for the customer's underlying mission. [S3]
- **Multiple buying roles:** Show how the change affects each role and how their missions connect. Avoid asking users to advocate a story their budget owner cannot understand. [S3]
- **Marketplaces:** Decide which side the entry message addresses. Raskin explicitly says he has found no universal rule; consider which side is needed and which can attract the other. Treat this as a contextual decision. [S3]
- **Pivots:** Seek a genuine umbrella for old and new audiences. If the pivot is too large, acknowledge the break instead of forcing a false connection. [S3]
- **Little evidence:** Deliver a clearly labeled hypothesis storyboard plus an evidence plan. Do not silently turn a proposed market shift into an established fact.
- **Homepage or keynote:** Establish the compact narrative first, then adapt the presentation. Preserve the requested channel rather than requiring the user to commission a deck. Raskin recommends slides as a forcing function for concise thinking, even when slides will not be shown. [S10]
- **Leadership disagreement:** Explain the implications of alternatives. Raskin puts responsibility for the top message with the CEO and expresses discomfort with A/B testing at that strategic level, while accepting A/B testing for messaging generally. This is not a ban on learning from buyers. [S8]

## Explicit warnings and revision

Raskin explicitly warns against interpreting the framework as a streamlined-product before/after, attacking competitors as the enemy, dressing superiority claims in storytelling, defining an overly vague mission, fragmenting the story by audience, and allowing prose to retain too many ideas. [S3][S4][S5][S6][S7][S10]

His published examples also warn against seller-centric openings, accusing buyers of flawed behavior, presenting capabilities before their context, and treating credentials as the first argument. His later AI commentary adds a warning about generic AI-era messaging. [S1][S2][S11]

Draft compactly, then seek comprehension and objections in buyer conversations. The Foxglove account reports real prospect conversations following the change-first opener; it does not establish a universal number of calls or a guaranteed conversion gain. [S4]

Revise the causal chain, not only the wording: change creates stakes, stakes motivate a mission, obstacles explain needed help, and evidence makes that help credible. Drop pieces leadership cannot truthfully stand behind. Raskin describes supporting a team's rejection of a narrative element that did not feel true enough; collaboration and alignment matter more than preserving the consultant's preferred line. [S13]
