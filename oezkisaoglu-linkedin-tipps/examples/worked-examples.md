## Illustration 1: A manufacturing CEO turns a bland announcement into a useful tip

### Brief and supplied evidence

Invented company: Havel Precision, a component supplier. Audience: production managers considering inspection automation. Purpose: become a credible source of practical implementation guidance. Format: CEO text post. The fictional brief supplies one approved observation: an internal trial found disagreement about what counts as a reject before the team compared inspection tools. No quantitative benefit has been established.

### Weak first draft

We are proud to announce that Havel Precision is revolutionizing quality with cutting-edge AI. Our innovative team always pushes boundaries. Automation is the future. Like this post if you agree and contact us today!

### Improved result

Before comparing inspection tools, agree on what a reject means.

In our internal trial, the first obstacle was not choosing the tool. Production and quality colleagues were applying different acceptance criteria to the same parts.

That matters when you automate inspection: a system can reproduce an unclear definition without resolving the disagreement behind it.

Our next step was to collect borderline examples and agree on how they should be classified before comparing tools.

If you are preparing an inspection pilot, ask production and quality to review the same small set of borderline parts. Record where their judgments differ. Resolve those differences before treating an automated result as evidence of improvement.

This is a preparation step, not proof that any particular system will reduce defects.

Where does disagreement usually arise in your process: defect definitions, measurement conditions, or escalation decisions?

### Supporting plan

- Evidence: Use the approved internal trial note; do not add percentages or claim customer outcomes.
- Konsistenz: Quality implementation fits the CEO's expertise and the company's technical role.
- Kontinuität: Illustrative choice of two posts per month, with reply time reserved. This is not an attributed rule.
- Participation: Contribute a relevant example under a quality professional's discussion, then answer questions on the CEO's post.
- Measurement: Review target-role conversations and relevant profile activity after four weeks; no result is assumed.

### Rules that made the difference

Method sections 1 and 3 replace self-promotion with a specific reader decision and supplied evidence. Section 2 aligns the topic with expert territory. Section 7 replaces reaction bait with a question that can produce useful professional discussion. Section 8 removes an unsupported performance claim. The problem-to-action sequence is the package's drafting adaptation.

## Illustration 2: A technical leader builds a newsletter without forcing employee posts

### Brief and supplied evidence

Invented company: Nordwerk Packaging. Audience: packaging engineers and operations leaders considering recycled feedstock. Purpose: build consideration for its technical expertise. The fictional brief provides an approved laboratory observation: a smooth initial machine run did not answer questions about later sealing checks. There is no evidence about customer-scale performance. The director prefers an interview to solo video production. Two engineers voluntarily want to contribute technical notes.

### Weak first draft

Newsletter: Sustainability is everything!

Recycled materials are the future and every responsible business must act now. Our world-class team guarantees a seamless transition. Every employee should copy this article to their profile this week. We will publish daily videos and measure success by likes. Subscribe so you never miss our revolutionary products.

### Improved result

**Newsletter title:** Before scaling recycled feedstock, decide what your trial must prove

**Recurring promise:** Practical notes for packaging teams turning laboratory observations into better trial questions.

A smooth first machine run answers one question. It does not answer every question about a new material.

In our laboratory discussion, the useful turning point was separating what we had observed during the run from what still needed checking afterward, including sealing behavior.

For a team considering a material change, that distinction helps prevent an early impression from becoming a conclusion the trial was never designed to support.

Before your next trial, write down three things:

1. The observation you can record during the run.
2. The checks that require samples afterward.
3. The evidence you still need before drawing a scaling conclusion.

Then ask the people responsible for operations and quality whether the planned trial covers their decisions. If it does not, revise the plan before adding more material or more production time.

Our laboratory observation does not establish commercial-scale performance, and it does not support a universal claim about recycled feedstock. It suggests a more precise starting question: what does this trial actually allow us to conclude?

Next issue: separating material observations from machine-setting assumptions.

Which decision is hardest to support with evidence in your trials?

### Supporting plan

- Format: Gather the director's insights through an interview, then prepare a written issue for factual review.
- Employee participation: One volunteer explains sample checks in their own words. Another contributes a technical qualification. Neither receives mandatory promotional copy.
- Corporate connection: The company page links to the issue with a relevant technical introduction; the director publishes the personal explanation.
- Kontinuität: Illustrative monthly issue, reviewed after three editions. No daily-video obligation.
- Conversation: Invite relevant technical questions and assign the director responsibility for responses, supported by the engineers.
- Measurement: Review subscriber trend where available, target-role questions, relevant invitations, and business conversations. Low likes alone do not trigger abandonment; absent evidence of useful attention does not justify scaling.

### Rules that made the difference

Method sections 3 and 5 establish evidence limits and a repeatable newsletter promise. Section 4 selects a manageable format. Section 6 preserves voluntary employee voices within corporate boundaries. Section 8 replaces vanity targets with relevant signals. The newsletter title, frequency, trial questions, and structure are invented adaptations, not claimed elements of Özkisaoglu's curriculum.
