# Layered hook method

## Principles and evidence

The opening should let the right viewer choose to continue. It needs a recognizable topic and curiosity relevant to that viewer. Contrast supplies curiosity: the viewer expects A, while the opening introduces B. Surprise without topic clarity is insufficient, and a clear topic without a reason to watch can still lose attention. [S1][S3]

Kallaway's central layered model combines the visible shot, overlay text, spoken opening, and supporting sound. These should express one proposition. His practical viewing model is that viewers inspect the visual and text, hear the speech, then look for visual confirmation. Use that model as an editing heuristic, not a scientific assertion about every viewer or processing speed. [S1]

The sections below paraphrase public teaching. Production constraints and evidence safeguards are this package's adaptations. The warning section distinguishes direct transcript evidence from reports in summaries or secondary analyses. See [sources.md](sources.md) for provenance.

## Visual-first selection

Start with footage or an achievable shot rather than a polished sentence. List what can appear in the first three to five seconds: an observable transformation, side-by-side comparison, unexpected object, live demonstration, physical model, or concrete illustration. The key visual should make the idea easier to understand at a glance. Then choose the angle and hook format around it. [S1]

For each candidate ask:

- Can a viewer identify the subject on a small screen?
- Does the shot show the specific thing the opening discusses?
- What expectation does the shot challenge?
- Can the team obtain or produce it with the available resources?

The last question is an implementation safeguard. If the visual is weak, Kallaway advises considering whether the video should be made at all, or constructing a useful visual with graphics or stock footage. Do not disguise an irrelevant shot with persuasive copy. [S1]

His floor-plan example works because people walking through projected rooms visibly support the spoken description. His weaker world-model example combines unfamiliar terminology with imagery that does not clearly establish the promised concept. The lesson is to make the phenomenon concrete before broadening its significance. [S1]

## Contrast and relevance

Write the baseline and alternative before drafting:

- **A:** What this audience normally believes, expects, or does.
- **B:** What the video reveals, demonstrates, or recommends instead.
- **Question:** What will the viewer want answered after seeing that difference?

A can be a present-versus-future expectation, old-versus-new process, assumption-versus-finding, or visible before-and-after. Favor the largest relevant difference the available material can support. Kallaway's scripting summary recommends gathering surprising facts and using a subjective 1-to-100 surprise score. Treat this as a comparison aid, not a probability of virality. [S1][S4]

Use stated contrast when the baseline is uncertain: name both A and B. Use implied contrast when the shot or a shared audience assumption makes A obvious. For a broad audience, omitted context is a common source of ambiguity. [S3]

Relevance changes the framing. Explain the viewer's problem, benefit, or interest rather than announcing the creator's excitement. Second-person phrasing often helps instructional content; first person remains appropriate when a real experiment or personal result is the point. Entertainment can offer delight or surprise without inventing a practical pain point. These are flexible applications of the relevance guidance. [S3][S6]

Never enlarge contrast by inventing a result, credential, benchmark, or certainty. This package requires evidence for claims and explicit labels for simulations. A surprising claim without support needs narrower language or a different angle.

## Hook formats

Choose by visual fit and contrast mechanism, not by whichever label sounds dramatic. The six primary archetypes come from [S1].

| Format | Contrast mechanism | Choose when | Execution rule |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Fortune Teller | Present reality versus possible future | A visible innovation suggests a change | Establish today, show the mechanism, frame the future as a possibility when uncertain. |
| Experimenter | Familiar process versus tested alternative | The audience can watch the trial or demo | Start with the problem, show the attempt, explain the meaningful difference. |
| Teacher | Current difficulty versus a learnable method | A lesson can explain why a solution works | Name the difficulty and teach the method with an example or artifact. |
| Magician | Expected appearance versus an attention-catching event | A purposeful reveal or movement points toward the topic | Use as a brief entry treatment or stack with another archetype. |
| Investigator | What viewers assume or do not know versus a finding | Research reveals something concrete | Surface the finding and its significance rather than withholding the entire topic. |
| Contrarian | Conventional belief versus a different position | There is a defensible challenge to common advice | State the disagreement clearly and support it in the body. |

Experimenter is peer demonstration; Teacher extracts and explains a lesson. A discovery can fit Investigator or Fortune Teller: choose whichever makes the visible subject easier to comprehend. Magician is distinctive because it can precede another archetype. An unrelated spectacle may attract attention while weakening relevance. [S1]

Another scripting lesson uses nine labels: secret reveal, case study, comparison, question, education, list, contrarian, personal experience, and problem. These are alternative framing options, not nine mandatory additions to the six-archetype system. Researching niche outliers can inform selection, but their success does not prove that a format caused it. [S4]

## Spoken hook and word audit

Write two to four short lines. The teaching uses three functional beats: [S1][S2]

1. **Context lean:** Name the topic and create common ground through a problem, benefit, familiar comparison, or concrete fact.
2. **Scroll-stop interjection:** Redirect the expectation, often through a contrast connector.
3. **Contrarian snapback:** Reveal the alternative direction while staying on the same topic.

A reversal changes the direction of the initial expectation; merely intensifying the same claim does not supply that function. When the key visual already makes the contrast clear, a separate connector can be omitted, as in Kallaway's floor-plan example. Do not add filler solely to complete a formula. [S1][S6]

The secondary six-word account describes four core functions: subject, action, objective or resulting state, and contrast. Proof and time are optional strengthening functions. These can be phrases, and one phrase can fill multiple functions. Use the audit to locate missing meaning, not to force six literal words into every opening. [S5]

Ask who or what is involved, what happens, what outcome matters, and what expectation changes. Add proof only if genuine; add a duration only if supported. Prefer demonstrated proof over a credential recital when the demo already establishes credibility. This package treats a visible or contextual function as sufficient if adding words would reduce clarity.

Simplify with fewer words, familiar language, and active constructions. Read the opening alone and remove plausible unintended interpretations. A roughly sixth-grade reading level is a plain-language target, not a mandatory score. Clarity rewrites must retain the original meaning. [S3]

[S6] presents AI as a rewrite tool rather than a blank-prompt hook generator. However, [S3] and [S4] also describe assisted generation and hybrid drafting. Do not turn the secondary account into a universal ban. For this skill, establish audience, visual, facts, and contrast before generating language, then check every proposed claim.

## Layer alignment

| Layer | Job | Review question |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Visual | Identify and demonstrate the subject | Does the key shot support the proposition? |
| Overlay | Clarify topic, promise, or comparison | Can a muted viewer identify the idea? |
| Spoken | Explain context and the expectation change | Does the sentence describe or illuminate the shot? |
| Audio | Support emphasis and mood | Does sound help comprehension without hiding speech? |

Alignment means compatible meaning, not identical wording or illustrating every incidental word. An overlay can name the benefit while speech names the object and the shot demonstrates it. Essential claims should have visual support or clearly signaled explanation. A soundtrack cannot substitute for a readable topic. [S1]

Use restrained motion that directs attention. Too much movement competes with comprehension; too little can leave the opening inert. A familiar reference can help anchor an unfamiliar idea, but it should clarify the mechanism rather than imply an endorsement. [S2]

Overlay text carries the hook's category or promise; captions transcribe speech. Keep their roles distinct in the storyboard. Package production guidance: avoid placing essential labels under interface controls, overwhelming the frame with both text layers, or relying on sound effects for a sound-off interpretation.

## Payoff and timing

Deliver the first useful answer, demonstration, or explanatory detail immediately after the opening. The body should fulfill the question the hook creates. Do not insert an introduction or logo sequence between the promise and its first evidence. [S2][S6]

Package timing guidance: identify the topic by about two seconds, build the initial contrast within roughly five seconds when feasible, and time the complete spoken opening honestly. Two to four lines need not all fit inside three seconds. Check delivery aloud; estimate around two to three spoken words per second when no recording exists, then adjust for pauses and performance. Mark estimates rather than claiming a preview was watched.

## Diagnosis and explicit warnings

The principal transcript explicitly warns that mismatched layers create confusion, advises stopping to rework an incomprehensible hook, and questions proceeding without a useful key visual. Its weak example warns against abstract framing and unfamiliar jargon unsupported by the shot. [S1]

The fetched summaries report four diagnostic failures and these remedies: [S3]

- **Delay:** Context appears late. Move the recognizable subject into the opening frame or sentence.
- **Confusion:** Wording is hard to parse. Simplify and remove ambiguous references.
- **Irrelevance:** Viewers cannot see why the subject matters to them. Reframe around their interest or problem.
- **Disinterest:** Context and relevance exist, but curiosity does not. Introduce a supported expectation change.

The visual lesson warns against both overwhelming and insufficient motion. The secondary analysis reports warnings against attracting the wrong audience through vague tricks, memorizing formulas without understanding their purpose, and reversals whose promised answer never arrives. These are reported warnings, not independently inspected video quotations. [S2][S6]

## Edge cases and stopping rules

- **No footage yet:** Produce a shot specification with asset requirements. A conceptual sound-off review is possible; observed playback validation is not.
- **Talking head only:** Add a relevant object, comparison, sketch, or demonstrative gesture if feasible. Do not assume a talking head automatically fails; evaluate whether the topic and contrast are visible or legible.
- **Unfamiliar concept:** Introduce its concrete consequence and a familiar anchor before specialized terminology. Do not replace accurate meaning with an inaccurate analogy. [S1][S2]
- **Silent opening:** The visual and overlay carry context and contrast. Mark speech and audio as absent; review their absence rather than forcing them into the scene.
- **Personal story:** Keep first person when the experience is the evidence or premise, then make the audience's reason to care clear. [S4][S6]
- **Future claim or edited demo:** Label forecasts and simulations. Disclose time compression or material test conditions when they affect the comparison.
- **Several good angles:** Pick the clearest supported angle for the available visual. Produce alternatives only when requested or when an unresolved constraint warrants them.
- **Unfixable promise:** Narrow the claim, choose another angle, or return the missing visual or evidence requirement. Never mark a finished storyboard as passing while its essential payoff remains unavailable.

The last five operational constraints are package adaptations. They make the public method usable without inventing assets, evidence, or review results. Finish with [the review checklist](../checklists/review-checklist.md) and return [the output template](../templates/output-template.md).
