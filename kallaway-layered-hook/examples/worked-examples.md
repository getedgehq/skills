# Worked examples

Both scenarios are invented illustrations for this skill. They are not Kallaway's examples, observed performance results, or real product claims. Source citations identify the rules applied, not evidence for the fictional products or outcomes. Timings are storyboard estimates at approximately two to three spoken words per second.

## Illustration 1: A software demo for operations coordinators

### Brief and weak first draft

An imaginary scheduling tool can flag overlapping room bookings. The available asset is a screen recording of a real collision in a fictional test calendar. The audience currently checks meeting times manually.

- Visual: A logo spins, followed by a wide shot of the entire calendar interface.
- Overlay: BIG UPDATE.
- Spoken: We are thrilled to introduce our innovative scheduling enhancement that optimizes resource allocation.
- Audio: Loud rising music and an impact sound.
- Next beat: A company introduction before the interface demonstration.

The draft delays the subject, hides the relevant interaction, uses abstract language, and supplies no understandable contrast. The audio competes with speech. Its first payoff is buried. These failures map to delay, confusion, disinterest, and weak layer alignment. [S1][S2][S3]

### Apply the method

The strongest visual is a highlighted collision between two bookings in the same room. Choose **Experimenter** because the tool can demonstrate the alternative directly. A is inspecting entries manually; B is a visible overlap flag. The curiosity question is whether the flag identifies the actual conflicting booking. [S1]

Keep the claim narrow: the tool flags the overlap. Do not invent a speed benchmark or claim it prevents every scheduling error. Cropping the interface to the two relevant entries improves comprehension without altering the test result.

### Improved hook storyboard

| Time | Visual | Overlay | Spoken | Audio |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0.0 to 2.0s | Close crop of two bookings for Room A at 10:00, both readable | Same room. Same time. | These bookings clash. | Quiet room tone; no music needed. |
| 2.0 to 4.0s | Cursor points between matching times; comparison remains visible | Checking each entry? | You could check each entry. | No additional cue. |
| 4.0 to 6.5s | The overlap flag appears beside those same entries | Overlap flagged | But this tool flags the overlap. | Soft interface click at the flag. |
| 6.5 to 10.0s | Open the flag to show both booking names and their shared time | Here is the conflict | It shows both bookings and their shared time. | Speech remains clear. |

The opening contains three short spoken lines. Its visible contrast begins with the collision and resolves through the flag; the complete speech is not forced into three seconds.

**Sound-off read:** Two entries share a room and time; the tool highlights the conflict.

**First payoff:** Opening the flag exposes the exact booking pair, substantiating the claim immediately.

**Optional next beat, 10.0 to 14.0s:** Move one fictional booking to an available slot and show the collision disappearing, provided the tool actually supports that action.

### Why the result improves

- The concrete collision replaces the decorative logo and anchors all layers. [S1]
- The viewer recognizes the topic in the first frame, avoiding delayed context. [S3]
- Manual checking versus an overlap flag states the relevant contrast rather than relying on a vague claim of efficiency. [S3]
- The demo supplies proof, while the optional time slot remains omitted because no benchmark exists. [S5]
- The first body beat reveals the conflict instead of postponing value. [S2]

**Review:** Conceptual checks pass. Actual sound-off playback, small-screen readability, and audio balance remain to be checked after recording.

## Illustration 2: A planning lesson for independent designers

### Brief and weak first draft

A fictional designer teaches how to turn a long task list into a realistic daily schedule. Available visuals are a paper list and a calendar sketch. The takeaway is to assign time before committing to the entire list, not to abandon task lists altogether.

- Visual: Static talking head holding an unreadable notebook.
- Overlay: Productivity thoughts.
- Spoken: I've been thinking about productivity, and I feel like people should probably be more intentional about planning their tasks.
- Next beat: A story about the creator's morning routine.

The subject is broad, the viewer's problem is unclear, and nothing demonstrates the distinction between listing and scheduling. A more provocative but inaccurate rewrite, such as saying all task lists reduce productivity, would exaggerate the lesson rather than improve it.

### Apply the method

Choose **Contrarian**: A is treating a written list as a finished plan; B is checking the work against available calendar time. The key visual puts the long list beside a visibly limited schedule. Keep the audience's list, then show the missing planning step. This preserves the real lesson while introducing a defensible contrast. [S1][S3]

### Improved hook storyboard

| Time | Visual | Overlay | Spoken | Audio |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0.0 to 2.0s | Close shot of a task list beside a mostly occupied calendar | List versus calendar | Your task list looks doable. | Natural paper sound. |
| 2.0 to 4.0s | Draw a bracket around the two available hours | Only two hours free | But you only have two hours. | No music required. |
| 4.0 to 6.5s | Move the first task onto a 45-minute calendar block | Give tasks time | Give each task time before committing. | Pencil sound supports the movement. |
| 6.5 to 10.5s | Label two blocks: draft 45 min, revise 45 min; leave 30 min unassigned | 90 minutes planned | Drafting and revisions take ninety minutes. Keep thirty free. | Quiet background. |

These durations are hypothetical planning estimates in the fictional scenario, not promises about how long design work universally takes.

**Sound-off read:** A long list competes with two free hours; two tasks occupy ninety minutes, leaving a buffer.

**First payoff:** The viewer sees a concrete way to allocate the available time rather than hearing another abstract productivity principle.

**Optional next beat, 10.5 to 14.0s:** Mark the remaining tasks for another day or explicitly reprioritize them.

### Why the result improves

- The list and calendar make the same proposition visible that the speaker explains. [S1]
- The second-person opening connects the issue to the viewer's planning problem. [S3]
- The reversal challenges treating a list as a schedule, without inventing a universal anti-list claim. [S1]
- Subject is the viewer, action is assigning time, outcome is a realistic commitment, and contrast is list versus available hours. These functions span the lines rather than crowding one sentence. [S5]
- The body demonstrates the recommended change immediately. [S2]

**Review:** Conceptual checks pass. Production must confirm legibility of the handwritten times and a natural delivery rate.
