# Method

Based on Rory Sutherland's public method. Not affiliated with or endorsed by Rory Sutherland.

## Scope and attribution

This is a synthesis of Sutherland's public interviews, not a transcription or an official procedure from Alchemy. His central contribution is to widen the set of explanations and interventions worth exploring when human choice determines value. The LinkedIn post establishes starting context only. [S1] [S3]

Source-backed principles appear below with citations. The brief structure, hypothesis cards, experiment thresholds, and review gates are package conventions: practical ways to make that perspective usable. They are not experimental protocols prescribed by Sutherland. An interview anecdote supports a possible mechanism, not a prediction that a new business will reproduce it.

## 1. Reframe value before optimizing a metric

Distinguish the proposed improvement from the benefit it is supposed to create. A shorter journey, a lower price, or a larger feature set is a means. The relevant outcome might be confidence, enjoyment, control, or a successful appointment. Sutherland challenges the tendency to define problems through the tools and metrics of the profession assigned to solve them. [S5]

Write two sentences: the organization wants to change a measurable attribute; the customer wants to achieve a result in a particular situation. Keep both visible. Inspect discovery, selection, booking, arrival, use, and completion. Locate the most unpleasant or uncertain moment rather than assuming that the most expensive subsystem deserves the next investment. [S5]

**Decision rule:** If improving the proposed metric would leave the customer's main difficulty intact, reopen the problem definition. If the metric directly addresses a demonstrated bottleneck, retain that conventional improvement as a serious candidate.

## 2. Separate essentials, performance, and delight

Sutherland discusses three kinds of attributes: requirements whose absence disqualifies an offer, performance attributes, and details that create delight or imply care. Their contribution to satisfaction differs. Meeting a basic requirement is not necessarily a differentiator; an apparently peripheral detail can matter disproportionately once the basics work. [S3]

**Decision rule:** Classify the actual problem before proposing a treatment. Fix a broken essential first. Compare further performance improvements with overlooked experience improvements rather than assuming a linear relationship between measurable performance and value.

**Edge case:** A reassuring confirmation cannot compensate for appointments that routinely fail. It may help alongside operational repairs, but its wording must match actual service capability. This truthfulness requirement is a package guardrail, not a claim that perception eliminates physical constraints.

## 3. Treat the economic story as one hypothesis

A stated reason is evidence about the person's account of a choice, not complete access to motivation. Sutherland discusses how presentation, available alternatives, coordination, and inferred meaning can change behavior without changing intrinsic functionality. Preserve the possibility that price really is the constraint while developing competing explanations. [S2]

Use a hypothesis card:

- Observed behavior and current evidence.
- Candidate explanation, including the conventional explanation.
- Specific barrier at a specific moment.
- Predicted difference if the explanation is correct.
- Observation that would weaken it.

Avoiding an awkward interaction can matter more than gaining a reward. A self-service channel may allow people to act without initiating an uncomfortable conversation. Do not mistake unwillingness to approach someone for lack of interest in the underlying service. [S4]

**Decision rule:** When explanations imply different behavior under the same price and capability, design a comparison that separates them. If evidence cannot distinguish them yet, call them hypotheses and recommend discovery or a prototype test.

## 4. Model the occasion, comparison, and downside

Specify who is deciding, what else they could do, what they expect, and what a bad choice would cost. People may prefer a dependable outcome over the best average outcome when a deadline or severe downside is involved. A route that is usually faster can be worse for someone who cannot afford a long delay and has no escape route. [S7]

The relevant comparison need not match the provider's category. Presentation, purchase units, and pricing can alter the reference against which people assess an offer. Investigate that reference before interpreting willingness to pay. [S3]

**Decision rule:** Record the comparison and the consequence of failure for each important occasion. Inspect completion, cancellations, regret, and complaints alongside initial uptake. These measurement choices operationalize the contextual principle.

**Edge case:** A premium presentation may reassure one audience and exclude another. A lower price may improve access or undermine a quality inference. Do not prescribe higher prices as a universal remedy, or assume that everyone can afford an offer simply because its framing improves. [S3]

## 5. Look where standard comparisons are silent

Reverse benchmarking examines what successful competitors handle poorly or overlook, rather than only copying what they already do well. It calls for curiosity about undervalued dimensions of the experience, including customers who receive little attention under the category's usual priorities. [S6]

Make a small map: competitive dimensions, neglected moments, affected customers, and feasible interventions. A category may compete on speed while leaving scheduling difficult. A product may have abundant features yet an intimidating first-use experience. These are diagnostic prompts, not findings about any particular market.

**Decision rule:** Choose a neglected dimension only when it plausibly matters to the target occasion and can be delivered well. Being different without creating experienced value is insufficient.

## 6. Reduce uncertainty and make credible meaning visible

Sutherland emphasizes that making a wait predictable can improve experience without making it shorter. Information can restore the ability to plan. Technology is valuable here because of the psychological problem it solves, not merely because it is technically impressive. [S2] [S5]

A signal invites an inference from something observable. His cafe furniture discussion illustrates a visible action that suggests the business is currently open. The operational connection makes the cue more credible than a generic assertion. Treat this as an illustrative mechanism rather than evidence of guaranteed sales lift. [S2]

For each cue, write the observation, intended inference, reason for credibility, and possible competing interpretation. Prefer demonstrable care, accurate information, and useful affordances over unsupported claims.

**Edge case:** An exact arrival estimate that repeatedly fails can increase disappointment. Offer a range or update procedure the service can sustain. A decorative quality badge has little substance if it conveys no verifiable information. These are application judgments, not universal experimental findings.

## 7. Generate alternatives and contextual opposites

Keep three distinct families in the shortlist: a conventional physical or economic improvement, a change to context or interpretation, and an alternative that would initially seem counterintuitive. Sutherland argues that restricting tests to apparently logical candidates excludes opportunities before evidence can assess them. [S3]

Also examine an opposite: abundant choice versus curation, attentive service versus privacy, a bargain versus an extravagance. Opposing approaches can appeal in different circumstances; a midpoint is not automatically best. Specify the condition that makes each sensible. [S7]

**Decision rule:** Do not reward novelty by itself. Shortlist ideas with a plausible mechanism, feasible delivery, meaningful upside, and an informative comparison. Retain the conventional option so the recommendation can be assessed against an actual alternative.

## 8. Test explanations without defending a story

Behavioral insight broadens the solution space; it does not provide engineering certainty. A coherent explanation can still be false. Experiments should expose a hypothesis to disagreement rather than serve as demonstrations arranged to confirm it. [S3] [S6]

Package convention: define the stimulus, inferred meaning, expected behavior, comparison, primary outcome, exposure limit, downside checks, and decision criterion. Hold unrelated variables stable where feasible. Select thresholds from the user's economics and available evidence; never invent a baseline or measured lift. If traffic is insufficient, use prototypes to investigate understanding and feasibility, then label the remaining causal uncertainty.

**Decision rule:** Choose a first test for information value as well as potential benefit. A failed test weakens the treatment or its proposed mechanism in that context. It does not justify replacing evidence with another appealing explanation. A positive result supports the observed outcome; it does not automatically establish the proposed psychological cause.

## What Sutherland explicitly warns against

- **Professional tunnel vision:** Defining a human problem solely through engineering or financial measures can privilege the profession's favorite solution. Give expensive conventional proposals creative scrutiny too. [S5]
- **Universal certainty:** Behavioral science cannot promise that a specific intervention will always work. Its value includes identifying surprising possibilities worth testing. [S3]
- **Plausibility mistaken for truth:** A sensible narrative is not sufficient evidence of the real explanation. Ask what observation could distinguish or contradict it. [S6]
- **Advertising as a cure for everything:** Sutherland explicitly limits the reach of advertising-led solutions while advocating trying persuasion where appropriate before defaulting to compulsion. This does not authorize delaying essential protections. [S4]
- **Universal psychological rules:** Opposite approaches can succeed under different expectations and occasions. Do not flatten them into an average prescription. [S7]
- **Aggregate success mistaken for individual benefit:** A favorable average can obscure variance, severe downside, or different consequences for different people. Preserve context when evaluating value. [S7]

## Additional package guardrails

Keep cues truthful, retain meaningful choice, and disclose total price and material conditions. Do not manufacture urgency, conceal restrictions, or invent endorsements. These are package requirements for responsible application, not quotations or an attributed Sutherland ethics code. Preserve the user's scope: developing a brief does not authorize a live price change, customer contact, or experiment launch.
