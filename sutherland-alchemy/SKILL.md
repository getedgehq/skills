# Sutherland Alchemy

Based on Rory Sutherland's public method. Not affiliated with or endorsed by Rory Sutherland.

## When to use

Use this skill when a problem involves human choice and the default proposal is a discount, a stronger incentive, another feature, faster delivery, or a larger budget. Look for a gap between objective performance and experienced value: a service may be technically excellent yet feel uncertain, embarrassing, hard to coordinate, or poorly suited to the occasion. Sutherland's perspective expands the explanations and interventions worth considering. [S2] [S5]

The deliverable is a decision brief with competing explanations, concrete interventions, and a bounded first test. It can accompany campaign copy, a booking flow, a pricing presentation, or a service prototype. Preserve the user's requested format and constraints; the brief supports their decision rather than silently replacing their assignment.

This package synthesizes public interviews into an agent workflow. It is not an official checklist, a reproduction of the book, or a guarantee of behavioral effects. The original LinkedIn source supplies event context only. Attribute methodological claims to the interviews, and distinguish evidence from hypotheses and invented illustrations. [S1] [S3]

Use physical improvements when basic performance is failing. Once essentials work, investigate psychological alternatives alongside engineering and economic options. A more appealing explanation cannot rescue an unusable service. Read the relevant sections of [references/method.md](references/method.md), then use the template and checklist as needed. [S3]

## Procedure

1. **Capture the decision.** Record the user's objective, requested deliverable, proposed solution, constraints, and available evidence. Identify the behavior that would make the work successful. Use the brief fields in [templates/output-template.md](templates/output-template.md); mark missing facts explicitly.

2. **Reframe the problem.** Write the organizational brief and the customer's desired experience separately. Replace a proposed means, such as reducing journey time, with the outcome it is meant to create. Apply the reframing rules in [references/method.md](references/method.md). [S5]

3. **Check the essentials and the whole journey.** Find broken requirements, then map discovery through completion. Identify the worst moment before improving an already pleasant one. Use the essentials and journey sections in [references/method.md](references/method.md). [S3] [S5]

4. **Challenge the default explanation.** Keep the economic explanation and add plausible alternatives involving uncertainty, coordination, embarrassment, comparison, or regret. For each, name an observation that would distinguish it. Follow the hypothesis rules in [references/method.md](references/method.md). [S2] [S4]

5. **Specify the occasion.** Describe the audience, alternatives, expectations, consequences of failure, and purchase context. Identify when reliability or an escape route matters more than average performance. Consult the context section in [references/method.md](references/method.md). [S7]

6. **Search neglected value.** Compare what competitors emphasize with what customers struggle to do. Inspect overlooked details, credible signals, and opportunities to make waiting predictable. Use the reverse benchmarking and signaling rules in [references/method.md](references/method.md). [S2] [S5] [S6]

7. **Build a diverse shortlist.** Include a conventional option, a psychological alternative, and a contextual opposite. Make each intervention tangible enough to prototype. Consult [examples/worked-examples.md](examples/worked-examples.md) for the difference between an attractive slogan and a usable proposal. [S3] [S7]

8. **Explain and challenge each mechanism.** State what changes, what inference it invites, why that inference is credible, and where the effect might reverse. Compare cost and delivery feasibility. Apply the decision rules in [references/method.md](references/method.md); reject unsupported promises. [S2] [S3]

9. **Select the first test.** Favor useful information at bounded cost. Specify comparison, exposure, primary outcome, completion measures, downside checks, and a decision threshold before observing results. Use [templates/output-template.md](templates/output-template.md). These planning fields are package conventions implementing the expert's experimental stance. [S3] [S6]

10. **Write the decision brief.** Recommend one first action conditionally, retain the conventional alternative, and explain what evidence would change the recommendation. Fill [templates/output-template.md](templates/output-template.md), adapting its length to the user's task.

11. **Review and revise.** Apply [checklists/review-checklist.md](checklists/review-checklist.md). Resolve failed items, expose remaining unknowns, and distinguish a proposal from an executed experiment. After a test, revisit the mechanism or context instead of inventing reasons to defend the original story. [S6]
