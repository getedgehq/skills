# Sutherland Alchemy

Based on Rory Sutherland's public method. Not affiliated with or endorsed by Rory Sutherland.

## When to use

Use this skill when a task involves human choice and the obvious answer is to lower prices, increase incentives, add features, reduce time, or spend more. Look especially for a gap between objective performance and experienced value. Sutherland's psychological perspective can reveal another cause of the problem before investment follows an untested economic assumption. [S2]

Use it to develop testable alternatives, not to promise that a behavioral effect will occur. This procedure synthesizes public interviews into an agent workflow; it is not an official checklist from the book. The starting LinkedIn post supplies context about an Alchemy event, rather than substantive instructions for the method. [S1] [S3]

## Operational procedure

### 1. Change the question before changing the product

Write the brief in two versions: the organization's proposed improvement and the experience or behavior it actually wants. Translate "make the journey faster" into "make people prefer and enjoy the journey." List what would count as success without committing to the proposed means. A professional specialty can make its favorite solution appear inevitable. [S5]

Separate physical requirements, useful performance improvements, and possible sources of delight. Repair a failing essential attribute before expecting a charming peripheral feature to compensate. Once the essentials work, do not assume that another unit of measurable performance creates an equal unit of customer value. [S3]

Inspect the whole experience, including discovery, booking, arrival, coordination, waiting, and departure. Identify the worst moment instead of optimizing the part already comfortable. Record one neglected irritation that could matter more than the expensive improvement in the original brief. [S5]

### 2. Put the conventional explanation on trial

Write the standard explanation of the behavior, such as "customers want the lowest price." Then produce competing explanations involving embarrassment, uncertainty, coordination, or the way available choices are presented. Treat stated reasons as clues rather than complete access to motivation. [S2]

For each explanation, identify an observable difference that could distinguish it from the others. For instance, would offering appointments by available day change uptake while price stays constant? Do not convert an entertaining explanation into a fact merely because it sounds coherent. [S2] [S6]

Include the option that people are avoiding an awkward interaction. A self-service channel may succeed because it permits distance and removes embarrassment, rather than because it offers a stronger financial incentive. Check which interaction the person currently has to initiate. [S4]

### 3. Model the decision as the customer experiences it

Specify the occasion, audience, alternatives, expectations, and possible consequences of a wrong choice. Ask whether the customer wants the theoretical best outcome or a dependable outcome with little chance of regret. Reliability can beat a higher average performance when failure would be costly. [S7]

Find the comparison that gives the offer its meaning. Record what the customer is comparing against, not just the category the business assigns it to. A new presentation or purchase unit can change that comparison and therefore willingness to pay without changing underlying functionality. [S3]

Design around the relevant situation instead of an imaginary average customer. Check whether a feature attractive to one group is unwelcome to another. Two opposite treatments can both work when expectations and context differ; their midpoint is not automatically a superior compromise. [S7]

### 4. Search for neglected value

Use reverse benchmarking: list what competitors measure and compete on, then inspect what they all handle poorly or leave unserved. Look for an experience people value that does not appear on the standard comparison sheet. Choose a candidate advantage there rather than automatically matching the category leader. [S6]

Examine small details with disproportionate meaning: a form, a confirmation, a sign, a visible act of care. Judge a detail by its effect on the experience, not by whether its budget is large enough to seem strategic. Make the proposed detail concrete enough to show or prototype. [S4]

Bring technical, economic, and psychological explanations together. Technology is especially useful when it changes how a situation feels, such as making a wait predictable. Do not assume that a technically more impressive solution is the one that creates more experienced value. [S5]

### 5. Build a portfolio of psychological interventions

Generate distinct candidates rather than several variations of the same discount. Include a conventional improvement, a change to context or comparison, and an apparently odd alternative with a plausible behavioral explanation. Expand the solution space before selecting the most defensible-looking answer. [S3]

Try uncertainty reduction before assuming speed is the bottleneck. Show what will happen, when, and what the person can do meanwhile. The target may be the inability to plan during a wait rather than the duration of the wait itself. [S5]

Audit signals. For each proposed cue, write the inference it invites and why it would be credible. Actions can communicate more than claims: movable furniture outside a cafe can imply that someone is present and the cafe is open. Distinguish a genuine signal from decorative assertion. [S2]

Explore a voluntary interface or persuasion change before assuming stronger compulsion is necessary. Ask whether the desired behavior lacks an easy, timely way to happen. Keep the intervention tied to the user's actual task rather than turning every problem into an advertising campaign. [S4]

Include a contextual opposite. If the obvious proposal is more choice, consider curated choice; if it is more elaborate service, consider unobtrusive service. State the condition under which each could work. Do not turn either direction into a universal psychological law. [S7]

### 6. Challenge the economic reflex

If the proposal is a price cut, write what the lower price might signal and calculate its cost relative to a change in presentation or coordination. Consider whether a higher price could imply confidence or quality, but frame that as a hypothesis rather than a recommendation to increase every price. [S3]

Check whether people care about variance, escape routes, and avoiding disaster. When there is a deadline or meaningful downside, assess a predictable option against a faster but unreliable option. Avoid treating a favorable average as sufficient evidence that a choice serves each person over time. [S7]

Give the conventional plan a creative challenge as well as giving creative ideas a financial challenge. Identify the assumption that protects the expensive proposal from scrutiny. Ask whether a different definition of value could meet the same objective more cheaply. [S5]

### 7. Test the surprising possibility

For each shortlisted candidate, state the changed stimulus, proposed inference, expected behavior, and observation that would undermine the explanation. Distinguish "worth testing" from "known to work." Behavioral knowledge improves the range of hypotheses available; it does not provide engineering certainty about their effects. [S3]

Choose a comparison that preserves the question being tested. For example, compare the same offer with and without uncertainty-reducing information before changing both price and delivery. Include a small or counterintuitive candidate that would otherwise be excluded as too trivial to matter. [S6]

Set a bounded exposure, an outcome, and a decision criterion appropriate to the task. These are workflow conventions for making Sutherland's experimental stance usable, not his prescribed statistical protocol. If results disappoint, revisit the mechanism or context rather than defending the story with another plausible explanation. [S6]

### 8. Return a decision document

Deliver a concise brief containing the original problem, its experiential reframing, competing explanations, relevant decision context, candidate interventions, and a recommended first test. For each candidate, include the proposed psychological mechanism and the conditions under which it might reverse. This format operationalizes the contextual alternatives discussed in the interviews. [S6] [S7]

Include the conventional alternative and its cost, what remains physically unchanged, and what new value the customer could experience. End with the evidence needed to choose, revise, or abandon the recommendation. Do not present confidence in a clever explanation as a substitute for testing it. [S3]

## Common failure modes

Watch for professional tunnel vision, especially when a problem is defined entirely in the measures an engineering or finance team already uses. Reopen the definition of the customer benefit before optimizing those measures. [S5]

Watch for benevolent service that creates unwanted attention, awkwardness, or obligation. An elaborate interaction can reduce value even when the provider considers it generous; offer a contextually appropriate alternative instead of assuming more service is always better. [S4] [S5]

Watch for using aggregate averages to erase meaningful differences between occasions or people. Preserve the segment or situation that gives an intervention its meaning rather than flattening it into a general rule. [S6] [S7]

## Worked example: invented illustration

A neighborhood repair business asks for a campaign promoting a 15 percent discount because inquiries rarely become bookings. The following is an invented application, not a Sutherland case study.

The agent reframes the task as helping people confidently arrange a repair. It records three hypotheses: the price is too high, customers cannot coordinate a visit, or they fear a wasted appointment. The available observations do not yet distinguish these explanations.

It proposes three candidates: the requested discount, an availability-first booking page, and a booking confirmation describing arrival updates and how rescheduling works. The availability-first page begins with "Which day are you home?" before asking customers to select repair categories. Its mechanism is reduced coordination effort, not greater motivation.

For the first test, the agent recommends comparing the existing page with the availability-first page while holding prices and actual appointment capacity constant. The business will track completed bookings and successful visits over a fixed trial period. It will choose a minimum worthwhile improvement based on its economics before starting, and inspect complaints or failed visits alongside bookings.

If bookings improve but completed visits do not, the agent will investigate scheduling mismatches rather than call the redesign successful. If neither improves, it will reconsider the coordination hypothesis and test another explanation. The final recommendation remains conditional on observed behavior.
