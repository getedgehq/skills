# Worked examples

Both scenarios are invented illustrations. Their business facts, copy, and proposed tests are fictional; neither is a Sutherland case study or a report of measured results. Citations identify the principles used, not evidence that these interventions will succeed.

## Illustration 1: Repair bookings that stall

### Scenario and weak first draft

A neighborhood repair company receives inquiries but few bookings. Its team proposes a 15 percent discount. Assume technicians generally complete visits successfully, prices are displayed, and the business has appointment inventory. No reliable conversion baseline is supplied.

Weak draft:

> Save 15 percent on your next repair! Our expert technicians offer outstanding service at unbeatable prices. Book now and stop putting off your repairs.

This assumes insufficient motivation or excessive price without examining whether people can arrange a visit. It offers neither a distinctive explanation nor a way to choose between explanations.

### Improved result: Decision brief

**Decision:** Test an availability-first booking flow before funding a broad discount campaign.

**Reframing:** The organization wants more bookings. The customer wants a repair arranged for a time they can manage, with confidence that the appointment will happen.

**Competing explanations:** The price is unaffordable; finding a compatible slot is difficult; uncertainty about arrival makes booking feel risky. Inspect abandonment by booking step and ask recent non-bookers what prevented completion. Their answers are clues, not conclusive diagnoses. [S2]

**Candidates:**

| Option | Concrete change | Proposed mechanism | Reversal condition |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Conventional | Offer the proposed 15 percent discount | Improves affordability | Costs margin without addressing scheduling |
| Psychological | Start with available days, then show compatible repair options | Reduces coordination effort | Selecting a day first wastes effort when the repair is unsupported |
| Contextual opposite | Offer a clearly priced flexible-day option alongside specific slots | Gives people who are often home a simpler choice | Unacceptable for customers who need a precise visit window |

**Prototype copy:**

> Find a repair visit that fits your week.
>
> Choose an available day to see appointment windows and supported repairs. Prices appear before you confirm. Need a different day? Use the rescheduling link in your confirmation.

Show only actual inventory and supported rescheduling conditions. Do not advertise arrival updates until the service can deliver them.

**First test:** Compare the current booking flow with the availability-first flow while holding prices, service categories, and appointment capacity consistent. Randomize eligible sessions if feasible. The proposed primary measure is completed visits per eligible visitor; completed bookings and abandonment by step are diagnostic measures. Review rescheduling, failed visits, complaints, and contribution margin.

**Decision criterion:** Before launch, the business must supply its baseline and minimum economically worthwhile improvement. Set a fixed exposure cap and an analysis plan from expected traffic. If evidence is too limited, report uncertainty and investigate usability rather than declare a winner. The brief authorizes no live launch by itself.

**Conditional recommendation:** Continue only if completed visits improve enough to justify implementation without unacceptable operational cost. If bookings rise but visits fail, investigate scheduling mismatches. If neither improves, revisit the coordination hypothesis and compare the price explanation.

### Rules that made the difference

Coordination becomes a competing explanation rather than a presumed fact. [S2] The entire booking journey replaces a narrow focus on promotional copy. [S5] The outcome checks completion, so an attractive flow cannot hide a worse service experience. This measurement choice is a package convention grounded in attention to consequences and context. ## Illustration 2: A commuter shuttle competing on speed

### Scenario and weak first draft

A regional shuttle operator wants to appeal to passengers traveling to an airport. It proposes spending on a faster route. Assume vehicles are safe and accessible, capacity is adequate, and current schedules are generally fulfilled. Passengers have different departure times and tolerance for delay.

Weak draft:

> Get there faster with our premium express shuttle. The fastest journey means a better trip. Upgrade today for unbeatable convenience.

This equates speed with value and makes unsupported superiority claims. It overlooks the passenger who wants to catch a flight with minimal risk.

### Improved result: Decision brief

**Decision:** Investigate whether a reliability-focused presentation improves successful airport trips before committing to a speed investment.

**Reframing:** The organization wants passengers to choose its shuttle. The passenger wants to reach the airport with enough time, without feeling stranded if the trip is disrupted.

**Competing explanations:** Travelers choose on journey time, on price, or on confidence that they will meet a deadline. Interview passengers with and without tight deadlines, then compare observed choices. Reliability and optionality may matter more than a favorable average when failure is costly. [S7]

**Neglected value:** Competitor pages emphasize headline duration. Inspect whether they leave pickup instructions, disruption procedures, luggage handling, or return planning unclear. Do not assume neglect without reviewing actual offers. [S6]

**Candidates:**

| Option | Concrete change | Proposed mechanism | Reversal condition |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Conventional | Invest in the proposed faster route | Reduces actual journey time | Cost is high and delay variability remains |
| Psychological | Show pickup instructions and supported delay procedures beside each departure | Makes the trip easier to plan | Detail overload or unreliable information reduces confidence |
| Contextual opposite | Present a compact express option and a planning-focused option | Fits different occasions | Artificial segmentation makes ordinary booking harder |

**Prototype copy:**

> Plan your airport trip with fewer unknowns.
>
> Check your pickup point, departure time, and luggage allowance before booking. See what happens if your service is delayed. Choose a departure that allows for your airline's arrival guidance.

Populate every linked detail from actual operations. Do not imply a guaranteed arrival or recommend a buffer without evidence.

**First test:** Compare the current departure listing with one that exposes the same accurate planning information earlier. Keep actual routes, schedules, and prices fixed. Track completed trips per eligible visitor, booking completion, support contacts, cancellations, and reported arrival problems. Use flight-deadline context only when it can be collected appropriately; otherwise state the segmentation limitation.

**Decision criterion:** Obtain current economics and traffic before setting a worthwhile improvement threshold and exposure cap. Continue if the completed-trip benefit meets that threshold and problems do not increase. Faster initial booking alone is insufficient. A positive result supports the presentation change; it does not prove that uncertainty reduction caused it.

### Rules that made the difference

The proposal distinguishes speed from reliability and the ability to recover from trouble. [S7] It uses information to reduce uncertainty while retaining engineering improvements as a comparator. [S5] It searches neglected experience dimensions rather than merely copying a competitor's headline metric. [S6] Contextual opposites remain conditional options, not a rule that every shuttle needs two tiers. [S7]
