# Positioning method and decision rules

## Reading map

Read the five component sections in sequence for a new positioning exercise. Use the absence-of-differentiation diagnostic when the team cannot identify a reason to buy. Use framing styles when comparing categories, and testing guidance when reviewing a candidate.

This package operationalizes public articles and a public post. Evidence labels, comparison tables, review gates, and the specific validation questions are package adaptations. They are not claims about a prescribed workshop format or reproductions of book material. Citations identify the source of a principle; they do not establish facts about the product being positioned.

## Core principles

Positioning establishes the context in which particular customers can recognize an offering's distinctive value. A category label triggers assumptions about capabilities, competitors, intended buyers, and price before a detailed pitch begins. An effective context makes useful assumptions accurate; a poor one forces the seller to repeatedly correct the buyer. [S2] [S5]

The components are connected: differences depend on alternatives; value depends on differences; customer fit depends on caring about that value; the frame should make the value visible to those customers. Start with actual buyer alternatives rather than choosing a category and reverse-engineering a justification. [S2]

Positioning precedes messaging and storytelling. A memorable narrative cannot compensate for wrong assumptions about customers, competitors, or value. Sales positioning concerns why a buyer should choose the available product in the present market. Future vision may matter to investors without being a sound current purchase argument. [S7] [S8]

## Evidence and scope

Capture the offering as sold today, the buying situation, available capabilities, current category, and the business decision this work supports. Seek input across product, sales, marketing, and customer-facing teams because each can hold a different part of the explanation for wins. Check existing buyers and uses before treating an internal belief as established customer value. [S1] [S4]

For this package, label inputs as:

- **Observed:** Supported by a specified interview, deal record, usage observation, demonstration, or customer result.
- **Hypothesis:** Plausible, but its relevance, frequency, or causal explanation has not been established.
- **Unknown:** Required to make the decision, but unavailable.
- **Future:** Not available in the present offering and excluded from current differentiated-value claims.

Keep an evidence identifier, date, and limitation with each substantial claim. A demo can establish a capability without proving a business outcome. A single enthusiastic customer can reveal a valuable use without establishing an entire segment. These distinctions are practical safeguards for the reliance on actual results advocated in the public method. [S7] [S8]

If evidence is sparse, complete a provisional analysis and list the highest-impact unanswered questions. Do not present brainstorming as validation. If the user needs only a positioning review, inspect the existing five components and return supported corrections rather than demanding a full workshop.

## Release the default frame

Write down the current description and the expectations it produces. Then allow other descriptions to compete with it. Founders' original product concept, the team's technical background, and the category historically used by marketing can anchor the company after the actual offering has evolved. [S4]

The practical test is whether the old frame puts the strongest present value at the center of the buyer's evaluation. If it makes a useful strength seem incidental while highlighting missing category features, reconsider it. Do not assume changing the category is always necessary; retain the current frame when it survives comparison. [S4] [S5]

## Competitive alternatives

Ask what the customer would use or do if this offering were unavailable. Include remaining with the existing workflow, manual labor, spreadsheets, a function of a larger suite, and named products actually considered. Inaction often means accepting a workable existing solution, not having no solution. Understand why that alternative is attractive before arguing for change. [S2] [S3]

Record each alternative's buyer situation, strengths, limitations, and evidence of consideration. Exclude products listed solely because they resemble yours. Monitoring a possible future competitor is separate from positioning against it today. Add it when evidence shows it entering relevant deals. [S3]

When different buying situations have different alternatives, preserve separate comparisons. For example, a buyer replacing a spreadsheet may require a different value argument from one replacing a specialist system. This is a practical application of the principle that differentiation is relative to the buyer's choices. [S2] [S3]

## Differentiated capabilities

For each real alternative, identify something the current offering can demonstrably provide that the alternative cannot in that buying context. Consider capabilities, business model, proprietary processes, relationships, and combinations of attributes. Being different does not require every constituent feature to be individually exclusive. [S2] [S4]

First capture unusual attributes without prematurely calling them good or bad. Extensive configurability may burden beginners but help specialists who need control. The value and customer steps decide whether the distinction matters. [S4]

Replace broad claims such as easier or stronger with a verifiable mechanism and comparison. Specify scope: a capability may distinguish the product from manual work but not from a named specialist. Never generalize a difference beyond the alternative against which it was established. [S2]

## When differentiation seems absent

Dunford separates an actual absence of distinct customer value from a failure inside the company to understand why customers choose it. Review wins and current use before accepting the latter as a product defect. [S1]

Investigate these causes identified in her public post:

- Some functions do not know why the business wins.
- Sales or marketing does not understand what the product's capabilities enable.
- Teams compare against irrelevant rivals or omit the current workflow.
- Attention to losses obscures the customers who choose the product.
- Pursuit of feature parity hides existing strengths.
- Segmentation is so broad that distinct value disappears in the average.

If reviewing those possibilities still yields no distinct, defensible value, positioning cannot invent a purchase rationale. Report the unresolved product or offer gap and the evidence needed to decide what must change. Do not manufacture a superiority claim or substitute persuasive language for missing value. [S1]

## Differentiated value

Translate each attribute into an enabled action and a meaningful customer consequence. Keep the chain explicit: alternative, different capability, enabled outcome, customer consequence, evidence. Features are useful inputs, but the purchase rationale is what their differences allow the customer to achieve. [S2] [S4]

Combine related outcomes into a small set of themes that explain the choice. A theme might concern responding during a live interaction, avoiding a labor-intensive reconciliation, or controlling a specialized workflow. Preserve the supporting mechanisms rather than reducing everything to generic efficiency. [S4]

Check whether the actual alternative already produces the outcome adequately. If it does, the outcome may be valuable but does not establish differentiated value. Where speed or cost is the difference, retain measurement conditions and do not invent a percentage. [S1] [S2]

## Best fit customers

Identify the conditions that make the differentiated value especially important. Industry, geography, and company size can help locate buyers but often do not explain why some care and others do not. Look for recognizable workflow requirements, data characteristics, dependencies, and urgent situations among customers who benefit strongly. [S4]

Describe both who fits and the boundary where the value matters less. An organization with large data volumes may only archive them; another may need immediate analysis during customer interactions. Volume alone does not identify the stronger buyer. [S4]

Compare segments on value intensity, supporting wins, ability to find and reach them, and whether the reachable opportunity can support the business objective. A narrower segment is useful when the advantage becomes clear, but narrowing beyond commercial viability is not a success. [S4] [S6]

If two segments require different alternatives and value explanations, prepare separate candidate canvases. Do not blur them into a persona that nobody matches. Treat a promising segment without customer evidence as a testable hypothesis. [S1] [S2]

## Market frame

After alternatives, capabilities, value, and customers are clear, compare plausible market contexts. For each, ask what customers would assume about required features, competitors, intended users, price, and evaluation criteria. Favor a context where those assumptions help the right buyer recognize the current value. [S2] [S5]

The package asks for at least two candidates to expose the inherited category to a real comparison. This is an operational aid, not a claimed numerical requirement from Dunford. A candidate need not be a fashionable category name; a familiar category with a meaningful qualification may suffice. [S2] [S4]

Avoid choosing a frame merely because it removes an uncomfortable competitor. The product still needs to meet the expectations that matter to the target buyer. A frame need not make the product ideal for everyone in the broad category; show where it is credible and exceptional for the selected segment. [S4]

## Framing styles and consequences

Dunford's public framing article distinguishes four choices. [S6]

| Style | Suitable decision logic | Main risk |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Compete across an existing category | The product can credibly win on the established criteria | Challenging leaders on strengths they already own |
| Win a subcategory | A recognizable group places exceptional value on the differences | A niche too small to support the business |
| Reframe an existing category | A real innovation changes how solutions should be evaluated | Difficulty changing buyer habits while incumbents defend them |
| Create a new category | Existing contexts cannot adequately convey the value and the company can fund education | Buyers must learn the category before evaluating the offering; later entrants can benefit from that work |

New category creation is not a default growth requirement or a way to escape comparison. An established context can transfer useful buyer understanding immediately. [S2] [S6]

Changing the frame can change expectations for pricing, channels, partnerships, implementation, support, and product direction. Record these implications before handing the decision to marketing. A new description alone cannot produce organizational alignment. Recommendations in a canvas do not authorize changes to the business. [S4] [S6]

## Document and translate

Document the five components and the chosen frame, with a one-line reason for the frame. Keep evidence gaps and rejected candidates in your working notes unless the user asks for them; the deliverable is the positioning, not the analysis behind it. Date the canvas so future reviews have a reference point. This template is an analytical record completed after investigation, not the traditional positioning-statement exercise Dunford criticizes for assuming the answers in advance. [S2] [S4]

Only then write a short buyer-facing explanation. It should identify what the offering is, whom it helps, and why its distinct value matters relative to a real alternative. Build messaging, sales narratives, and demos from the canvas without treating any sentence as the positioning itself. [S4] [S7]

## Test and revisit

Test the candidate with suitable buyers and people handling actual deals. The public exercise explicitly calls for customer testing and iteration. The following questions are package adaptations intended to expose errors rather than elicit compliments. [S4]

- After hearing the description, what does the buyer think the product does and does not do?
- Which options would they compare it with, including their present approach?
- Which stated outcome matters enough to consider a change, and under what conditions?
- What proof would be needed, and what expectation does the category create that the product cannot meet?

Record responses and their fit with the target definition. Confused classification suggests a frame problem; an unexpected alternative changes the comparison; an unimportant outcome challenges value or segmentation. Revisit the earliest broken component before polishing language.

Repeat the review when capabilities, buyer options, or category expectations change. A formerly unique capability can become standard. Positioning may also evolve as strategy opens a new segment, but current claims must still reflect the product and market at that stage. [S4] [S8]

## Explicit warnings to preserve

- Do not use a fill-in positioning statement to discover answers it already assumes. It also creates a misleading sense that positioning is permanently complete. [S2] [S4]
- Do not position against every possible rival or omit the current workflow. [S2] [S3]
- Do not infer that a product can only occupy the category in which it began. [S4]
- Do not invent distinct value where none exists. [S1]
- Do not replace actual customer results with an internally convincing story. [S7]
- Do not assume category creation is required for growth or guarantees durable leadership. [S2] [S6]
- Do not use future investor ambition as the main answer to a buyer's current choice. [S8]
- Do not treat a change in positioning as a copy-only change or a permanent one-time exercise. [S4]
