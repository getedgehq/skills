# Obviously Awesome Positioning

Based on April Dunford's public method. Not affiliated with or endorsed by April Dunford.

## When to use

Use this skill when a product has customers who value it but prospects struggle to understand why to buy it, when a team disputes its differentiation, or when a launch, website, or sales pitch needs a defensible positioning foundation. It is especially useful for products whose original category no longer describes where they win. Positioning describes why a particular buyer would choose the product over their real alternatives today. Messaging, storytelling, and a tagline come after that choice is clear. [S1] [S2] [S5] [S7] [S8]

## Procedure

### 1. Establish the evidence and current frame

- Record the product as it exists today, its current category claim, the customers who buy and stay, recent wins and losses, sales objections, actual usage, and any credible customer interviews. Separate observed facts from internal beliefs and future roadmap promises. Positioning must explain how the offering wins now, while a vision describes a possible future. [S1] [S8]
- Ask sales, product, marketing, and customer facing people for examples of recent wins, especially customers who get strong value. If someone says the product has no differentiation while customers keep choosing it, investigate why those buyers chose it before accepting the verdict. Look for knowledge gaps about the product, overlooked win patterns, and a segment definition so broad that the advantage disappears. [S1] [S7]
- Write down the default category inherited from the original product idea, then set it aside as a hypothesis. The product may have evolved past the market its founders first imagined, and that inherited frame may highlight weaknesses while hiding its strongest use. [S4]
- Do not fill in a traditional one sentence positioning statement at the start. A product can have several plausible categories and customer groups, so the exercise would silently assume the very answers the analysis needs to discover. [S2] [S4]

### 2. Identify competitive alternatives from the buyer's view

- For each meaningful buying situation, ask what the buyer would do if this product vanished. Include their current workflow, a spreadsheet, manual labor, an incumbent suite, postponement, and named products only when buyers actually consider them. Record which alternative appears in real deals and why it remains attractive. [S2] [S3]
- Treat a buyer's decision to keep the current approach as a genuine alternative. Determine what that approach already does well, such as low cost or convenience, because a claim of being better than a named rival will not move a buyer who is content to stay put. [S3]
- Exclude theoretical rivals that never appear on buyers' shortlists. A market map containing every similar product dilutes the comparison and can make the team overlook the alternative it must actually beat. Revisit an excluded rival if it starts appearing in deals. [S2] [S3]
- If different buyer situations have different alternative sets, keep separate working rows rather than forcing one universal competitor list. The differentiators that matter depend on the alternative used in that situation. [S2] [S3]

### 3. Find capabilities that are genuinely different

- Compare the current product with each real alternative and list specific features, capabilities, delivery traits, or business model facts the alternative lacks. A difference can be a combination of ordinary features; record what can be demonstrated and the exact comparison that makes it different. [S1] [S2] [S4]
- At this stage, capture unusual attributes before judging whether they are attractive. What appears to be a drawback to a general buyer, such as complexity or high configurability, may be a strength for a buyer who needs control. [S4]
- Challenge vague claims such as better, easier, or more powerful by asking what a prospect could verify in a demo, implementation, or customer result. Discard an alleged difference if the alternative can do the same thing in the buyer's relevant context. [S1] [S2]
- When the list looks empty, compare it with won deals and how those customers use the product. A lack of internal awareness of winning capabilities is different from a lack of distinct buyer value. If no distinct, demonstrable value exists after that check, mark the positioning problem as unresolved and identify the product or offer gap. Do not fabricate a reason to buy. [S1]

### 4. Convert attributes into differentiated value

- For each credible attribute or useful combination, ask what it lets the buyer do that the actual alternative does not. Keep following the consequence until it becomes a meaningful outcome in the buyer's work, such as a faster decision, avoided cost, new revenue, or reduced risk. Preserve the link from attribute to outcome so the value is explainable. [S1] [S2] [S4]
- Group related outcomes into a small set of value themes and attach evidence from customer behavior, results, demos, or win notes. The answer to why a buyer picks this product is the distinct value, not the feature inventory alone. [S1] [S2]
- Test each theme against the same alternative that produced its attribute. If the competing path delivers that outcome just as well, the theme is general value rather than differentiated value and cannot carry the position. [S1] [S2]
- Keep customer language and observed results visible. A compelling internal story built from brainstormed assumptions can sound coherent while failing in the market; check the themes against customers who actually bought and used the product. [S2] [S7]

### 5. Select customers who care intensely

- For each value theme, identify the conditions that make it urgent and valuable. Name observable traits such as workflow, data type, regulation, scale, purchasing trigger, or reliance on a particular outcome. A broad industry label is insufficient when only some members have the need. [S2] [S4]
- Examine the customers who received exceptional value or bought with little persuasion, then describe what they share and how a seller could recognize another one. Prefer a target where the product's unique value is central to the purchase rather than a buyer who merely could use it. [S1] [S2] [S4]
- Compare candidate segments on strength of value, evidence from actual wins, ease of finding them, and whether the segment can support the business goal. If a segment has weak evidence, label it as a hypothesis to test instead of presenting it as an established ideal customer. [S2] [S4] [S6]
- Do not average all prospects into a universal persona or let poor fit losses obscure a strong pattern among wins. A product may look undifferentiated across a vast market while being exceptional for a narrower group. [S1] [S4]

### 6. Choose a market frame that makes the value obvious

- Generate at least two plausible market frames only after the alternatives, attributes, value, and best fit customers are clear. For each frame, write the assumptions a buyer will immediately make about required features, competitors, intended user, and price. Prefer the frame whose assumptions are accurate and whose usual buying criteria spotlight the differentiated value. [S2] [S4] [S5]
- Consider an established category when buyers already understand it, a narrower subcategory when the product can win a valuable niche, or a reframed existing category when an innovation credibly changes evaluation criteria. Claim broad category leadership only when the product can compete on that category's expected criteria. [S2] [S6]
- Treat creation of a new category as a costly choice that requires teaching buyers what the category means before they can evaluate the product. Do not invent a label solely to avoid comparison. An understandable existing frame often gives the buyer useful context immediately. [S2] [S6]
- Check the chosen frame by asking whether it causes prospects to ask the comparison question the team can answer well. If it invites comparison with a leader on dimensions where the product is weak, or forces a long explanation of why familiar category expectations do not apply, revise the frame. [S2] [S4] [S5]

### 7. Test and operationalize the position

- Present the candidate frame and differentiated value to suitable prospects and to sales people who handle real deals. Watch whether buyers quickly understand what the product is, why it matters to them, and why it beats their current path. Record confused assumptions, missing alternatives, and objections, then revisit the relevant component instead of merely polishing words. [S2] [S4]
- Align product, sales, marketing, and customer success on the chosen five components. Translate them into a sales story, demo emphasis, website messaging, and customer proof only after the position is settled; a polished story cannot repair inaccurate positioning inputs. [S4] [S7]
- Recheck the position when buyer alternatives, product capabilities, or market expectations change. A once unique feature may become standard, and a useful category may stop framing the product correctly. [S4] [S8]

## Finished output

- Deliver a compact positioning canvas with real competitive alternatives, differentiated attributes, each attribute's linked value theme and proof, recognizable best fit customer traits, and the selected market category. Show the evidence or uncertainty behind every major claim. [S1] [S2] [S4]
- Include the principal alternative frame considered and why the selected one makes the product's distinct value easier for the target buyer to see. State any unresolved evidence gap, especially if no defensible differentiation was found. [S1] [S2] [S4]
- Add one concise buyer facing explanation grounded in the canvas, plus a short list of customer questions or deal observations that would confirm or falsify it. Treat that explanation as a translation of the strategic choice, not as a substitute for the canvas. [S2] [S4] [S7]

## Illustration

An invented product, ShiftLedger, helps regional food distributors track refrigerated deliveries. Its current pitch calls it a general fleet management platform. In recent wins, buyers replaced driver spreadsheets and phone calls; they did not compare it with the largest fleet suites. ShiftLedger records temperature exceptions automatically and sends a shipment specific audit packet. For distributors serving hospitals, that combination turns a frantic manual evidence hunt into a prompt response when a buyer questions a delivery. The strongest observed segment is distributors with strict proof requirements and frequent temperature sensitive routes. A plausible frame is cold chain delivery evidence software for regional distributors, because it makes the audit result central while leaving broad fleet suite expectations out of the first comparison. Before adopting it, the team would verify the claimed outcome with those buyers and test whether prospects understand the frame without a long explanation. [S2] [S3] [S4] [S5]
