# Persona, Angle, Format Messaging Buckets

Based on Dara Denney's public method. Not affiliated with or endorsed by Dara Denney.

## When to use

Use this skill when asked to audit existing paid social creative, find new ad concepts, organize a testing plan, or turn customer research into production briefs. It is especially useful when a team has many ad formats but cannot explain which audience or message each ad tests. [S1] [S6]

The unit of work is a testable concept for a specific customer situation: a trigger based persona, a reason that person might care, a messaging angle, an awareness stage, and only then an execution format. An angle is the portable persuasive idea; a format is how that idea appears in a particular channel. [S2] [S6]

## Procedure

### 1. Assemble the evidence before ideating

- Collect the brand's offer, product use cases, current ads, historical winners and losers, customer reviews, testimonials, support questions, comments, relevant social discussion, competitor ads, and available account results; label any unavailable input instead of filling the gap with an invented audience fact. [S3] [S4]
- Read positive and negative customer material together: positive material can reveal the desired outcome, while complaints and low ratings can reveal objections, unmet needs, and language that polished brand copy misses. [S4]
- Extract repeated customer situations, triggering events, stated problems, desires, objections, and product outcomes; retain a link or source reference for each signal in the working notes so a later persona can be checked against evidence. [S3] [S4]
- Treat AI clustering as an assistant to research, then inspect the underlying reviews or comments for every proposed cluster; reject a cluster whose supposed trigger cannot be found in customer evidence. [S3]
- If the brand has no customer history, use comparable brands' customer feedback, especially middling and negative reviews, to form provisional hypotheses; mark those personas as provisional until the brand collects its own response data. [S3]

### 2. Diagnose the persona gap

- Describe actual buyers from reviews or interviews separately from the people implied by current ads; compare the two lists and identify buyers with strong evidence but little corresponding creative. [S3]
- Name a persona by the event, problem, or change that puts someone into a buying state, then add useful demographic context and the deeper desired outcome; do not substitute an age or gender label for the trigger. [S3] [S6]
- Rank customer situations twice, once by how often they appear and once by emotional intensity; investigate a highly emotional segment even if its volume is smaller, and document why a high volume segment may still deserve first priority. [S3]
- For each existing ad, ask which intended audience its creator, imagery, wording, and premise would attract; if the answer is only a broad demographic, revise the audience tag to a concrete situation or mark it unknown. [S2] [S3]
- Write a one or two sentence diagnosis that links the evidence gap to the next tests, such as an underserved trigger, misunderstood objection, or overused audience; if the diagnosis would justify any random batch of ads, make it more specific. [S3]

### 3. Build messaging angles with four lenses

- For each priority persona, write the angle as a claim, tension, or transformation that would matter to that person even if the ad changed from a video to a static image; keep the intended buyer and the reason to believe visible beside the angle. [S2] [S8]
- Classify the message using Dara's four lenses: human desire, demographic or role context, strategic angle, and user journey; use the lenses to expose missing persuasive routes rather than to force four ads for every persona. [S2]
- In the human desire lens, ask whether the message promises connection, romance, calm, power, or another evidenced emotional outcome; state the actual customer desire, not a generic benefit that could sell any product. [S2] [S3]
- In the demographic lens, use age, role, hobby, or job only when it changes the experience or proof needed; tie that context back to the trigger so the concept does not become a shallow identity callout. [S2] [S6]
- In the strategic angle lens, consider a credible story device such as a personal history, comparison, taboo truth, negative framing, or recognizable spokesperson; choose it because it sharpens the persona's problem or belief, not because the device itself is fashionable. [S2]
- In the user journey lens, tag whether the buyer is unaware, problem aware, solution aware, product aware, or most aware; match the message to what the buyer already knows, and include broader entry points when the current account speaks only to people ready to buy. [S2] [S5]
- For each proposed angle, record the customer signal, the belief to change, the proof available, the likely objection, and the awareness stage; discard angles that depend on unsupported product claims or merely rename an existing hook. [S2] [S3] [S4]

### 4. Choose execution after the message

- Select creator or talent for fit with the persona and the credibility of the story; assess whether an apparent winner worked because of the person on screen before attributing success to its script or format. [S2]
- Select the visual format and platform treatment that can make the angle clear: for example, a demonstration for a use case, a testimonial for lived experience, a comparison for a tradeoff, or a simple graphic for a concise proof point; note imagery, setting, and production style as separate execution choices. [S2]
- Carry a promising angle into more than one format when the message deserves another expression, while checking each execution on its own merits; a line that worked in user generated video is not automatically a strong static headline. [S2] [S5] [S7]
- Do not declare a format a strategy: if two proposed concepts differ only by grid, carousel, talking head, or aspect ratio, identify the missing persona or message test before assigning production. [S2] [S6] [S8]
- Write each brief with persona and trigger, one or two sentences describing the idea, three messaging options or hook directions, proof assets, selected creator and format, and the distinct question the test should answer. [S3] [S4]

### 5. Prioritize and read results as learning

- Keep unselected ideas in a central backlog; choose a small set for the monthly roadmap using evidence strength, confidence, likely impact, production speed, and room for an occasional high upside experiment. [S3]
- Plan larger persona and content pillar coverage by quarter, decide actual production tasks weekly, and avoid reacting to the latest format trend in place of the roadmap. [S3] [S6]
- Before launch, write a hypothesis and what result would change the next decision; if no one can say what the test teaches, revise or drop it. [S4]
- Separate primary outcome measures such as spend, results, cost per result, and return from diagnostic measures such as click through, play depth, comments, and shares; use outcomes to identify winners and diagnostic signals to investigate why. [S2]
- Compare a result with the account's other creatives, the broader performance pattern, and relevant competitor or platform patterns; then read comments and customer feedback for objections or new language that the dashboard cannot supply. [S2] [S4]
- After a test, record whether the persona, angle, creator, and format were supported, contradicted, or still unclear; scale and reinterpret a promising message across executions, and revise the diagnosis when the evidence points to a different buyer need. [S2] [S3] [S7]

## Checks and common failure modes

- Check that every concept names a concrete customer trigger, one main angle, one awareness stage, and a plausible proof source; a broad persona plus a trendy format fails this check. [S2] [S3] [S6]
- Check that the roadmap covers genuinely different audience needs and messages, rather than many surface variations of the same sales claim; repeated formats alone do not establish creative diversity. [S6]
- Check that a strong click through or watch metric is not being presented as proof of commercial performance, and that a winning result is not attributed to format without examining creator and message. [S2]
- Check that any use of customer wording remains faithful to the source and any performance or product claim is supportable; customer language is evidence for an angle, not automatic proof of every claim the ad might make. [S3] [S4]
- Check that each test has a planned next move and that the team can state why it is being made now; an unranked list of ad ideas is not a diagnosis or a roadmap. [S3] [S8]

## Finished output

- Deliver a compact evidence summary, two persona views (actual buyers and current ad targets), the priority gap, and the one or two sentence diagnosis. [S3]
- Deliver a concept table with persona and trigger, messaging bucket, angle, awareness stage, customer signal, proof or objection, creator, format, hypothesis, priority, and proposed next test. [S2] [S3]
- Deliver the first production briefs with three message options per concept, plus a short measurement and review plan that distinguishes commercial outcomes from explanatory signals. [S2] [S3]
- State missing evidence and provisional assumptions so a reader can see which decisions are grounded in customer material and which still need testing. [S3]

## Illustration: invented scenario

An invented refillable hand soap brand has reviews from apartment renters who dislike bulky bottles on a small sink, while its ads mainly show eco lifestyle imagery. The evidence suggests a trigger based persona: renters trying to clear sink clutter after moving into a smaller bathroom. A useful diagnosis is that current creative sells environmental identity while buyers describe a space problem. This is a hypothetical example, not a claim about a real brand. [S3]

The first angle is "a cleaner sink without storing large bottles," tagged as a calm or convenience desire for a problem aware buyer. The brief asks a renter creator to show the crowded sink, the refill process, and the cleared space in a short demonstration; two more messaging options test less clutter and easier storage. A second execution could be a static before and after image carrying the same angle. The test asks whether the space message earns better purchase results than the existing eco message, while comments are reviewed for refill objections. [S2] [S3] [S5]
