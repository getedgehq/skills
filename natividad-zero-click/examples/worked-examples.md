# Worked examples

Both scenarios are invented illustrations. Organizations, research notes, drafts, and proposed review periods are fictional. No outcome is reported or promised. The operational advice in the drafts illustrates the method; it is not advice attributed to Natividad about those industries.

## Illustration 1: A webinar invitation for scheduling software

### Brief and evidence

A fictional scheduling vendor wants to help dental practice managers handle cancellations and promote a webinar. Invented support notes show a recurring question: which waitlisted patients should staff contact first? The business contribution is familiarity with the vendor as a practical source of scheduling help. LinkedIn is a provisional destination for this illustration, subject to audience validation. This connects a specific trigger to a distribution decision. [S4] [S6]

### Weak first draft

> Cancellations costing your practice money? Discover our three secrets to a better waitlist. Join Thursday's webinar and learn how to fill more appointments. Register here: [registration link].

The reader receives a problem statement and a promise, but no usable answer. Removing the invitation leaves no distinct insight. It also implies an outcome without evidence. [S1]

### Improved result

> A name on your waitlist is not enough to tell you who can use a cancellation slot.
>
> Before an opening appears, record three constraints for each interested patient: appointment type, times they can attend, and the notice they need.
>
> When a slot opens, filter for those constraints first. Then contact eligible patients according to your practice's established priority policy. A patient who needs two days' notice is not a match for an opening this afternoon.
>
> A shorter list of suitable people gives staff a clearer starting point than a long list of names.
>
> Thursday's webinar shows this workflow with a sample schedule and discusses how to keep the list current. Register here: [registration link].

### Rules that changed the result

- **Observed trigger:** The post answers the invented support question rather than promoting the abstract topic of scheduling. [S6]
- **Complete insight:** Readers receive the matching rule and an example within the post. The registration link can disappear without removing its usefulness. [S1]
- **Additive CTA:** The webinar offers a demonstration and maintenance guidance after the central answer has been delivered. [S1] [S2]
- **Bounded recommendation:** The post preserves the practice's priority policy and makes no promise that every opening will be filled. This is a package safeguard.

### Distribution and review

An inbox derivative could demonstrate the same rule with a fictional slot and patient constraints. A later post could address the separate question of keeping availability current. Each derivative should resolve its own bounded problem. [S4] [S6]

For an illustrative six-week review, monitor relevant questions and shares, registrations, qualified inquiries, and available branded-search trends. Compare with the preceding period, noting other promotions. Registrations are directly relevant, but a change does not prove that this one post caused it. [S2] [S5]

## Illustration 2: An inbox-native newsletter from a reporting tool

### Brief and evidence

A fictional reporting platform publishes a newsletter for small agency account leads. Its proposed premise is practical reporting decisions that make client discussions clearer. Invented interviews suggest that leads struggle to explain conversion-rate changes when the traffic mix shifts. The business contribution is helping users interpret reports more effectively. [S3] [S6]

### Weak first draft

> Subject: Three must-read reporting resources
>
> Reporting is changing fast. This week we have an amazing guide to conversion rates, a useful dashboard checklist, and our latest product update.
>
> Read the conversion guide: [link]
>
> Download the checklist: [link]
>
> See what's new: [link]

This is a directory of promises. Someone who opens the email but clicks nothing learns almost nothing. Its premise does not provide a clear editorial standard. [S3]

### Improved result

> Subject: Check the traffic mix before explaining a rate change
>
> A lower overall conversion rate does not always mean that each channel is performing worse.
>
> Imagine two channels. Search converts 100 of 1,000 visits and social converts 20 of 1,000. The combined rate is 6%. Next month, search still converts 100 of 1,000, while social converts 60 of 3,000. Each channel's rate is unchanged, but the combined rate is now 4%.
>
> Before explaining an overall change, compare both the rate and the share of visits for each channel. In this example, the larger share of lower-converting social visits explains the drop. In your report, other factors may also be involved.
>
> **A useful reporting check:** Put the overall rate beside channel-level visits and conversions. This gives you a starting point for distinguishing a mix change from a change within a channel. Our checklist adds prompts for checking tracking and lead quality: [checklist link].
>
> **Product tip:** If you use our reporting tool, save the channel breakdown beside the summary card so both views are available during the client discussion. Setup instructions: [help link].
>
> What reporting question did a client ask you this week? Reply and tell us.

### Rules that changed the result

- **Specific business purpose:** Advice is selected to help users interpret the product's reports rather than fill a newsletter. [S3]
- **Inbox usefulness:** The numerical example and reporting check explain the central insight without a click. [S1] [S3]
- **Premise-led selection:** Every section supports clearer reporting decisions. The newsletter does not need to force the source's three-three-three structure onto this narrower issue. [S3]
- **Feedback loop:** The reply prompt collects the next set of content triggers. [S4] [S6]
- **Honest context:** Numbers are illustrative, and the explanation allows for other causes in real reports. This is a package safeguard.

### Distribution and review

A native social derivative could show the fictional figures in a small table and explain the same mix effect. It should contain the conclusion locally rather than link to the newsletter for the answer. [S4]

For an illustrative review across three issues, examine replies, available email behavior, and user accounts of applying the advice. Include the newsletter in customer discovery questions where relevant. Monitor related product adoption if that data is available, without treating every subsequent action as caused by this issue or promising the open rates reported in Natividad's own example. [S3]
