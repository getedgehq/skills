# Worked examples

Both scenarios below are invented illustrations. The founders, companies, drafts, facts, and signals are fictional, not Gerhardt case studies or documented outcomes. Citations identify the principles applied. All quoted drafts are original illustrative writing, not quotations from sources.

## Illustration 1: An unknown cafe-scheduling founder

### Inputs and weak first draft

Maya managed weekend shifts at three independent cafes before building a scheduling tool. Its current capability is collecting staff availability before a manager publishes the rota. It does not forecast absences or guarantee full coverage. She can contribute a weekly interview. In this scenario, customer interviews indicate that local cafe owners read an existing trade newsletter; her own social following is small.

Weak narrative and draft:

> I have always been passionate about innovation. Our revolutionary platform eliminates staffing problems for every cafe. We are excited to announce the future of work. Follow our journey and book a demo today!

The draft hides relevant experience, overstates product capability, and gives the owner no useful lesson. Starting a daily LinkedIn campaign from this draft would multiply those weaknesses.

### Applying the method

1. Niche sentence: Maya helps independent cafe owners plan shifts around staff availability, drawing on firsthand responsibility for weekend rotas.
2. Story: She repeatedly published a rota before collecting constraints, then spent the weekend rewriting it. She built a tool to make availability an input to planning.
3. Villain: The practice of discovering conflicts after the schedule is published, rather than employees or a named competitor.
4. Beliefs: Collect constraints first; distinguish unavailable staff from uncovered shifts; make unresolved gaps visible before publishing. Each belief comes from the fictional interview, not invented expertise added by the editor.
5. Alignment: Promise earlier visibility of availability conflicts, not the elimination of staffing problems.
6. Distribution: Offer a useful founder lesson to the existing trade newsletter, with draft social adaptations if needed. Confirm editorial fit before proposing outreach or publication.

### Improved result

Revised narrative:

Maya built her scheduling tool after managing weekend shifts at three cafes. Repeated late changes taught her that a rota is only as reliable as the availability information behind it. Her product collects those constraints before publication so owners can see conflicts while there is still time to make a plan.

Revised founder draft:

> I used to publish Friday's rota and spend Saturday rewriting it.
>
> Often, I had asked who could cover a shift before asking who was unavailable. The schedule looked finished while the inputs were still missing.
>
> For next week's rota, try collecting availability on Tuesday. Mark uncovered shifts separately. Then publish with the gaps visible instead of treating silence as agreement.
>
> This will not stop every last-minute absence. It gives you one fewer avoidable surprise.

Operating plan: A weekly 20-minute interview produces one lesson and a backlog item. Maya reviews the facts and voice before use. The supporting marketer prepares the newsletter version and records owner questions. This cadence is an illustrative capacity choice.

Feedback decision: If relevant owners repeatedly ask about holiday coverage, prepare a lesson on holiday constraints before building a larger series. A qualified inquiry mentioning the lesson is a business-interest signal, not proof of revenue causation.

### Rules that made the difference

Relevant backstory and a process-based villain made the narrative credible. [S2] Product alignment removed an unsupported guarantee. [S1] Audience evidence determined distribution. [S5] A useful customer action replaced promotion, and a recurring question defined the next asset. [S1] [S2]

## Illustration 2: An established company with a quiet technical founder

### Inputs and weak first draft

Arun founded an inventory-reconciliation product after working on warehouse software. The company has an opted-in customer newsletter, while Arun dislikes solo video recording. Existing customers include operations managers at small distributors. The product flags conflicting stock records for investigation; it cannot determine every discrepancy's cause. Arun's interview describes teams adjusting stock counts before investigating handoff errors. He is also an enthusiastic cyclist.

Weak strategy and draft:

> Arun should post daily about cycling, success, AI, and entrepreneurship, then launch a weekly video show. Our community will receive product webinars and promotional updates. First post: Great leaders always embrace innovation. Our AI inventory solution guarantees perfect stock accuracy. Join our webinar!

This plan confuses personality with expertise, ignores existing reach and recording preferences, and creates a company-centered community. The draft makes an unsupported guarantee and supplies a generic opinion instead of Arun's real observation.

### Applying the method

1. Niche: Inventory reconciliation for small-distributor operations teams, grounded in Arun's warehouse-software experience.
2. Capture: Two short audio interviews per month, transcribed into a voice guide and idea backlog. Retain cycling as occasional personal texture.
3. Story: Building warehouse systems exposed a recurring pattern: teams corrected the number before checking how the conflicting records arose.
4. Belief: Investigate the handoff before treating the stock-count correction as the solution.
5. Product connection: The tool makes discrepancies visible; investigation still requires human judgment.
6. Distribution: Introduce Arun's short written lessons through the existing customer newsletter. No separate discussion group is needed unless customers want a useful peer exchange.

### Improved result

Series benefit: Help operations managers distinguish stock-count symptoms from repeatable process failures.

Revised founder draft:

> A corrected stock count can hide an unresolved problem.
>
> Working on warehouse systems, I saw teams make the adjustment and move on. The next discrepancy then looked like a new incident, even when the same handoff was breaking again.
>
> Before closing the next discrepancy, write down where the two records first diverged. Was it receiving, a transfer, or dispatch? Keep that note next to the adjustment.
>
> Our tool flags conflicting records. It does not tell you the cause. That investigation is where your team's judgment matters.
>
> Which handoff creates the most reconciliation work for your team?

Operating plan: The marketer turns each interview into two candidate lessons. Arun checks the experience, argument, and language. Start with two newsletter lessons per month, then adapt one to a buyer-relevant social channel if there is capacity. This is an illustrative schedule, not an expert requirement.

Feedback decision: Log replies by customer role and recurring handoff problem. If receiving questions recur, expand that topic into a practical lesson. Review newsletter response, qualified conversations, and self-reported discovery together. A high reaction count from unrelated viewers would not justify abandoning the niche.

### Rules that made the difference

The company's distribution introduced the founder rather than forcing him to build reach from zero. [S1] Interview capture preserved his thinking without requiring solo video. [S2] [S4] Niche focus separated useful expertise from personal texture. [S3] Clear audience benefit and human judgment replaced promotion and an inaccurate product promise. [S1] [S2] The feedback loop produced a specific next content decision. [S2]
