# Dave Gerhardt's Founder Brand
Based on Dave Gerhardt's public method. Not affiliated with or endorsed by Dave Gerhardt.

## When to use

Use this skill when a founder or supporting marketer wants to turn the founder's experience and beliefs into trust, awareness, and commercial interest. It fits an unknown startup building an audience before launch and an established company whose founder has useful expertise but little visibility. Treat the founder as a recognizable person buyers can learn from, rather than simply another account distributing company announcements. [S1] [S2]

The procedure below operationalizes Gerhardt's public interviews, including later explanations of his publishing practice. Its central sequence is storytelling, publishing, and feedback. The output structures are practical adaptations for agent work, not a claim that Gerhardt prescribes these exact templates. [S3] [S5]

## Operational procedure

### 1. Establish the ingredients and founder commitment

Gather the intended customers, their recurring problem, the product's current capabilities, the founder's relevant experience, existing audience channels, and the requested deliverable. Ask what the founder is already discussing with customers, partners, candidates, and investors. Those conversations are raw material for content because the founder is unusually close to the business and market. If facts are missing, identify them as questions rather than inventing a more impressive biography. [S2] [S3]

Check whether the founder wants to contribute ideas and make a sustained investment. Distinguish discomfort with a format from unwillingness to participate. An introvert can write; someone uncomfortable recording alone can have an interview conversation. If the founder expects immediate pipeline from a few posts or wants marketing to manufacture all their opinions, explain the mismatch and propose a supported format before producing an expansive publishing plan. Willing founders were a key ingredient at both Drift and Privy. [S2] [S3]

### 2. Choose the expertise the business needs to be known for

Write a working sentence identifying the audience, the topic worth being known for, and why this founder can help. Favor a narrow area where founder experience overlaps with customer needs and the company offering. Evaluate each proposed topic against that sentence. Gerhardt found that writing about many unrelated interests weakened audience growth, while a recognizable B2B marketing focus supported his business. [S4] [S5]

Separate credibility from personality. Relevant experience supplies a reason to trust the founder's judgment; occasional personal details supply a reason to feel connected. A finance founder's skateboarding history might humanize them, but it does not establish financial expertise. Preserve personal texture without letting it displace the niche people followed the founder to learn about. [S3] [S5]

### 3. Build the story before filling a calendar

Interview the founder for the backstory, the problem they experienced, why existing approaches disappointed them, and why they decided to build this company. Assemble a narrative connecting those facts to the customer's world. Prefer a specific, relevant history over a generic declaration of passion. Prior success is useful evidence, but an authentic encounter with the problem can supply the connection when famous employers or acquisitions are absent. [S3]

Identify the villain as an obstacle, practice, or status quo the customer recognizes. It need not be a named competitor. Drift used cumbersome lead forms as an opposing force for its conversational approach. State what the founder wants customers to escape and what better experience becomes possible. Reject a villain that is dramatic but unrelated to the problem the business solves. [S3]

Extract two or three substantive beliefs from the story. Ask what the founder has seen, done differently, or disagrees with in their field. For each belief, capture the observation behind it and the implication for the customer. If nothing meaningful emerges, return to interviewing and reflection instead of generating a month's worth of filler. Posting frequency cannot compensate for having nothing interesting to say. [S2]

### 4. Make the narrative a company commitment

Compare the story with product behavior, roadmap direction, and the sales promise. For every major positioning claim, identify what the company actually does that supports it. At Drift, the sales and marketing narrative also shaped integrations and product decisions. If the story promises something the product cannot deliver, narrow the claim or identify the gap explicitly before using it in content. [S2]

Use this check to keep the founder brand connected to company strategy. A charismatic founder discussing unrelated subjects may attract attention without creating a useful association with the business. The story should explain both why this person is credible and why this company exists for these customers. [S2] [S3]

### 5. Select a publishing route from existing strengths

Inventory distribution before recommending new channels. An already recognizable founder can lend an audience to an unknown company, as David Cancel did at Drift. A company with substantial customer reach can introduce a knowledgeable but less visible founder, as Privy did with Ben Jabbawy. Choose the first move according to those ingredients instead of copying the same launch sequence for every business. [S2]

Choose the social channel where intended buyers are present and receptive. Gerhardt's Drift team invested in LinkedIn after seeing their buyers there and strong responses to their writing. Recommend LinkedIn for a suitable B2B audience with that rationale, rather than treating every founder as having the same audience. Specify the people the founder should converse with and the questions those people care about. [S6]

For a podcast, recurring lesson series, or speaking topic, write the listener's benefit before the show description. Privy's short ecommerce lessons helped small store owners learn marketing; Seeking Wisdom offered learning for its audience rather than a stream of product pitches. Decide whether an interview, solo lesson, writing, or talk best exposes this founder's knowledge. Channels can reinforce one another without a mandatory social-then-podcast-then-event order. [S2] [S3]

### 6. Capture and publish the founder's real thinking

Create a lightweight capture process using interviews, voice notes, useful customer conversations, and the founder's existing writing. When supporting a busy founder, arrange recurring interviews and turn their transcripts into a voice guide and idea backlog. AI can help organize arguments, research supporting material, and draft from these inputs, but the distinctive beliefs and personality must come from the founder. A polished generic executive voice defeats the reason for using a person as the brand. [S5]

For each draft, combine one meaningful observation, its context, and a useful implication for the chosen audience. Retain specific details and natural phrasing from the founder's material. Reject drafts consisting mainly of award announcements, webinar links, vague inspiration, or self-congratulation. Gerhardt treats social as a channel for useful conversation and expertise, not simply promotion. [S2] [S5]

Set a sustainable cadence and maintain a backlog. Gerhardt often writes daily and keeps ideas for future posts, but he explicitly rejects the idea that daily posting alone produces results. Schedule according to the founder's capacity and material quality. Treat his posting times and scheduling preferences as personal experiments, not universal algorithm rules. The check is whether the next post has something worth saying. [S2] [S4] [S5]

### 7. Turn audience signals into the next asset

Collect replies, direct messages, email responses, questions from events, customer reviews, and observations from sales or customer research. Keep the people closest to customers involved. Record which audience problem the signal reveals, rather than merely counting reactions. Do not build a company-centered community when the audience needs a clear benefit for themselves. [S3]

Expand ideas that resonate into a newsletter, podcast lesson, webinar, or speaking session. Gerhardt uses successful social topics to inform longer content and popular podcast guests to inform event programming. Also revisit strong older posts because much of the audience never saw them. Refresh outdated details and preserve the underlying idea instead of requiring every asset to start from a blank page. [S4] [S5]

Use feedback as evidence, not a vote that automatically overrides judgment. Distinguish a request from a buyer, an interesting comment, and repeated demand across channels. State what you will explore next and why. Do not pretend that every podcast reaction should dictate a product roadmap or that every popular suggestion must be accepted. [S3]

### 8. Evaluate trust and business interest together

Report audience reach alongside inbound interest, website visits, email subscribers, and self-reported discovery. Collect concrete evidence such as a prospect naming the podcast during a demo or a customer describing why they trust the founder. Gerhardt combined numerical reporting with screenshots and customer signals because a single attribution spreadsheet could miss the effect of the audience relationship. Avoid interpreting likes alone as commercial success or promising revenue causation from correlation. [S2] [S4]

Diagnose weak results in sequence. Check founder participation, story relevance, substantive ideas, audience fit, and actual audience responses before increasing output. Continue or adjust based on evidence of useful relationships and inbound demand; do not abandon the approach merely because a short trial lacks perfectly attributed pipeline. [S2] [S3]

## Finished output

Deliver a compact founder-brand package containing the target audience and expertise sentence; a factual founder narrative with problem, villain, and relevant credibility; two or three evidenced beliefs; product alignment notes; the chosen channel and customer benefit; founder-voiced draft content; a realistic capture and publishing schedule; and a feedback plan connecting audience signals to the next content decision. Mark missing founder facts and explain material choices so the user can review the package. [S2] [S3] [S5]

## Worked example, invented illustration

Maya founded a scheduling tool after managing weekend shifts at three independent cafes. Her audience is cafe owners, her expertise is shift planning, and her villain is last-minute staffing confusion. Her narrative links that experience to a product that lets staff confirm availability before schedules go live. Her first belief is that availability should be collected before assigning shifts, not repaired afterward. This illustrates relevant credibility and narrative alignment, not a real Gerhardt case. [S2] [S3]

An illustrative draft reads, "I used to publish Friday's rota and spend Saturday rewriting it. Asking for availability first removed one avoidable source of chaos. Before you buy another tool, try collecting next week's constraints on Tuesday." A founder interview supplies two further lessons for a weekly series aimed at cafe owners. Repeated questions about holiday coverage become the next lesson; a qualified inquiry mentioning the series joins the feedback log. The topic and channel would be chosen from Maya's actual audience evidence. [S3] [S4] [S6]
