# Founder-brand method

## Scope and evidence

This method builds a recognizable founder voice around expertise that matters to prospective customers. The organizing cycle is story, publishing, and feedback. Use the founder's particular ingredients rather than copying a famous startup's output. Public interviews supply the principles; the intake fields, claim ledger, review process, and schedules below are operational adaptations. Sources are indexed in [sources.md](sources.md). [S1] [S2] [S5]

## 1. Establish inputs and participation

Collect six inputs: the audience, their recurring problem, actual product capability, relevant founder history, existing distribution, and the requested deliverable. Begin with material already available from conversations with customers, partners, candidates, investors, and the team. The founder sees patterns across the business that can become useful teaching material. Ask for concrete incidents rather than a list of inspirational adjectives. [S2]

Useful interview prompts:

- What happened that made this problem impossible to ignore?
- What did you try before deciding to build something?
- What question do customers repeatedly ask you?
- What do people in your field often misunderstand?
- Which recent decision taught you something other customers could use?

Check willingness separately from format preference. A founder who dislikes video may write comfortably or speak naturally in an interview. Introversion does not remove the opportunity. A founder who contributes neither ideas nor review is a different constraint. Gerhardt describes willing founders and patience as important ingredients in his Drift and Privy work. [S1] [S2]

**Decision rule:** If the founder will participate, select the easiest authentic capture format. If participation is uncertain, scope a small capture session and draft. If no authentic material is available, deliver targeted interview questions and mark the content blocked on founder input. Never manufacture a personal memory. This stopping rule is a package adaptation.

## 2. Select the expertise

Complete: For **[specific audience]**, this founder helps with **[recognizable problem or topic]**, drawing on **[relevant experience]**. Prefer the overlap between customer need, founder knowledge, and the business. A broad interest in leadership is usually less useful than a specific area where the founder has earned judgment. Gerhardt describes clearer audience growth when he focused on B2B marketing rather than many unrelated interests. [S3]

Distinguish credibility from human connection. A hobby can make the founder more relatable without proving domain expertise. Gerhardt's finance-founder illustration makes this distinction: an unrelated sporting background is texture, while the business-relevant history must carry the argument. Do not require impressive employers or an acquisition. A firsthand encounter with the customer problem can explain why the founder built the company. [S2]

**Decision rule:** Keep a topic when the chosen audience can see why it belongs to this founder and this business. Use occasional personal details naturally. Do not impose Gerhardt's reported personal/professional content mix as a mandatory ratio. [S3]

## 3. Build the story

Construct a factual chain: backstory, problem, failed or inadequate approach, decision to build, and intended improvement for the customer. The story should explain the business through the founder's experience. Avoid a biography that never reaches the buyer or a mission statement that could describe any company. [S2]

The villain is the obstacle the customer wants to escape. It can be a cumbersome process, mistaken assumption, or status quo. It does not require a named competitor or hostile attack. Gerhardt describes Drift's opposition to burdensome lead forms as a way to frame its conversational approach. Choose a tension that the product can meaningfully address. [S2]

**Decision rule:** Ask whether the customer recognizes the problem and whether the founder has a credible reason to care. If either link is missing, revisit the interview. Do not invent drama, adversity, or a universal customer outcome.

**Edge case:** Before launch, teach from the problem and the founder's experience while clearly distinguishing planned capabilities from what exists. Gerhardt's early Drift work built audience interest before the product was ready. [S2]

## 4. Extract beliefs and check product alignment

For each of two or three beliefs, write the position, its supporting observation, and its implication for the audience. Useful beliefs offer a decision or changed practice. An unsupported declaration of disruption provides little teaching value. Gerhardt rejects publishing volume as a substitute for having interesting ideas. [S1]

Build a claim ledger as a package adaptation:

| Claim | Evidence available | Product connection | Treatment |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Founder experience | Interview or existing record | Why judgment is relevant | Retain after verification |
| Product behavior | Confirmed capability or demonstration | How the promise becomes real | State within its limits |
| Future direction | Explicit plan | Proposed improvement | Label as future |
| Outcome | Actual observation and context | Benefit experienced | Avoid extending beyond evidence |

The story should influence the company promise and be supported by product decisions. Gerhardt explains that Drift's sales and marketing positioning also shaped its integrations and roadmap. If the content promises a customer experience the product cannot deliver, narrow the promise or identify the gap. [S1]

**Edge case:** A founder may hold a useful view that is broader than the product. Preserve it as domain expertise while explaining the connection. Do not force every teaching post into a product pitch, and do not let the entire founder identity become unrelated to the business. [S1] [S2]

## 5. Select distribution and format

Inventory the assets before choosing tactics. An established founder audience can introduce a new company. An established customer base can introduce a less visible founder. Gerhardt contrasts these routes through Drift and Privy, rather than offering one launch sequence for everyone. [S1]

Select the channel where buyers actually spend attention and where this founder can contribute. Gerhardt's historical LinkedIn investment reflected both buyer presence and strong response to his team's posts. That history supports an audience-led channel decision, not a current platform guarantee. Do not recreate old connection volumes, posting tricks, or launch tactics simply because Drift used them. Gerhardt explicitly encourages finding what works in the present context. [S5]

Write the audience benefit before the series description. Educational content should help customers become better at something they care about. Privy's short lessons served small ecommerce businesses; Seeking Wisdom gave listeners learning value beyond product information. Choose solo lessons, interviews, writing, or speaking according to the founder's strengths. Social, podcasts, and events can reinforce each other in different orders. [S1] [S2]

**Edge case:** A community does not require a new Slack group or forum. Gerhardt describes engaged followers, subscribers, listeners, and attendees as a community before there was a dedicated discussion space. Recommend a hosted group only when interaction gives members a clear benefit. [S5]

## 6. Capture and publish

Maintain an idea backlog from founder interviews, voice notes, writing, and customer discussions. Gerhardt describes using recurring interviews and transcripts to develop a founder's voice guide and feed an AI writing assistant. AI can organize an argument, support research, and create drafts, while the founder supplies the ideas and personality. [S4]

A practical voice guide records characteristic vocabulary, argument structure, recurring beliefs, examples, and phrases the founder would avoid. Ground it in this founder's material rather than another creator's style. Check generated claims independently; an AI-generated statistic is not evidence. These specific review fields are package adaptations of the capture approach. [S4]

Build each draft from one observation, context that makes it credible, and an implication the audience can use. Preserve the founder's concrete detail and natural language. The content can end with a useful question when there is something worth learning from the response. Do not add engagement bait merely to inflate comments.

Choose a cadence supported by material and capacity. Gerhardt writes frequently and keeps a backlog, but he also rejects daily posting as a sufficient strategy. His reported scheduling preferences include acknowledged uncertainty about their effect. Treat timing and scheduler choices as experiments; do not claim a penalty or universal best hour. [S1] [S3] [S4]

## 7. Learn from audience signals

Collect meaningful replies, direct messages, email responses, event questions, reviews, and customer-facing team observations. Record who raised the issue and what need it suggests. Gerhardt stresses that people close to customers supply important insight and warns against communities organized around company promotion rather than member benefit. [S2]

Expand strong ideas into longer assets, and refresh older useful content. Gerhardt describes using popular podcast guests to help select event speakers and using social response to guide subsequent content. Preserve the value of an idea while updating facts and presentation for the new setting. [S3]

**Decision rule:** A repeated question from relevant buyers is stronger evidence of a content opportunity than a large reaction count from unrelated viewers. Explore a plausible signal with a proportionate next asset. Do not treat audience preference as a binding vote or equate podcast feedback with instructions for the product roadmap. Gerhardt explicitly describes feedback as an additional input to judgment. [S2]

## 8. Measure and diagnose

Pair reach and audience growth with qualified inbound interest, discovery mentions, website or subscriber trends, and specific evidence of trust. Gerhardt describes screenshots of customer and prospect feedback alongside numerical reporting. Imperfect attribution does not erase meaningful signals; signals also do not prove that a post caused revenue. [S1]

Use a dated review log: what was published, who responded, what need emerged, whether business interest appeared, and what changes next. Set a review date appropriate to the assignment. The log and review interval are adaptations, not expert benchmarks.

When results are weak, inspect participation, relevance, idea substance, product credibility, channel fit, and response quality before increasing output. Early publishing can help a founder find their voice even with limited engagement. Continue learning without promising immediate pipeline or declaring success from likes alone. [S1] [S2]

## Explicit warnings and their operational consequences

| Gerhardt's warning | Consequence for this skill | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Social reduced to announcements and promotion misses the opportunity | Teach something relevant and create room for conversation | [S1] |
| Daily frequency alone cannot rescue empty content | Return to experience and beliefs before adding posts | [S1] |
| A company-focused community lacks an audience benefit | State what members gain independently of buying | [S2] |
| Feedback can be taken too literally | Keep founder judgment and triangulate signals | [S2] |
| Unrelated topics weaken a recognizable area of expertise | Make the niche clear without erasing personality | [S3] |
| Generic AI content loses the distinctive person | Use founder inputs and review for real voice | [S4] |
| An old successful playbook is not a ready-made current strategy | Reassess audience, channel response, and available ingredients | [S5] |

Gerhardt also acknowledges personal visibility carries risk while arguing that excessive caution can make a founder say nothing useful. Apply this as a reason to preserve substantiated views and human detail, not permission to fabricate provocative opinions. [S1]
