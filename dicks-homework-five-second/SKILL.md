# Homework for Life and the Five-Second Moment
Based on Matthew Dicks's public method. Not affiliated with or endorsed by Matthew Dicks.

## When to use

Use this skill when someone wants to notice meaningful moments in ordinary life, start or review a daily story bank, find a personal story in notes or memories, or shape a true experience for a talk, essay, interview, conversation, or presentation. The daily capture practice can be useful even when no story will be told publicly. [S1][S7]

Do not treat an assignment to invent fiction as an invitation to supply the user's life events. If the user has given only a topic, offer a capture prompt or request a real incident before drafting a first-person personal story. Dicks's method depends on a person's actual moments and the change they experienced. [S2][S4]

## Procedure

### 1. Set the capture frame

1. Ask what part of the user's experience is available: today, a named day, a set of diary entries, an interview transcript, or older memories. If the task is a daily practice, use one dated row per day; if it is a one-off story task, treat the supplied episodes as candidate rows. A date and a compact description make later retrieval possible. [S2][S6]

2. For a daily review, ask a version of Dicks's question: if the person had to tell a brief story about today, what would they choose? Give the day a quick mental replay before judging its value. Look for a conversation, action, thought, surprise, embarrassment, tenderness, mistake, or small change that distinguishes the day. Spend roughly five minutes, not an open-ended journaling session. [S1][S2]

3. Capture at least the best available moment, even when it seems minor or does not yet qualify as a full story. Record a date and one or two sentence fragments that will bring the person back to the incident. Allow multiple moments on a day that genuinely contains them. The entry is a memory cue, not a polished paragraph or a claim that every entry must become a performance. [S2][S5][S6]

4. If reviewing the day unlocks an older memory, capture it separately and label it as a recalled earlier event, with its date or age marked approximate when unknown. Do not make the old memory look as though it happened today. Dicks describes daily review as a way forgotten memories resurface, while dated entries keep the bank navigable. [S2][S5]

5. Preserve the small details that would help the owner recognize the moment later: who was present, where it happened, the key action or line, and what the person felt or noticed. Keep these as brief retrieval cues. If the user has supplied no detail, leave a question in the entry rather than inventing sensory facts, dialogue, or another person's motives. [S2][S6]

### 2. Distinguish material from a story

6. Scan the captured entries for an internal shift. Write a candidate change in two parts: before, the person believed, felt, or was one way; after, they believed, felt, or were another way. Dicks calls this the five-second moment. It can be a realization about the world or a transformation in the person, and the decisive instant may be shorter than five literal seconds. [S4][S5][S6]

7. Do not rank candidates by spectacle. Prefer a modest moment with a clear, relatable change over a dramatic trip, accident, or accomplishment that only provides a sequence of events. Dicks explicitly favors small changes because listeners can often recognize themselves in them. [S4][S5]

8. If the entry merely says what happened, ask what changed for the narrator at one identifiable moment. If there is no defensible before and after, retain it as a memory cue or anecdote; do not manufacture revelation to force it into a story. A vacation itinerary or chronological report is not enough by itself. [S3][S4][S5][S6]

9. When several possible shifts appear, choose one that the available evidence supports and that matters to the intended listener. State it in an ordinary sentence before drafting. If the shift cannot be named clearly, ask the owner for the missing inner experience or select a different incident. The eventual story is built to make one turn legible. [S4][S6]

### 3. Build from the ending backward

10. Treat the chosen change as the destination and likely ending. Locate the earliest scene needed to show its opposite or a close counterpart. Starting from that prior state lets the listener perceive a change rather than merely hear the narrator announce a lesson. The opening need not come from the first event in calendar order. [S4][S6]

11. Enter the opening through action in a specific place. Delay biography, background lists, and explanations until the listener needs them. Dicks warns that beginnings are valuable attention territory; a moving scene earns room for later context. [S4][S6]

12. Select only events, details, and context that help the listener understand the initial state, wonder what will happen, and recognize the final shift. Move necessary background into the path after the story is underway. Cut a detail when removing it does not weaken the change or the listener's understanding of the stakes. Dicks describes story construction as making choices, including the choice to omit true but irrelevant material. [S4][S6]

13. Keep checking whether the audience has a live question or concern. If the next event is fully predictable and carries no emotional consequence, clarify what the narrator wants, fears, risks, or misunderstands at that point. Do not create suspense by adding an event that did not happen; locate the uncertainty already present in the real experience. [S4][S6]

14. Place the five-second moment as late as the truthful sequence permits, then stop soon after the new understanding is clear. An extended explanation after the turn can make the audience hear a lecture instead of experiencing the change. In a short piece, the shortest version that still makes the change intelligible is usually the strongest candidate. [S4][S5]

### 4. Check the result

15. Read the draft once for the before and after: can a listener describe the narrator's starting belief or state, identify the specific event that changed it, and say what is different afterward? If any part is missing, repair that part before polishing language. A striking event without an internal turn remains an account of events. [S4][S5][S6]

16. Read the opening separately. If it begins with a résumé, an itinerary, a list of character traits, or a promise that the story will be interesting, replace that opening with the first useful action in the scene. Then add only the context the listener needs as the event unfolds. [S4][S6]

17. Keep the narrator's change proportionate to the evidence. A small new thought is sufficient; do not inflate it into a permanent life transformation. When the user supplies uncertain recollections, preserve uncertainty in the draft or query it. Selection and compression should make the true moment clear, not make unsupported facts seem known. [S4][S5]

18. If the user requested a capture habit, deliver a dated story bank with brief entries and a reusable daily prompt. If the user requested a story, deliver the chosen moment, its before and after, and the draft or concise outline. If the candidate does not pass the change test, say so plainly and keep the useful memory cue for later instead of presenting it as a finished story. [S2][S4][S7]

## Common failure modes

- **Waiting for extraordinary events.** The practice is meant to train attention toward ordinary moments; filtering out small ones defeats the daily search. Preserve a quiet moment before deciding whether it can later carry a story. [S1][S2][S4]

- **Writing a diary instead of a story bank.** A long account of the day can bury the one scene worth recovering. Date the entry, select the distinctive moment, and leave just enough information to retrieve it. [S2][S6]

- **Confusing activity with change.** A busy day, exotic setting, or series of funny incidents does not by itself supply the inner turn. Ask what the person came to see differently and when. [S4][S5]

- **Explaining everything before anything happens.** Opening with background asks listeners to care before they have a reason. Begin at an active scene and reveal context as needed. [S4][S6]

- **Promoting every entry into a story.** Homework for Life is also an archive of remembered days. Keep entries whose potential is unknown; only draft the ones with a supported moment of change. [S1][S2][S7]

## Worked example: illustration

This scenario is invented to show the procedure; it is not a memory from Matthew Dicks or the user. A teacher's dated entry reads: "Tuesday, classroom door. I assumed Lena had ignored the supply request; she arrived with one pencil for herself and another for a classmate." As a capture entry, that sentence is enough to recall the scene without turning the nightly practice into a full essay. [S2][S6]

For a possible story, the change is: **before**, the teacher sees Lena's empty bag as carelessness; **after**, the teacher sees that Lena has quietly planned for someone else. The five-second moment is the teacher noticing the second pencil offered across the desk. The scene begins with the teacher reaching for the spare-pencil box, then Lena enters. Details about the entire school day would be cut unless they make the teacher's mistaken assumption or Lena's gesture clearer. [S4][S5][S6]

A short ending might stay with the teacher putting the unopened spare-pencil box back on the shelf. The action shows the revised view without a paragraph explaining that generosity can be invisible. If the real teacher could not confirm the assumption or the second pencil, the agent would ask rather than present those details as fact. [S4][S6]
