# Method in depth

## Scope and attribution

This reference paraphrases Dicks's public explanations of daily memory capture and personal story construction. Citations identify the supporting sources in [sources.md](sources.md). It covers two connected practices: collecting moments and shaping selected moments around a change. It does not reconstruct his paid courses or book. Practical instructions for an agent are explicitly labelled adaptations and should not be attributed to Dicks as quotations or universal requirements.

## 1. Capture trains attention

The daily assignment asks the owner to look back over the day and select the moment they would choose if they needed to tell something from it. The purpose is a deliberate search, even on an ordinary day. A conversation, a thought, an uncomfortable interaction, a small kindness, or an action can supply material. Do not wait for a trip, achievement, or emergency. [S2][S5]

Spend roughly five minutes reviewing and recording. The essential constraint is low effort and repeatability, rather than a stopwatch target. Dicks's later public course overview describes an even shorter habit, while his article and interview describe five minutes. Preserve the brief daily practice rather than treating either duration as a performance requirement. [S2][S4][S6]

Keep a dated entry with only enough detail to recover the moment. Dicks describes a spreadsheet with a date column and a wide entry column; a few fragments or sentences suffice. The medium is flexible. The deliberate search for a distinctive moment differentiates this practice from a broad diary of thoughts or a report of everything that happened. [S2][S4][S5]

Record more than one entry when the day offers several worthwhile moments. One useful entry is sufficient for a quiet day. Capturing is not a promise that each entry will become a story. Preserving a remembered day has value in its own right. [S1][S2][S4]

**Agent adaptation:** Use a two-column table by default. Add optional tags only if they aid the user's retrieval. Do not require completion of a large schema every night. Save recognizable cues such as the place, person, key action, and inner response; avoid decorative details that make capture burdensome.

## 2. Protect the distinction between today and recalled memories

Daily review can surface forgotten experiences. Dicks describes coding such entries as memories so they are not mistaken for events occurring on the capture date. Record them even when the trigger is unclear. [S2][S4]

**Agent adaptation:** Keep the capture date and the approximate event date or age distinct. For example, a row dated today may say that it recalls a school incident at about age eleven. An unknown date stays unknown. Do not silently relocate childhood material to today's timeline.

When reviewing a bank, allow distance to change your judgment. Dicks likes to get hundreds of entries beyond a moment before searching for stage material, because immediate significance can fade and initially minor events can reveal a longer pattern. This is his review preference, not a required waiting period. A user with a deadline can work with current material. [S4]

**Agent adaptation:** If the user missed days, restart with the present day and backfill only memories they actually recover. Do not create plausible entries to complete a streak. Preserve unresolved candidates for later review without presenting the delay as a failure.

## 3. Find the internal change

For this method, the central story event is an internal transformation or realization. Something about who the narrator is, what they believe, or how they see the world changes. External activity provides the setting and circumstances; it does not by itself establish the story's emotional center. [S3][S5]

The five-second moment names the compact turn around which the telling is built. Do not demand a measured five-second duration. Identify a specific action, observation, thought, or exchange that makes the shift legible. A change in opinion is enough; it need not be a permanent personality overhaul. [S3][S5]

Use three diagnostic statements:

- Before: What did the narrator believe, expect, want, or feel?
- Moment: At what identifiable point did that state shift, and what caused it?
- After: What became different, however slightly?

These diagnostic fields are an agent adaptation of Dicks's distinction between the prior state and the ending. They help locate evidence before prose makes a vague lesson sound convincing. [S3][S5]

An anecdote may be amusing, unusual, or useful while leaving the narrator essentially unchanged. Retain it as such. Chronology is allowed; the problem is relying on a sequence of events to do the work of an internal turn. Travel is not disqualified, but a list of destinations and meals is insufficient. [S3]

### Selection decisions

| Evidence available | Decision |
| --- | --- |
| Clear before, specific turn, supported after | Develop a story around that turn. |
| Distinctive event, no supported internal shift | Keep a memory cue or anecdote; ask what changed if development is requested. |
| Several plausible changes | Choose one principal destination and retain other candidates separately. |
| Broad life change, no identifiable incident yet | Ask for a concrete episode where the new understanding became apparent. |
| Dramatic event with thin inner experience | Do not prefer it over a quieter but clearer change. |
| Only a theme or desired lesson | Prompt for actual experience before drafting a true first-person story. |

The classification and information-handling rules are agent adaptations. The preference for relatable small changes and the distinction between anecdotes and change-centered stories come from Dicks's interview. [S3]

## 4. Build from the destination backward

Know the ending before choosing the opening. The ending is the change the story is trying to clarify. This destination determines which events matter and which true details can be omitted. [S3][S5]

Look for an opening that shows the opposite of the final state. Dicks allows an approximate counterpart when there is no exact opposite. If the ending involves trusting someone, begin where the narrator's distrust is visible. Avoid inventing an extreme initial belief merely to increase contrast. [S3]

Start as close to the ending as you can while still making the change understandable. This may be a scene halfway through the actual day's events. Enter through action in a place. Introduce history and relationships when the listener needs them, rather than opening with a biography or explanation. [S3][S4][S5]

For oral storytelling, Dicks favors recognizable location nouns over extensive descriptive passages. A kitchen or classroom gives listeners a mental scene quickly; its exact furnishings matter only when they affect the events. He also recommends present tense where it works for the teller, with past tense available to distinguish backstory. [S4]

**Agent adaptation:** Preserve the user's requested format and voice. A written essay may benefit from more description or past tense. Select useful specifics instead of mechanically deleting every adjective. An action opening does not require danger, a chase, or a provocative hook.

## 5. Keep a live audience question

Stakes give the listener a reason to want the next sentence: a worry, hope, concern, or unresolved question about the narrator. They support the internal story without requiring spectacular events. Ask what the narrator wanted and why the outcome mattered at that time. [S3][S5]

One public technique is to reveal the narrator's plan before events test it. When the audience knows the hoped-for outcome, they can share the narrator's frustration or apprehension when the plan fails. Use it when an actual plan is part of the supplied experience. It is optional, not a compulsory beat in every story. [S3]

**Agent adaptation:** For each major beat, identify what the audience still wonders. If nothing remains, remove the beat or clarify the real uncertainty. Do not fabricate danger, hide essential facts dishonestly, or attribute motives to other people just to create suspense.

## 6. Select and compress responsibly

Dicks explicitly distinguishes removing unnecessary material from adding events that did not happen. He describes omitting people who play no role and compressing elapsed time to simplify a telling. His stated constraint is working with the events available to him. [S3]

**Agent adaptation:** Omit irrelevant pauses without silently changing factual dates, causal order, or who did something. When consequential timing matters, preserve it. If a user specifically requests a condensed retelling, make the compression transparent where it could otherwise mislead. This is a more conservative operating rule for handling another person's account, not a claim that Dicks prohibits all time compression.

Unknown dialogue should become indirect speech, a placeholder, or a question. Unknown sensations, thoughts, and motives stay unknown. The owner can confirm their own remembered internal experience; the agent cannot supply it. For uncertain recollections, retain qualified language. These are package safeguards derived from the truthfulness constraint. [S3]

## 7. Land the turn and stop

Choose details that make the ending understandable and emotionally perceptible. Place the principal change late in the telling, with only the aftermath needed to show what is different. Dicks treats the change as the destination and favors concise stories over unnecessary length. [S3][S4][S5]

**Agent adaptation:** A brief final action or thought can carry the new understanding. If an internal shift would otherwise be invisible, name it plainly. Do not insist on showing everything through gestures, invent a symbolic action, or attach a broad inspirational lesson unsupported by the incident. Five minutes is a common performance length, not a required length for every conversation, essay, or interview answer. [S3]

## 8. Explicit warnings and their practical consequences

- **Do not wait for extraordinary material.** Dicks prefers many small moments because listeners can relate to them. Search ordinary interactions before rejecting a day as empty. [S3]
- **Do not make capture a writing chore.** Excessive writing makes it easier to abandon the habit. Use short retrieval cues and develop prose separately. [S4]
- **Do not confuse activity with a story.** Interesting events without a change may remain anecdotes. Locate the inner turn before polishing. [S3]
- **Do not bury the opening in lists or background.** Start the experience, then supply necessary context along the way. [S3][S5]
- **Do not invent additions for impact.** Dicks describes omission and selection rather than making up events. Repair structure using supported material. [S3]
- **Do not assume constant humor is required.** Dicks distinguishes storytelling from stand-up and values emotional movement beyond laughter. Use humor if present, without making it a gate for storyworthiness. [S3]

Dicks's reports of greater recall, richer days, and a changed sense of time explain his motivation for the practice. They are personal and participant reports, not guaranteed therapeutic or scientific outcomes. Keep the deliverable focused on memory cues and the user's requested story. [S1][S2][S4]

## 9. Completion decisions

For capture-only work, return dated cues plus a reusable daily prompt. For selection, return candidate statuses and the strongest supported before, moment, and after. For drafting, return a concise outline or story with relevant unresolved facts clearly separated. Use [the template](../templates/output-template.md) and [the review checklist](../checklists/review-checklist.md).

Stop development when the supplied evidence cannot support a change and further detail requires the owner. Complete the useful capture or diagnosis already possible, then identify the specific missing information. Do not force an invented revelation. This stopping condition is an agent adaptation that keeps the method faithful to actual experience.
