# Worked examples

Both examples are invented illustrations. All people, organizations, interview notes, metrics, and outcomes below are fictional. They demonstrate the public structure in [S1] and the interview-to-draft loop in [S3][S4], not results achieved by notus.

## Illustration 1: A relevant transformation with measured results

### Brief and fictional input

Writer: founder of a customer onboarding consultancy. Reader: operations leaders at B2B software firms with slow implementations. Client: Lena, operations lead at fictional CedarOps. Permission to name her is assumed only inside this illustration.

Interview notes: median implementation time was 42 days across 12 completed projects in January; it was 28 days across 15 completed projects in June. The consultancy mapped handoffs, piloted a readiness checklist, and then discovered that customer data access remained a bottleneck. An early access check was added. CedarOps also changed staffing, so the consultancy cannot claim sole causation. Weekly reviews continue.

### Weak first draft

We helped CedarOps transform onboarding with our proven system. Lena is an amazing leader and our world-class team delivered incredible results. First we did strategy, then execution, then optimization. Now the company is thriving. Want the same results? Book a call.

### Improved post

Lena's customers were waiting six weeks to finish onboarding, even though her implementation team knew the product inside out.

At CedarOps, the problem was the handoff. Sales promised a start date before anyone checked whether the customer had supplied the data the team needed.

Lena wanted a shorter path to launch without asking her team to work longer hours.

We began with Handoff Mapping: tracing where projects waited and who owned each decision.

Next came a Readiness Pilot: a checklist tested on the next customer cohort.

The first version missed something. Customers could complete the checklist while their data access was still unresolved.

So we added an Access Check before scheduling kickoff, with one owner for exceptions.

By June, median onboarding time was 28 days across 15 completed projects, compared with 42 days across 12 in January.

The team also changed staffing during that period. Our work addressed the handoffs; it was one part of the improvement.

Lena now has a weekly view of blocked projects, and we still review the exceptions together.

### Why it improved

- The opening makes a recognizable problem emotionally meaningful without exaggeration. [S1]
- The starting situation explains the contradiction between expertise and operational friction. [S1]
- Noun-based stages contain concrete actions and a real adjustment. [S1]
- The outcome specifies cohorts, dates, qualitative benefit, and ongoing work. [S1]
- The attribution qualification comes from this package's evidence safeguards, not an asserted notus rule.

Internal handoff: verify the fictional January and June records in a real assignment, confirm permission, obtain founder feedback, and place the post in the aspirational pillar. Do not treat the draft's existence as publication authorization.

## Illustration 2: An anonymized story with no revenue claim

### Brief and fictional input

Writer: founder of an expert-content consultancy. Reader: founders of industrial software businesses. Client: an anonymized technical founder; permission covers broad company type and aggregate figures, not name or distinctive project details.

Interview notes: the founder's previous posts described features. Prospects repeatedly asked the same implementation question. A weekly interview surfaced a failed pilot and a useful lesson. Over eight weeks, the team produced 16 approved posts. The company logged five inquiries referring to those posts; two became demos. No closed revenue is available. The engagement has ended.

### Weak first draft

We made an industrial SaaS founder the category leader. Our ghostwriting generated huge revenue and viral content in just eight weeks. All it took was our weekly podcast. We continue scaling their brand every day.

### Improved post

This technical founder could explain every feature, but prospects still could not see how the software would fit into their working day.

They led an industrial software company. Their posts were accurate, but read like release notes.

The goal was to answer the implementation questions that kept returning in sales calls.

We started with Question Mapping: reviewing those questions and choosing the ones a buyer needed answered before a demo.

Then came Weekly Interviews. In one conversation, the founder explained why an early pilot had stalled. That experience gave us a more useful story than another feature announcement.

During Draft Review, we kept the founder's explanation and removed technical shortcuts that assumed too much reader knowledge. Each post came back to the founder for correction before use.

Across eight weeks, the team approved 16 posts. Five inquiries explicitly mentioned them; two progressed to demos.

We do not yet have a closed-revenue result.

The engagement is complete. The founder now has an interview routine and a bank of buyer questions the team can keep using.

Client identity is withheld by agreement.

### Why it improved

- The opening and initial situation connect to a buyer's actual obstacle. [S1]
- An interview surfaces lived experience instead of invented expertise. [S3][S4]
- The process includes actions and review, rather than a magical tool claim. [S1][S3]
- The outcome combines modest, specific evidence with a lasting capability. [S1]
- Anonymization, removal of unsupported category leadership, and the truthful finished-engagement close apply the package's editorial adaptations.

Internal handoff: the inquiry log supports mentions, not proof that posts alone caused the demos. Confirm the permitted anonymity level. Route the revenue question to the commercial owner; do not add an estimated revenue number to make the story sound stronger.
