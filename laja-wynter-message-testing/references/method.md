# The message testing method

Based on Peep Laja's public method. Not affiliated with or endorsed by Peep Laja.

## Reading map and attribution

Follow scope, buyer context, study selection, stimulus, questions, diagnosis, revision, and retest in order. The four-layer framework and differentiation principles come from Laja's articles. Operational study defaults come from Wynter's public playbook; the question design guidance also draws on Kate Meda's public guide. Local reporting conventions, evidence labels, and decision gates below are this package's implementation guidance, not claims that Laja published a specific scoring rubric. [S1][S3][S4][S6]

## Scope and evidence

Write a research question such as: can operations directors at regional logistics companies understand this homepage and explain why they would consider its route-planning software over spreadsheets? Identify the decision, page, audience, desired action, and hypothesis. Record what observable buyer interpretation would resolve the uncertainty. Avoid an arbitrary universal score threshold: the sources prescribe diagnosing buyer questions, not a single pass score for every market. [S1][S6]

Message testing is qualitative diagnosis. Analytics may show a disappointing conversion rate but do not locate a confusing claim or explain a buyer's resistance. A/B tests can estimate relative behavioral performance when designed with sufficient data; a message test explains what buyers understand and what should change. Combining the methods is useful, but favorable message feedback alone does not establish conversion lift. [S2][S6]

Use explicit evidence labels:

- **Observed:** Actual respondent answers or documented buyer research, with a retrievable source or response ID.
- **Inferred:** The analyst's causal interpretation of observed evidence.
- **Proposed:** A rewrite or study that has not been tested.
- **Illustrative:** Invented teaching material, never part of a client's evidence.

If research is unavailable, perform a provisional expert review. List missing buyer evidence, formulate hypotheses, and prepare questions. Do not supply imagined findings. Internal review can expose an inconsistency but cannot stand in for target-market response. These labels are package reporting conventions.

## Buyer context

Laja distinguishes knowing buyers' current problems from knowing their response to your message. Both matter. Demographic personas alone miss priorities, measures of success, constraints, desired gains, and buying doubts. Prefer recent, specific evidence from interviews, buyer surveys, sales conversations, and win/loss research over a generic persona document. [S2]

Document the buyer's priority problem, visible symptoms, value of solving, purchase trigger, decision process, budget owner, discovery channels, and competitive perception. Wynter groups foundational research into pain and value, buying behavior, and competitive perception. Brand awareness and pricing perception are added when the decision calls for them, rather than bundled automatically into every survey. [S4]

If current credible research covers these questions, reuse it. If the problem itself is uncertain, a test of finished copy cannot resolve which problem deserves the promise. Gather context first. A new segment warrants segment-specific research; stale research should be reconsidered when the market changes. The playbook warns against treating research older than 12 months as current in a fast-moving category. [S4]

## Study selection

| Decision | Appropriate study | Interpretation boundary |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Understand pains, triggers, buying process, alternatives | Buyer survey or interviews | Context for messaging, not validation of a page |
| Diagnose one homepage or core product/feature page | Message test | Interpretations, motivation, and objections |
| Diagnose a high-intent campaign landing page | Landing page test | Keep campaign audience and source promise in context |
| Understand plan choice and price communication | Pricing page test | Comprehension and perceived value, not proof of willingness to pay |
| Choose among two or three viable directions | Preference test | Preference and reasons inform strategic judgment |

These routes reflect the public playbook and visible example types. Avoid routine message tests on blogs, careers, about pages, support documentation, or competitor comparison pages designed for late-stage buyers who already trust the company. This is a scope rule for the playbook's diagnostic workflow, not a claim that those pages never merit other research. [S4][S7]

Preference tests should follow an initial narrowing of options. Eight variants spread attention and avoid the strategic decision. Do not use a preference vote to outsource responsibility for positioning or settle internal politics. [S4]

## Stimulus and audience

Capture a live URL or faithful page image and record its version. Show the actual message in context, not isolated claims that remove necessary explanation. For a homepage, inspect the whole page and the hero separately; highlight the sections where the persuasion work happens. Note when buyers understand the offer only after scrolling. [S5][S6]

Choose respondents by relevant role, seniority, industry, company size, and buying involvement. Explain any deviation from the intended market. Separate prospective buyers from existing customers, whose familiarity may conceal first-visit ambiguity. Keep different roles identifiable instead of averaging their priorities into a fictional universal buyer. Audience tracking and customer separation are package safeguards grounded in the sources' emphasis on target buyers. [S2][S6]

Do not encode brittle interface limits into the method. The fetched guide mentions three highlighted areas, while the fetched example page shows up to seven. Select a few important sections and verify the current interface when running a study. The diagnostic principle is page-level and section-level feedback, not a fixed highlight count. [S6][S7]

## Questions and sampling

Use consistent ratings plus open-ended explanations for clarity, relevance, value, and differentiation. Capture brand perception separately when needed. Friction is a cross-cutting diagnostic lens; the playbook's fifth reported dimension is brand, so do not silently relabel brand scores as friction. [S1][S4]

Suggested neutral prompts, adapted for this package:

- What do you think this company offers? Explain it in your own words.
- Who would use this, and for what situation?
- Which part relates to a current priority in your work? What feels less relevant?
- What result would you expect, and how useful would that be?
- What would you use instead? What reason, if any, does this page give to choose this offer?
- What makes you hesitate or doubt a claim? What information is missing?
- Which section first helped you understand the offer? Where did you get confused?

Ask spontaneous interpretation before revealing the intended category or benefit. A leading prompt can teach the answer and create false evidence of comprehension. Keep rating anchors and question order documented; a rating without explanation is weak guidance for a rewrite. Open questions are supported by Meda's guide; prompt ordering and avoidance of leading wording are package study-design safeguards. [S6]

Wynter recommends 15 respondents as the usual message-test starting point, with 30 as a practical maximum and separate 15-person segment tests preferred when more data is needed. Qualitative surveys range from 15 to 50; preference tests start at 30, with 50 preferred. The playbook routes percentage, segment, and trend reporting to quantitative surveys of 100 or more. These are vendor planning defaults, not universal sample-size calculations or guarantees. [S4]

Inspect whether new answers still reveal new consequential themes. Heterogeneous audiences and broader questions may require more work. Report recurring themes and counts with denominators when helpful, but do not convert a small panel into a population estimate, assert statistical significance, or treat its average score as a precise market measure. Meda's guide describes saturation as the relevant qualitative principle. [S6]

## Ordered diagnosis

The order matters because an upstream failure contaminates downstream answers. Diagnose one primary failed layer, propose one causal hypothesis and focused revision, then inspect it again before optimizing the next layer. Proof and friction belong throughout. [S1]

### 1. Clarity

Can buyers name the offer's category and explain what it helps them do? Failures include wrong category labels, contradictory paraphrases, unexplained jargon, or repeating a slogan without a concrete account. Fix the category cue and plain-language explanation first. A striking headline cannot compensate for an unidentified offer. [S1]

### 2. Relevance

Does the message match a priority this buyer has now? A recognized problem may belong to another team, be low priority, or be framed too broadly. Adjust the audience cue, priority problem, and role-specific use case. Resist adding generic benefits to include everyone. [S1][S2]

### 3. Value

Does the buyer want the promised outcome enough to pursue it? A capability list can leave its result implicit. Make the gain concrete in the buyer's work and address perceived cost, effort, or risk. Use verified scale or timing where available; invented numbers and stronger adjectives do not create evidence. [S1]

### 4. Differentiation

Can the buyer explain why this option deserves consideration over a named alternative or the current approach? A claim that any competitor could make is weak differentiation. Look for a supported product, approach, specialization, business model, or brand advantage and make its relevance clear. Do not invent a category label or adjective to simulate a difference. [S1][S3]

When feedback conflicts, inspect segment, experience, and stimulus location before concluding the copy is inconsistent. One dissenting opinion is not automatically a strategic gap; repeated misclassification within the intended audience is more consequential. The segmentation and clustering procedure operationalizes the sources' target-buyer focus. [S2][S3]

## Proof, friction, and sameness

Build a claim audit covering category, audience, problem, outcome, difference, proof, and next action. Compare with the homepages of alternatives buyers actually consider. Mark repeated promises and claims transferable to another vendor. Distinguish common category requirements from a difference the company can substantiate. Novel wording on a common offer is insufficient. [S3]

For each important claim, record the evidence available, its scope, and any missing support. Proof might be a documented result, product demonstration, genuine testimonial, integration detail, or concrete explanation of how the outcome happens. Place it where it answers the buyer's doubt. Capture anxieties about implementation, switching, reliability, or effort when respondents raise them. Do not invent reassurance that the business cannot honor. [S1][S2][S6]

Laja warns that constant competitor benchmarking creates conformity. He also warns that research can produce sameness when teams implement average feature requests without a clear strategy. Learn what is missing, but retain deliberate positioning and its tradeoffs. An objection may reveal a poor segment fit rather than a sentence or feature that must be added. [S3]

## Revision rules

Rewrite around the earliest failed buyer question. Give the hero an identifiable offer, audience or use case, and meaningful outcome. Add a defensible reason to choose it where supported. A useful sequence is category and promise, explanation, desired result, difference and proof, objection handling, then next action; adapt the layout to the audience and evidence rather than enforcing a universal page formula. [S1][S3]

Simplify presentation when it delays comprehension. Laja's Qualified notes criticize an unhelpful narrative headline, an intro that omits the Salesforce differentiator, distracting visuals, and three use cases that explain essentially the same thing. Combine repeated explanations and link to detail where useful. The recommendation to cut that particular homepage heavily is a case-specific critique, not a rule that all pages must be halved. [S5]

Prioritize revisions by the primary failure and the importance of the underlying buyer question. Connect each change to evidence and a hypothesis. If proof is missing, mark the claim for verification, soften it to supported language, or remove it. Never fill proof placeholders with plausible statistics.

## Retest and maintenance

Retest a substantive rewrite before calling it fixed. Keep the ICP definition, page scope, rating anchors, and questions comparable; use comparable respondents without requiring the same individuals. Record recruitment or stimulus differences so changes in interpretation are not automatically attributed to copy. Compare actual paraphrases and objections as well as scores. [S1][S4]

Use a project-specific decision gate: recommend ship when the primary comprehension or motivation failure is resolved with credible evidence and consequential objections are answered; revise when it persists; gather buyer context when the message is built on an unsupported priority or audience assumption. This gate operationalizes the playbook's pretest, ship, or iterate loop. It does not certify conversion performance. [S4][S6]

Skip a fresh message test for typos, minor wording polish, or design-only edits. Use usability or design research when interaction or layout is the actual question. Wynter suggests at least annual core-page testing and buyer-context refreshes, with more frequent work for heavy traffic/spend or changing markets. Trigger targeted research for a new segment, product launch, competitor repositioning, pricing change, rebrand, or material buyer-behavior shift. Treat cadence as risk-sensitive planning guidance. [S4]

## Explicit warnings to retain

- Laja: jargon and unclear category cues derail later persuasion; generic benefits and adjective-based differences do not fix the failed layer. [S1]
- Laja: copying competitors and treating every customer request as an instruction can erase a defensible position. [S3]
- Laja: distracting detail and repeated use-case blocks can delay understanding despite good visual polish. [S5]
- Wynter: do not test copy before obtaining necessary buyer context, rerun surveys whose answers already exist, or call cosmetic edits a substantive fix. [S4]
- Wynter: avoid routine tests outside the persuasion path, unnecessary minor-edit retests, too many preference variants, and using preference data as the strategic decision itself. [S4]
- Meda: message diagnosis and A/B measurement answer different questions; qualitative research should not be judged by the statistical-significance standard of an experiment. [S6]
