# Method: reader-centered marketing writing

Based on Ann Handley's public method. Not affiliated with or endorsed by Ann Handley.

## Scope and interpretation

This is a practical synthesis of public articles and the public Writing GPS infographic, not a summary of unpublished or purchased book text. The core sequence comes from S1. Later public guidance adds nuance about voice, story, and human authorship. Handley treats writing as learnable through practice and editing, while allowing different processes. Use the sequence to organize decisions, not to force the same creative form on every assignment. [S1] [S2]

Sections explicitly called agent adaptations are operational choices for applying the public principles. They are not claims that Handley prescribed an automated workflow. Citations identify the rationale for the adaptation. Method citations generally belong in internal rationale, not in the marketing copy itself.

## Purpose and reader consequence

Start with two distinct statements: what the business hopes to achieve and what the reader needs to understand or accomplish. A business goal such as increasing appointment bookings is legitimate, but it does not explain why a reader should care. Keep the goal specific enough to guide choices, then translate it into a reader consequence. [S1] [S3]

Handley's short prompt is "So what?" Ask it of the business goal and each proposed benefit. Answer with a reason that matters to the reader, then question that answer again. Move from an attribute to its practical effect and, when relevant, its significance in the person's life. Write the useful endpoint as one plain sentence that can guide the draft. [S1] [S3]

Decision rules:

- Choose a consequence the intended reader would recognize in their actual situation. A procurement reader and an end user can value different consequences of the same feature. [S3]
- If an answer merely repeats the feature in more flattering language, keep questioning. If the next answer adds only vague happiness or unsupported outcomes, use the last concrete answer. This stopping rule is an agent adaptation. [S1] [S3]
- Do this thinking before generating polished prose. Handley warns that highly optimized or attractive content can still be weak because it advances the company's agenda without examining the audience's needs. [S3]
- When a business purpose and reader value conflict, revise the angle rather than disguising the conflict with warmer language. This is an operational consequence of audience-centered framing. [S3]

Example of the reasoning, not a sourced customer claim: reservable repair appointments can help a cyclist plan when their bike will be unavailable. They do not establish a guaranteed repair time or a stress-free week. The evidence boundary is an agent adaptation of the support-gathering stage. [S1]

## Human thinking and agent assistance

Handley's public AI guidance is stronger than a request to humanize generated prose. She argues that the initial draft is where the author thinks and discovers their ideas. Regularly handing that stage to AI can reduce creative practice, confidence, and differentiation. She acknowledges useful AI applications while cautioning against outsourcing the act of putting one's own thought on the page. [S7]

Agent adaptations:

- Prefer the user's notes, outline, voice memo transcript, observations, or existing draft as the starting material. Keep the distinctive thinking visible while revising. [S7]
- If the user requests a draft from a brief, provide a proposed draft within that scope. Do not claim Handley endorses this substitution for a human thinking draft. Do not invent lived experience to fill the gap. [S7]
- Ask for one consequential missing observation when a personal story depends on it. For an ordinary product email, proceed from known facts rather than requiring a memoir. [S7]
- Mark unresolved experience placeholders in review notes. An invented illustration is appropriate for teaching, but must never masquerade as a real customer account. [S5] [S7]
- Do not infer that an agent's revision pass provides the same mental distance as a person's break. Use a fresh, explicit review against the brief instead. [S1]

The public survey discussed in S7 reports an association between writing practice and self-reported results. Do not turn that association into a causal guarantee or a universal productivity target. This is an evidence-handling adaptation, not a separate claim by the expert. [S7]

## Evidence and customer contributions

Gather credible support before increasing persuasion. Data, examples, and relevant personal experience can all help establish the idea. The type of evidence should fit the assertion: an anecdote illustrates a situation, while a measured outcome needs a relevant measurement. [S1]

Agent decision rules: record which supplied fact supports each material claim; preserve the scope of numbers and comparisons; narrow or remove unsupported promises. If the claim is essential but cannot be verified from available material, identify the missing evidence outside the copy. Do not use fluent phrasing to conceal the uncertainty. These rules operationalize S1's support stage. [S1]

Customer-centered storytelling can include real customer contributions rather than merely making the brand sound empathetic. Handley's public example recommends seeking republishing permission and crediting contributors. She also warns against contribution requests that demand too much effort for a campaign seeking broad participation. Match the effort to the goal. High-effort participation can be appropriate for a deliberately selective project. [S5]

For user-generated content, retain the contributor, provenance, permitted reuse, and attribution in working notes. A public social post is not proof of permission to republish it. If permission is unresolved, propose the angle or request instead of embedding the material as cleared content. Do not contact contributors unless the user has authorized that action. These are agent adaptations of the permission guidance. [S5]

Do not promise that one campaign will create a thriving community. Handley emphasizes ongoing engagement and collaboration rather than a single solicitation. Her historical examples are illustrations, not current vendor recommendations or guaranteed performance benchmarks. [S5]

## Structure and story

Choose the form that makes the reader's task easiest. An explanation answers why or what; instructions answer how; a case story makes a change concrete. Outline before polishing so that the order follows the reader's need rather than the business's talking-point list. [S1]

For narrative pieces, balance three discoveries: the reader learns something meaningful about the writer, sees something of their own situation, and understands a new idea. These are functions of a story, not three mandatory headings. One small observation can serve more than one function. [S6]

Handley explicitly warns about three imbalances:

- Too much writer revelation becomes self-absorption.
- Too much reader reflection becomes pandering or conspicuous over-explanation.
- Too much idea exposition becomes a lecture when a well-chosen story would illuminate it. [S6]

Agent adaptations: shorten autobiographical setup when it delays the reader's payoff; remove flattering assumptions about how clever or special the audience is; connect the incident to a concrete insight instead of adding a moral detached from the event. Never fabricate an incident to satisfy the narrative test. Apply the test to origin stories, essays, and case narratives, not to every button or transactional message. [S6] [S7]

The customer should remain an actor: someone choosing, solving, learning, or contributing. The product can help without claiming credit for every part of their achievement. Customer participation can also be visual, but a writing task does not automatically expand into media production. [S3] [S5]

## Drafting and reader revision

Write toward one identifiable person rather than an abstract segment. Imagine their situation and decision, then use direct address where it fits the brand. Explain the subject as if it may be unfamiliar while respecting the reader's intelligence. Accessible writing should remove unnecessary decoding, not flatten expertise. [S1] [S2]

The rough draft is for discovering and arranging thought. Awkward transitions or placeholders are acceptable at this stage. Handley explicitly distinguishes this intermediate stage from permission to produce substandard finished work. Do not polish the opening indefinitely while the central idea remains unresolved. [S1] [S2]

Separate discovery from revision. A person can step away when time permits; an agent should perform a separate review anchored in the reader's need. Ask whether the draft answers the important question, whether the connections are understandable, and whether passages remain only because the writer likes them. Substantive rewriting takes priority over punctuation. [S1] [S2]

When editing existing material, preserve its useful specificity. Rewrite a confusing sentence without replacing a distinctive observation with stock phrases. If the premise is weak, address the premise before generating alternative phrasings. This adaptation combines clarity editing with the protection of human thinking. [S2] [S7] [S8]

Deadlines help move writing into completion. Handley warns against treating creative work as something that must wait indefinitely for ideal inspiration. Under a short deadline, compress passes but still separate the decisions: purpose, support, structure, clarity, and final delivery. The compressed sequence is an agent adaptation. [S2]

## Voice and language

Voice concerns how the brand sounds, including on supporting surfaces such as confirmations, error pages, and FAQs. Begin with the organization's real character, product, working style, and relationship with customers. If there is no established guide, propose approximately three specific attributes and make them concrete with examples of language choices. [S4]

Attributes that almost every business should possess are insufficient as the whole voice definition. Handley's article, including its quoted practitioner guidance, cautions against generic virtues, empty innovation claims, and familiar buzzwords. Convert a chosen attribute into practical behavior: a hands-on brand explains what the reader can try; a restrained brand avoids inflated excitement. These examples are adaptations. [S4]

Record pronoun choices for both business and reader. Avoid drifting between a warm first-person company voice and an unexplained third-person corporate narrator. Consistency follows the selected context; quoting another speaker or intentionally changing sections is not automatically a failure. [S4]

S2 strongly criticizes jargon; S4 explicitly qualifies Handley's earlier blanket objection. Specialist vocabulary can express shared understanding or useful precision. Keep it when this audience benefits, define it when necessary, and remove it when it obstructs comprehension or performs expertise. Do not enforce a universal plain-language ban on technical terms. [S2] [S4]

Mentally remove the logo or company name. If competitors could use the copy unchanged, look for particular perspective, concrete detail, or language rooted in the brand's character. Unusual vocabulary alone is insufficient differentiation. [S4] [S8]

Handley's later sentence analysis values rhythm, memorable wording, and language that expresses identity. Read important sentences aloud or inspect cadence. Preserve an expressive construction if it helps connection and clarity. Humor, alliteration, and passive voice are context-dependent tools rather than required effects or automatic errors. Do not import Handley's own comic personality into an unrelated brand. [S8]

## Headline, editing, and delivery

Give the headline a deliberate revision pass after clarifying the body. The public GPS treats it as deserving substantial attention. Compare options for relevance, specificity, and fidelity to the delivered idea. Do not turn this into a fixed time quota for every short assignment. [S1]

An agent adaptation: reject a headline that promises unsupported results or information absent from the body. Strong curiosity should lead to a fulfilled promise. For a transactional label, prioritize the action or state the reader needs to recognize. [S1] [S3]

Seek another editor when available for grammar, usage, punctuation, and style. If unavailable, conduct a separate self-review and say so when review status matters. Do not imply independent approval. Fix substantive clarity before treating mechanical correctness as completion. [S1] [S2]

Inspect the delivery surface. Use short, manageable paragraphs, helpful subheads, and lists where they improve scanning. Do not strip out necessary explanations just to make the page look sparse. The reader should understand both what matters and why. [S1] [S2]

End with a proportionate next step. The public GPS pairs publication with a final question about what readers should do next. The agent's writing endpoint is prepared copy within the user's scope; actual publication remains a separate action requiring appropriate authorization. Where the purpose is understanding rather than conversion, the next step can be applying the idea or making an informed choice rather than buying immediately. This is an agent adaptation. [S1] [S3]

Deliver finished copy separately from working rationale. Include unresolved factual claims, missing experience, and reuse permissions in concise notes. If a critical gap prevents usable copy, provide the supported portion and identify the exact blocker. Check the finished work with the review checklist before calling it ready.
